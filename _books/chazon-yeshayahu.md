Volume II of *Kisvei Rav Yeshayahu Glazer*, the collected writings of Rabbi Simon (Yeshayahu) Glazer, edited, annotated and expanded by Simon Springer. A discourse on every parsha of the Torah.

## Introduction

The sermon during the morning services on Sabbaths, and on High Holy days, and Festivals was first introduced in Alexandria, Egypt. Philo, in his “*Quod Omnis Probus Liber*” mentions the Derasha as a part of public worship in the Alexandrian Synagogue. And, although the Derasha was never a part of either the Tabernacle or Temple service, Agadah occupied a very high position among the learned classes both, in Palestine and in Diaspora from the earliest times. The Beth-ha-Midrash is so called because of the Agadah, or Derash, which was expounded therein. Long before the Sidur, or the Mahzor, was known or accepted, the Derasha had already taken root in the heart and mind of many generations.

“*Whose Sabbath was it*”, or, *“What subject in the Agadah was expounded”* (Hagigah 3a), were no mere inquisitive questions of the fastidious. They were sincere questions, backed by a desire to learn, first, who the honored preacher was, and second, what new development of his discourse brought forward. In fact, ever since Moses had ordained to read parts of the text of the Torah on Sabbaths and holy days, the desire to understand the Torah became a part of the make-up of the inner soul of the people of Israel. The sages knew that in order to satisfy this desire they would have to expound the Torah in the vernacular of the masses, and the Derasha in the Bet-ha-Midrash did just that.

But the text of the Torah could not have been interpreted in its literal sense alone. The sages, during the earliest times, recognized this difficulty. The Word of God had to be transmitted to posterity in the language of man, with a full exposition of both, the letter and the spirit, of the entirely inexplicable and contradictory verses in some parts of the text. To accomplish this end, Moses, in his day, and forty generations after him, till Mar, son of Ashi, created the Mishnah, the Talmud, the Sifa, sifre, Tosefta, Mehilta, and the entire structure of Midrashic and Agadic literature, which, together with the Hermoneutical rules, became the means of all succeeding generations of sages from Mar to Maimonides, and from him till this day, whereby all difficult text is an open book.

It is because of the lack of knowledge of, or the lack of desire to attain that knowledge, that many scholars outside of Jewry, or outside of the Bet-ha-Midrash, were scorched by strange fires of gross errors in their effort to pass through the Divine Vineyard by breaking down and removing ancient landmarks.

Moses was the father of the Oral Torah. From him every recipient in uninterrupted succession added to it. In the days of Ezra oral interpretation of the Torah in the form of translation was instituted. It then became the accepted doctrine that the Torah, as expounded by the Oral school, rules by “Four Lights”, namely: (a) Peshat; (b) Derash; (c) Remez; (d) Sod.

\(a\) The accepted meaning of the words which compose the text, as, for example, the Verses of the Torah dealing with civil law, persons, places as well as with ritual which are non-controversial, and no necessity for the application of any of the hermeneutic rules in order to clarify the text or the Verse exists, is called - Peshat. But even under the term Poshat a figure of speech must be recognized. The expression of “*the Hand of God*”, the “*Eyes of God*”, may be cited as examples of the figures which are recognized as such by the Peshatists.

\(b\) That which clarifies, reconciles, interprets, or throws light on Verses of the Torah in harmony with the text or context, which otherwise appear contradictory, ambiguous or inexplicable is called - Derash. However, the function of Derash is far more extensive than the mere overcoming of the extraordinary Verses of the Torah for, in reality, its objective is to reach out and unfold the moral forces behind the dead letters. In order to do both, the sages bestowed upon Derash certain privileges and endowed it with working tools consisting of hermeneutical rules, Rabbi Ishmael’s thirteen and Rabbi Jesse the Galilieean’s thirty-two, along with many more of un-named authorship. A Nineteenth Century Rabbi, Malbim, in his work “*Ayelet Hashahar*”, which he compiled as an introduction to the Book of Vayikra, brought out the amazing fact that there are at least six-hundred and thirteen rules to serve Derash for its many purposes.

\(c\) An elaboration upon both, the Peshat and the Derash, which, in itself, is an addition of deep spiritual thought, giving to every Verse in the Torah an entire new significance is called - Remez. The rationalization of Remez and its use in Biblical commentary during the post Talmudic era was finally endorsed by Maimonides, who, in a letter to his son Abraham extolled the work of Abraham Ibn Ezra, particularly the application of Remez as a means of elucidating certain events described in the Torah. Maimonides even praised in glowing terms Ibn Ezra’s commentary on Exodus, twenty-three, Verse twenty, in course of which he likens the sun to the Intelligence of God and the moon to the intelligence of man. Maimonides himself, in that very letter, to his son’s delight, indulged in a bit of Remez, saying: “*My son! it is necessary for you to know that Pharaoh, king of Egypt, in truth, symbolizes the creator of Evil; that Israel, as its composite name indicates, are also, truly speaking, a mirror of a subject in intellectuality; that Moses, our Master, peace be unto him!, is theoretically the image of Divine Intellect; that Egypt as a whole mirrors theoretically the entire body, the main members whereof represent the officers, and the rest are slaves in theory to those officers; that the land of Goshen represents the seat of the heart. Know, therefore, that it was Israel, the Intellect, over which the wicked Pharaoh had dominion and made it perform the workingman’s labor by means of slavery. As for Moses, there was no possible contact between him and them, and they could not rule over him, etc.*”

\(d\) A speculative philosophy which deals in abstracts only, and reaches out to zones far beyond the physical interpretations applied to the text of the Torah by the foremer three is called - Sod. Every letter, every accent, every crown-mark, every syllable receives new meaning under the microscopical scrutiny of Sod. The hidden mysteries of the Creator, creation and being become fascinating revelations in the blazing glory of Sod. But around a flaming sword no one can sketch a circle with bare hands. Before any one can reach out for the last horizon of intellectual attainment he must traverse a myriad of regions first, then if he beholds part of the light which the rays of that horizon recollect he may advance a step forward. In his “*Secret Roll of Parchment*”, Maimonides, although reputed to have disregarded the study and instruction of theosophy, praises the Kabala and indicates that Exodus, 25.22. is a key to this science. Evidently what he did disregard, and rightly so, was operative Kabala, which modern scholars interest as the “mystic theosophy” of the “Hebrews”. The truth of the matter, however, can be found by each scholar in attempting to study Speculative Kabala. He will then find that what the physicists discover by means of higher mathematics, the theological philosopher, a searcher of the solution to the greatest problem this side of the grave, will discover by means of Speculative Kabala. Sod, the fourth and highest light in the firmament of Jewish learning, is a branch of Kabala.

With these lights and working-tools it is difficult to find the reason, or even the apology for the existence, not to say the need, of high or low critics of the Bible. Lack of full knowledge of the extent of the Talmudic and Midrashic literature, with particular reference to the searching character of the numerous commentaries upon the Bible, is invariably found to be the source whence Bible criticism sprang forth. Conjectural amendments, due to ignorance in most instances, were taken in exchange for the Massoritic text of the Torah, with the resultant chaos as the price of that folly. Because civilization has advanced as far as it did, because of the Natural Application of science in this Machine Age, the Bible as a Book of Life is most necessary in order to keep the social structure happy and to prevent its disintegration.

Because Christian sectarianism could not digest the coeval origin of Hebrew consonants and vowels, a school of Controversialists was founded and, after the Reformation, in order to create room for theological and dogmatical religiosity, a school of Textual criticism was established. Becoming, during the past two centuries, almost universally recognized as an aid to the student among Protestants, it crossed the boundary-line and invaded Judaism, that is to say the unripe minds whose extent of knowledge of the Bible, the Talmud, the commentaries, the hermeneutic rules, and the authenticity of the Massoritic text was limited to average passing-marks during a few semesters of study in some Jewish seminary wherein secular work has the upper hand, indeed where the future Rabbi’s college degree is the main objective.

In brief, the story of the origin of higher criticism, upon which the foundation of that school was laid, is this: In 1753, a French physician, named Joan Astruc, published a book under the title of, “*Conjectures les memoires originaux dont il parait que Moyse s’est servi pour composer le livre de la Genese*”, wherein he propounded the this is that Moses had the use of several different documents out of which he constructed the Book of Genesis. As evidence Astruc cites the use of the Name ELOHIM in one part of the Book and YAHWEH in the other. With this as a starting point Protestants in Holland and in England, followed by violent colleagues in Germany, proceeded to construct a vast literature out of which the school for higher criticism sprang forth.

Loss than a century later, pioneers of the Reform movement among the Jews of Germany and of the Hashkalah movement in Russia found in higher criticism much to their liking and slowly but surely drew near it. The Torah had thus become an opinionated instrument in the hands of theirreverent. The followers of the Massoritic text, at first, did not realize the great, important and far-reaching eventualities higher criticism had in store for religion generally and remained, either indifferent or scoffed at the very notion that few atheist would indent a most telling chapter along the path of progressive thinking. This was particularly true among the vast majority of Russian Rabbis and Orthodox scholars, who refused even to dignify the leaders of the movement by talking about them.

As a matter of historic truth, the Talmudic authorities, centuries before the Controversialists, made the declaration that the Hebrew vowels, in so far as the pronunciation is concerned, are not of Sinaitic origin but an innovation of a much later period. The vowels are employed to facilitate reading but never as a means of changing the meaning of the text. In the Scroll of the Pentateuch the text consists of the Ot—letter—and Taag—crown-mark only; the *Nekudah*—vowel—and *Taam*—accent—are eliminated from the text. The Sephardic Jews use the traditional pronunciation of Spain, Africa and, as they claim, Palestine, whereas the Ashkenazim use a pronunciation singularly their own; but in either instance the meaning of the text remains the same.

The remnant few in the succeeding generations since the days of Moses were not only sages but prophets too. They found and established rules to protect the steadfastness of the Massoritic text and to prevent any power from breaking down the fences they had builded around the Vineyard of God. In fact, the very remnant few did it all by pointing out every apparent contradiction in the Pentateuch and provided the reconciliation thereto. Every letter, every syllable, every word, every Verse received careful study and analysis. There are more than sixteen thousand source references, collected by many scholars and published in the Hebrew editions of the Pentateuch, in the Talmud, Sifre, Sifra, Mehilta and Tosefta alone, not to mention the Midrashic literature, all of which define, clarify, reconcile, interpret and elucidate all of the difficult textual matter in the Torah. This treasure is now an open book to the English-speaking world. The author made a complete translation of all SUCH REFERENCES and had it published as a supplement to the edition of the Pentateuch to which he gave an English version of the commandments in harmony with the Massoritic text and the Talmudic deductions (1936).

“*One in a city, and two in a family*”, was the prophet’s dream concerning the number of the remnant few. Maimonides, in his introduction to the “*Mishnah Torah*”, speaks of the “*remnant few*” as the God-chosen in each generation upon whom He calls to steady the wavering ranks. The sponsors of Roman, Greek, Egyptian, Babylonian, Arabic, Spanish, Italian, French, German, Russian, British and American assimilation successively faded into nothingness along side the remnant few. An hundred generations of well-meaning, and often very able such sponsors discovered, too late for themselves, that neither the Torah nor Israel can be rationalized. They erred in one particular point, which, in Jewish history, is most important: they thought that the Torah is the product of Israel, whereas, in truth, Israel is the product of the Torah. The different interpretations of the philosophy of Jewish history, given during the centuries, never became firmly established as a means of instruction because theories are inapplicable to matters which transcend natural phenomena.

After Moses and Joshua came the Elders, the prophets, the Men of the Great Assembly, the Tannaim, the Amoraim, the Saboraim and the Gaonim, which closed with the Gaon Hai son of Sheriro Gaon (1038), and Rabbinism was established. Thus, nine centuries elapsed since the Rabbi became supreme in Israel as the Urim and Thummim—Light and Perfection—in interpreting the Word of God. Since the Rabbi is the direct successor of the scholar and not of the priest, Rabbinism is not confused with the priesthood. The office of Rabbi, is not an office at all. It is neither hereditary nor elective. It is not at all clerical. In the majority of instance the early Rabbis were physicians, fronting their divine authority, attained by their great position as scholars, by a lay calling out of which they derived a livelihood. The extraordinary feature of this system, or lack of it, was the unanimity of obedience in given territories to the decisions of their respective Rabbis. With the disappearance of the Exilarch system after a rule of a thousand years, democracy in Jewish learning returned back to Israel.

Modern conditions of life, emigration of millios and expulsion of tens of thousands of Jews from their native lands, beginning with the Spanish Inquisition and expulsion, changed the position of the Rabbi a great deal, in that, that they were forced to accept the mandate of the newly-founded communities, and often even their bounty, so as to enable them to devote all of the learning at their command to the use of their respective districts. However, some of the greatest Rabbis even in modern times refused to accept office or bounty and made their livelihood from secular callings. The outstanding examples were the Elijah, the Gaon of Vilna during the eighteenth century and Israel Meir ha-Kohen, the “Hafez Haim” of Radin in our own times.

The only ancient Institution which remained steadfast and unaffected by the radical changes of the centuries was the Yeshivah. Learning, in all branches that the term Yeshivah implies, remained as democratic as it was throughout the Jewish history. Modern educators are craving for the democracy of learning in the universities which the Yeshivah enjoyed, and the ultimate in the freedom of the student in the higher spheres of learning, in so far as selection of subjects, reading and examinations are concerned, will be just what the Yeshivah always was. The Yeshivah, however, was never intended to become a professional school, out of which Tannaim, Amoraim, Saburaim, Gaonim, or Rabbis should graduate and go out to practice. Even during the era of the Exilarchs the Yeshivah was not reduced to the grade of a school of professional Gaonism, although the Exilarchs often conferred the degree of Gaon regardless of merit, for political, or other reasons.

In recent years, due to many causes brought about during the last half of the Nineteenth Century Reform and Hashkalah, the Rabbi, in most Jewries, particularly in the New World, became a real professional entity. That view of the people called forth the establishment of Jewish seminaries, and the study of the “Rabbinical profession” became a feature of Jewish life. The chief cause of this radical change was the great demand of the synagogue in most communities for preachers, particularly for such as are able to preach in the native language of the respective Jewries, both in Europe and in the New World. That persistent demand forced the different seminaries to grant a Rabbinical degree to the student at the termination of a very limited space of time. Hebrew grammar, Bible, Homiletics, theology, Jewish history, elementary Jewish philosophy, a scanty course in Rabbinics and—with fair passing-marks the student was ordained and given a community to lead. Now, all of the named subjects never belonged to the inner walls of either the Yeshivah or the Beth-ha-Midrash. Excepting theology, philosophy and homiletics, those studies belonged to the primary school—Heder—, as for the rest the student usually had to shift for himself about them.

The Yeshivah was a real democratic seat of learning, where the student was expected to acquire a complete and through knowledge of Talmud and Poskim. Only after he did acquire such knowledge did he consider whether or not he should pursue other studies. That, of course, mainly depended on his own judgment. History reveals that there were many great Rabbis who had never even attempted to deliver a sermon. That function was left to the Maggid. Traditionally the Rabbi was expected to deliver two sermons annually, on the Sabbath before the Day of Atonement and on the Sabbath preceding the Passover Festival. But even these two occasions were not employed by the Rabbi to exhibit his homiletical ability but were rather devoted to a discussion in Halakah, out of which a great moral was deducted. Of such dimensions was the Yeshivah from the days of Jabneh till the close of the glorieous existence of Vahlozin.

Historically, the horizon of the Rabbi is limitless. Like unto the Torah itself the Rabbi, in the past nine centuries, had to undergo many interpretations as to his functions in the community. But no one ever attempted to restrain him from interpreting the Torah, unless he be lacking in the necessary knowledge. Now, in order to be able to attend to his chief function the Rabbi had to have a working knowledge of Peshat, Derash, Remez and Sod. Of course, to these branches of higher learning there is no limit, but a comprehensive understanding was the minimum requirement. Because of this knowledge, plus the Halakah Mekuyemet—sustained law—of the entire Talmud as a minimum requirement to receive Semichah—ordination—, the average age of the youngest Rabbis was not under thirty. Taken in consideration that the Rabbis attended no secular schools it required a quarter of a century of study, without vacation, to obtain the degree of Rabbi.

Thus, the first prerequisite of the Rabbi, from time immemorial, was to know the full scope of the Torah, Written and Oral, in order to be able, when in office, to lead his community. The Pashtan—using mainly Peshat to elucidate the Torah—and the Darshan—using mainly Derash—, are exemplified in history. The greatest Darshan among the ancient Rabbis was Ben Zoma, of whom the Mishnah speaks in glowing terms, saying: *“When Ben Zoma died the Darshanim were set aside”* (Sotah, 15). The greatest Pashtan in post-Talmudic days was Rashi, whose commentary upon the Bible remains matchless till this day. Of course, the other great Darshanim after Ben Zoma and great Pashtanim after Rashi received full recognition, but the outstanding fame of both is best evidence that Rabbinical influence was not limited to any particular field of learning.

Maimonides, in his Introduction to the Commentary on the Seder Zeraim had promised to elucidate the Derash, which, for some reason, he did not do it, but his son, Abraham, while not a treatise of elucidation on the Derashoth, did leave behind him a system how to identify a Derash in the Talmud, or Midrash, and how to classify all of the Derashoth, which he established to be five kinds. And, because of the great value of the service Abraham Maimon rendered to Darshanim, the essence of his thesis is herein given: “*My father, of blessed memory! thought of compiling a book to elucidate the Derash, as he made mention of it in the introduction to the Mishnah; but in the end he turned away and did not compile it. Moses feared to come near it, as he said in his introduction to the Guide—Moreh Nebuhim—, but I did not turn away from it... And, after this preface, with a prayer to the Name that He aid me to understand the truth and preserve it!, I say: All of the Derashoth which are to be found either in the Talmud or elsewhere among the works of the sages are five kinds, which are classified as follows:*

I. “*These are Derashoth in their ordinary meaning, wherein there was no other thought, save alone their simplicity, intended. And, although this class of Derash is self-understood and there is need to cite an example, I will, nevertheless, impart to you a quotation of the Talmud as an example to add its clarification. For instance: “Said Rabbi Johanan in the name of Rabbi Simon Ben Yohai, ‘It is forbidden to fill one’s mouth with laughter in this world, for it is said; Then will our mouth be filled with laughter”* (Berachos 31a; Tehillim. 26.2).

II\. “*These are Derashoth which have an outward and an inner meaning, and the spirit and not the letter must be followed in expounding it. The outer appearance was put in a form directly contradictory to the inner intent for good reasons, most of, but not all such Derashoth were long since expounded in the Moreh Nebuhim and in the Commentary of the Mishnah. To quote an example: Said Rabbi Elazar, ‘The Holy One, blessed be He! will arrange a dance for the just—Zadikim—and He will be in their midst, in the Garden of Eden, and each one of them will point with his index finger, even as it is said: And he will say in that day lo, this is our God, for whom we waited, we will be glad and rejoice in his salvation’*” *(Taanit, 31, last paragraph of tractate; Yeshayahu 25:9). No one endowed with sense, and no believer in God, will accept this Derash in its outer garb and believe it. But the true subject which was in the mind of Rabbi Elazar referred to the reward in store in the World to Come for the just, that reward being the ability of each one to attain such understanding of God that they could not under any circumstances have attained on this earth. And, this is the extreme end of good with none higher. The joy thereof he (Rabbi Elazar) likened to the joy of a festive dance. In like manner he pictured the joy of every individual. Zadik when he will find out that he can reach out for that which he never could hope to reach, and thus each one will point his index finger with pride.*”

These are Derashoth which have no inner subject but the point to either is plainly indicated in the Peshat alone. Yet, to know the Peshat of the text and understand it is very difficult to the majority of students. Either it is impossible to comprehend the subject matter, or, if it be not lacking in that the subject itself will appear wanting, or wrong. Often, because there are so many Derashoth of this type, one will behold a paradox, both, because of the oddity of the subject and because of their co-mingling. In this particular, this class is alike to the preceding class, because of its difficult text and hidden meaning, save only that in some instances it is still more difficult and hidden so that there can be no comparison between the two. It is, therefore, necessary to be careful in giving any exposition and deciding upon an interpretation of the class of Derashot treated herein, lest one who does it in haste arrives at an error and loses his way entirely. For example: “*Let one ever cause the Yezer Tov—creator of good—to cast fear upon the Yezer ho-Raa—creator of evil, even as it is said: Tremble, and sin not. If he conquered the Yezer ho-Raa, it is well, if not, he should read the SHEMAH, even as it is said: Commune with your heart upon your on bed. If he conquered the Yezer ho-Raa, it is well, if not, he shall remind himself of the day of his death*” (Berachos 5a.; Tehillim 4:5). Now, this derash is TO BE INTERPRETED IN ITS LITERAL SENSE ONLY, for there is no hidden meaning, nor no inner thought concealed in the text; but the text itself creates the great difficulty in understanding it. The abstract terms of Yezer ho-Raa and Yezer Tov are mysteries in themselves. Likewise are causes mentioned hard to understand, and the remedies themselves are not comprehensible. But I will endeavor to make its literal meaning simple by giving the text a proper interpretation. The Yezer ho-Raa and the Yezer Tov are not outside forces, for they exist within the body and soul of every individual. The Yezer ho-Raa represents the evil side, and the Yezer Tov the good side in every person. The textual language, too, is literal. One should cast fear upon his evil side, should conquer it and dominate it. He should be master of himself by ruling his being with the good that is in him. If he finds himself failing, he should read the sections of the SHEMA which will remind him both, of his weakness and of his strength, of his weakness, even as the Verse teaches, “*Commune with your heart upon your bed*”, to remind him how uncertain his health is, how easy it is to lose it and be bedridden; of his strength, by reminding him that he may be holy before God. Then, if the evil within him still appears to be stronger, he must remind himself of the day of his death...

IV\. “The interpretation of Verses in the Torah if a poetic sense, by utilizing words entirely foreign to the intent of Verse, but giving them, nevertheless, an entirely new and beautiful significance. For example: ‘Thou shalt surely tithe all the increase of thy seed, that which is brought forth in the field year by year’—Asair to—Asair—(Devarim 14-22). The accepted meaning of the verse is obvious and not disputed. But, Rabbi Johanan says: ‘Asair—give the tithe so that you may become rich—She—Tisasaiy (Tannit 9a; Shabbos 119a). A word play, based upon the beauty and poetic expression of the Hebrew language. Or, for example: ‘Now Jethro, the priest of Midian, Moses’ father-in-law, heard of all that God had done for Moses, and for Israel His people’ etc. (Shemos 18:1), Rabbi Joshua says: He had heard the war with Amalek etc. Rabbi Eliezer ha-Modayi says: He had heard of Revelation’ etc. (Zevachim, 116a) The opinion of neither was based upon fact, for then it could not be a matter of opinion; but both planned their opinions according to the covert meaning of the word Vayishma. Or, for example: ‘And the Canaanite, the king of Arad’ etc. (Bamidbar 33:40), We have studied, Sihon, Arad and Canaanite are one and same person; he is called Sihon because he is like desert mule; Canaan, because of his nationality, and Arad because that was his name’ (Rosh Hashana 3a.q.v.). Neither is this word-play based upon fact. But the truth is that no one but a fool, or unreasonable person would protest against this kind of Derash. In this class of Derash are included many divisions of many subjects, even as the poets’ subjects are numerous”...

V. “Derashoth coated in legends, parables and exaggerations, such as for example: And Azel had six sons, whose names are these: Azrikam, Bocru, and Ishmael, and Sheariah, and Obadiah, and Hanan. All these were the sons of Azel, (I Divrei HaYamim 8:37-38) Said Mar-Zutra: From the Azel to the last Azel a load of Derash requiring four hundred camels to carry can be found’(Pesahim 62b).[^1]

But this kind of Derash, in comparison to other divisions, is small indeed. However, the great mass of stories come under this heading, which, in turn, are divided in four categories, the first being the category of stories which have actually happened, and these stories, too, are subdivided in four different categories.

\(a\) 1st Stories out of which a benefit is derived in establishing laws as for example: Did it not once upon a time happen that the Elders of Shammai and the Elders of Hillel went to visit Rabbi Johanan Ben Hahornit and they found him sitted with his head and most of his body in the Sukkah but his table was set in his house? Stories of this type are, indeed, too many to enumerate.

2d Stories out of which a benefit is derived in establishing ethical rules of conduct, or ideal ways of living as, for example: The Rabbis expounded: Let one ever be humble as Hillel and not as impatient as Shammai. Once upon a time two people made a bet etc. (Shabbos 30b - 31a). The lessor taught is too obvious to require further interpretation, and similar to this story are many.

3d Stories out of which a benefit is derived in establishing a principle in the fundamentals of Faith, as for example: Once upon a time they said to Honi (Onias) Hameaggel, pray and let the rain come down etc. (Ta’anis 19a) Like stories are numerous in the Talmud, and are brought out to sustain the belief that God hearkens to the prayers of His servants, the Zaddikim.

4d Stories which manifest either extraordinary reasoning or pave a way for psychological insight as, for example: Once upon a time, Rabbi Meir, Rabbi Judah and Rabbi Jesse were travelling, and they lodged in a certain Inn. Said Rabbi Meir to the Innkeeper, My son, what is your name? Kidor, answered he. They gave to the Innkeeper their valuables, but Rabbi Meir refused etc. (Yoma 83b). Rabbi Meir interpreted the name of the Inn-keeper Kidor to mean the same as the two words *Ki Dor* of Devarim, 32.20 - ‘For they are a very froward generation - . The remarkable thing about this story is, that Rabbi Meir was right in refusing to trust the Inn-keeper.’

\(b\) Stories in the form of dreams, as for example: ‘The Rabbis learned: Said Rabbis Ishmael, once upon a time I entered to burn incense in the Inner Sanctum etc’. (Berachos,7c). Or visions related in the form of a story, or stories whorning mention of demons is made, even as my father, of blessed memory! expounded concerning these type of narration in the Moreh Nebuchim.

\(c\) Stories which really did happen, but which are related in exaggerated language, knowing that no one with a reasonable understanding would fail to get kernel and throw away the chaff. The sages permitted themselves to do it under the well-established rule in exegesis that, ‘The Torah at times speaks in exaggerated language, so do the prophets, and so do the sages’ (Tamid 29a). Under this heading the following examples may be cited: Rabba and Rabbi Zeira feasted on Purim together and became intoxicated. Rabbi was so roused that he slaughtered his friend. The day after, he prayed, and his friend recovered. It surely is not to be inferred that he severed Rabbi Zeira’s trachea and gullet and actually killed him. He wonded him sorely in the neck. Likewise, the following: Rabbi Haninai Ben Hachinai...returning home, after an absence of ten (twelve) years in the Yeshivah, could not recognize his native city because of the many changes in construction. Sitting near the river he heard some one calling a girl Miss Hachinai, Miss Hachnai, let us go home. Thereupon Haninai followed the girl for he understood that she must be his daughter. When his wife saw him, the emotion affected her heart and the spirit of life left her! Whereupon he delivered this prayer: Lord of the universe! Is this the reward of this poor soul? I pray to have mercy upon her. And she was resurrected! (Kesubos 62b). Now it surely was not death that she was stricken by, but merely a fainting spell.

\(d\) “True stories, wrapped in a cover of allegory, parable, or riddle, as for example, the following: Once upon a time there were two Ethiopians standing before king Solomon. Elihoreph and Ahijah, the sons of Shisha were Solomon’s secretaries (I Melachim 4:3). On that day they saw the Angel of Death in a mood of sadness, and they asked him: Why are you sad? And he answered: Because I was instructed from above to end the lives of these two Ethiopians. Whereupon they were turned over to demons, with instructions to send them to Mehozah Delah, a cown where the Angel of death never comes. When they reached Mehozah Delah in due time, they died. On the morrow Solomon’s secretaries saw the Angel of death laughing, and they asked him: Why are you laughing? Because, said he, you sent them to very place where they were wanted. Whereupon Solomon exclaimed: The feet of a man are his sureties; to waterer place he desires to go, there they lead him. (Sukkah 52b). The story itself, no rational being will accept. But there is no doubt that the two men came to Solomon for advice. They probably were sick, in body, or in spirit. They were wandering from place to place to find a cure! No doubt Solomon knew them. In his wisdom, he advised them to change the climate, and they did. But they died. Their time had come. The decree of God, no amount of wisdom can change. The demons, the Angel of Death and the other language, was employed in allegoric style!...”[^2]

Now, in order to derive the very last particle of benefit of these words of wisdom of Abraham Ben Maimon, it is necessary to place any quotation from the Talmud or Midrash, or even from the Torah itself, in its proper classification. The only question remaining then, will be, Is the subject to be in harmony with this category of Derash? Each Porosha in the Torah offers a number of subjects, either in Derash or in Halakah. But care must be taken in the selection of both, the subject and the material. To facilitate both, the Rabbi will find in this work ready source references on all the Poroshath of the Torah. The sermon on each and every Poroshah, is only intended as an example of the kind of homiletical discourse fits in with the subject selected from the Poroshah.

In order to be able to derive a forceful lesson, in so far as it is lending itself thereto, from the Halakah, two works are herewith highly recommended for the purpose i.e. The “Sheiltot” by Rav Ahi Meshabhi, and the “Halokot Gedolot. Both these works contain the essence of the entire structure of Halakah. The foremer is constructed in the Talmudic language, and easily understood; the latter, however, requires a thorough knowledge of Aramaic, as it is constructed entirely in the language of the Talmud Yerushalmi. But as it is arranged according to the Poroshath of the Torah, no Rabbi will find it too difficult to understand. In looking up the source references given at the end of each sermon in this work, and in looking the treatise of the Sheiltot on the Poroshah, the Rabbi, or student, will master all of the branches of homiletics in all their beauty and forcefulness.

And, as this work is unique in more than one way, particularly because it reproduced from the original manuscript prepared upon a typewriter by the author, who, since the first day of Rosh-ha-Shanah, 5697 (Thursday, September 17, 1936) is an invalid, due to a heart attack, the Rabbi, or student, will find in it more than a sample of Peshat, Derash, Remez and Sod, in addition to all of the categories of Talmudic quotations enumerated by Abraham, son of Maimon. It is, moreover, the author’s intention not offer this work for sale, either to the general public, or to the type of free-lances generally. The edition is a very limited one, published at a great sacrifice, under the most trying circumstances, so that each copy is practically a manuscript. Care was taken in every instance to check every source reference, with inavoidable limitations, of course, in so far as typographical errors and some divisions of words at the end of some lines. But, as the whole is a reproduction of typewriting, the study of the work will be found to be much easier on the eyes than ordinary books are.
