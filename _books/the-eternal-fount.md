## Influence of the Aggadah on Jewish Life

*By way of introduction*

<span dir="rtl">רצונך להכיר את מי שאמר והיה העולם למוד אגדה</span> “If thou desires to comprehend the greatness of Him at Whose word the universe was created, study the Aggadah” (Sifri, Ekeb).

<span dir="rtl">האגדות הנם אמצעים יקרים לקדש על ידיהן שמו של הקב</span>”<span dir="rtl">ה</span> “The aggadot are the most precious means whereby to sanctify the name of the Holy One blessed be He” (Yalkut, Psalms, 672). The powerful influence exerted by religious faith over Jewish life can be properly understood and evaluated only through the concepts of Divinity and of life’s essence as expounded in the Aggadah.

The Halakah, that portion of the Written and Oral Laws which prescribes the laws and commandments, the positive and negative rules of man’s proper conduct toward God and toward neighbor, has rendered the Jewish nation <span dir="rtl">חטיבה אחת בעולם</span> “utterly unique on earth.” The Aggadah, with its remarkable religious presentations, moral precepts, and elucidation of human reward and punishment, has given Israel a special heavenly niche. As the Halakah demonstrated how the Jew may attain eternal life, the Aggadah provided the purpose, the motive, for desiring immortality.

<span dir="rtl">אגדה אינו אוסר ואינו מתיר</span> “The Aggadah neither forbids nor permits” (Jerus. Horayot, 5,3). It merely offers counsel as to the proprieties of conduct which will assure joy of life in this world and the world to come. It solaces man in hardship, bids him not despair in adversity. “The main purpose of the Aggadah,” declares Leopold Zunz (Gottesdienstliche Vortraege), “is to bind heaven and earth together, to bring heaven nearer mankind and to elevate man toward heaven.”

It is noteworthy that the Aggadah was ever more completely accepted by Jewry as persecutions and misfortunes multiplied. When the murky clouds swept over Jewish skies, it was the aggadists, the homilists, who offered comfort to the people, who with their divine portraitures drove off despair and gave them faith to live and fight on for their ideals.

Immediately after destruction of the first temple this phenomenon became apparent. The distinguished aggadists of the era were R. Johanan ben Zaccai, Rabban Gamliel, R. Joshua ben Hananiah, R. Ishmael, R. Akiba, R. Tarphon, R. Eliezer ben Hyrcanus, R. Jose the Galilean, R. Eleazar ben Azariah, and many others. Their example was followed by the amoraim, geonim, rabbis, and preachers of all ages. Their words had a continuing power over Jewish life, helping to maintain popular courage and the faith that renders the Jewish race immortal.

In exposition of Holy Writ, Halakah seeks the direct meaning of the text, while Aggadah searches out its inner purpose. Halakah is patent; Aggadah is revelation. Halakah is the prosaic research of the intellect; Aggadah appeals poetically to the heart, the emotions.

It may be well to refer to several instances wherein the Aggadah reveals the hidden concepts, the thoughts between the lines, in the biblical text.

<span dir="rtl">וירא אלהים את כל אשר עשה והנה טוב מאד</span> “And God saw all that He had made, and behold it was very good.” To this obvious declaration the Midrash adds, “The words ‘very good’ mean the evil inclination. For <span dir="rtl">בלי יצר הרע לא היה ישוב בעולם</span> ‘Without the evil inclination there would be no settlement of humans in the universe’ ” (Bereshit Rabbah 42). There is a profundity of wisdom in this appended animadversion.

It is asked, Why was Abraham called the Hebrew (Ivri)? Because <span dir="rtl">כל העולם כולו מעבר אחד והוא מעבר אחד</span> “All the universe stands on one side (ever), and he stands on the other” (ibid.). This one phrase has more homiletic power than all the interpretations and explanations of linguistic study.

Of Adam God spoke, <span dir="rtl">אעשה לו עזר כנגדו</span> “I will make him a help meet for (against) him.” The Midrash, employing the dual meaning of the final word kenegdo, thus comments, <span dir="rtl">זכה עוזרתו, לא זכה כנגדו</span> “If a man prove worthy, his wife is indeed his helpmeet; if unworthy, she becomes his opponent (and embitters his life)” (Jebamot 63).

Often the Aggadah expresses a deeper meaning in some halakah: <span dir="rtl">כקטן כגדול תשמעון</span> “Ye shall hear the small as well as the great” (Deut. i, 17). The midrashic comment on this phrase reads:

<span dir="rtl">שיהא חביב עליך דין של פרוטה כדין של מאה מנה</span> “That you may be bent on righteous judgment in litigation concerning value of the smallest coin as in litigation that is concerned with value of much gold” (Sanhedrin 8).

The Aggadah has been specially helpful in maintaining Israel’s nationalist spirit. Its wondrous songs of praise of Palestine, its inspiring tales of the Land, have fostered the Jewish hope to return to the ancient national home. Always this literature has declared that, despite present oppression, if the Jews will obey their Holy Law and maintain their faith, their nation’s redemption is certain to come.

When the editors of “The Day” requested me to contribute the series herein reproduced, I determined to present the aggadic conception of many passages in the weekly portion, particularly of those concerning Jewish nationalism. Each contribution was limited to two columns, in which space I could not offer more than this major viewpoint. It is my intention, however, to prepare a new series which will illuminate the many other lofty ideals of the Torah, on the same basis of aggadic interpretation.

I have tried to indicate the sources of the many homiletic comments here employed. It has been my purpose to provide the reader with the true intent of the aggadic phrases, as I understand them. I have also made use of the comments of noted preachers of all times, with due credit.

If I have succeeded in clarifying but one passage in the Torah or one paragraph from Talmud or Midrash, <span dir="rtl">והיה זה שכרי</span> that will be sufficient reward for my efforts.

H. L. Selig

New York, 5700

## Translator's Note

In 1932 I had occasion to compile and edit an anthology of sermons by American rabbis on the fifty-four annual portions of the week. In my preface therein I protested against the inferior books and plays, the current news stories and political aberrations, which had become the texts of countless synagogue addresses. It was my purpose to “see a compilation of Jewish sermons that reverted to the old forms and manners.”

Whatever strictures were expressed or implied in that anthology (somewhat flamboyantly titled “The Books of Moses”) applied, of course, only to the sermon in English, and hardly at all to those delivered or written in Yiddish and Hebrew. For the old time orthodox Jewish preacher has always found his inspiration in the words of Holy Writ, and has always been more intent on preserving traditional Jewish values than on uttering apparent profundities that might catch the eye of newspaper religious editors.

It was with interest and pleasure, therefore, that I perused Mr. Harris L. Selig’s “*Fun Ebigen Kval*” in the original. For here were sixty discourses on the weekly portions and the holidays that were filled with biblical, talmudic, and midrashic texts; that declaimed in favor of Torah and Palestine and all Jewish loyalties; that even in their discursive portions sought with single-mindedness to bring the people of Israel back to the Law of Israel.

In another respect these were not ordinary sermons. Limited by the restrictions of their original newspaper publication, they were more exactly material for homilies than simple addresses. Within their few pages they contained as many as a dozen good ideas, each one of which might be expanded into a full-length sermon. Here was an opportunity to present to English homilists and readers much of the religious material that delighted—and improved—our forbears.

On this ground I undertook the translation of Mr. Selig’s work. I have tried to retain his terseness and clarity. In almost every case I have sought and included the actual source of quotations. Generally, transliterations of Hebrew works have followed the spelling of modern scholarship. If errors have remained in my rendition, they in no wise qualify the value and usefulness of the compilation.

*Abraham Burstein*
