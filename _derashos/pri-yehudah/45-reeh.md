“Behold, I set before you this day a blessing and a curse. The blessing, if you shall hearken unto the commandments of the Lord your God, which I command you this day” (Deuteronomy 11:26-27).

The Parshah begins with the word “REEH,” “see,” as speaking to an individual and continues with the word “LIFNECHEM,” “before you,” in the plural form. It, like any other Parshah, teaches us not only the commandments of God, but also, from the unique construction of sentences do we learn important lessons.

Good and evil, blessings and curses, depend on us alone. With the creation of the world God also created everything necessary for man’s happiness. God gives everyone the same opportunity, but there are no two people whose wants and needs are alike. Many a time we find that certain things prove to be a blessing for one, and a curse for another. Good health is considered a blessing, which it is. But to some it may well be a curse. A healthy person, in the midst of a family of weaklings, may be used unmercifully by the others to such a degree until he too would wish to be sick for a while. One of the Jewish poets once said: “Do not profane the power of the weak for therein rests great strength.”

Wealth is also normally considered a blessing. Yet we find in Ecclesiastes 5:12, “There is a sore evil which I have seen under the sun, (namely) riches reserved for their owner to his own hurt.” Yes! Everything in life can be a blessing to one and a curse to the other. It wholly depends on the individual and what he makes of it. Time and place also play a big part in that factor.

The Torah points to every individual with the words “REEH,” “see.” See it, observe it for yourself. Choose the things that fit you best. Do not look with envy at the other side of the fence. For, that which is good for others may not be good for you. Our sages said: “K’SHEM SHEPARTZUFEIHEN ENON DOMOT, KACH DEOTEIHEN ENON SHAVOT,” “In the same way as their outer appearances are not alike, so are their characters not the same.” Therefore, the same thing may be a blessing to one and a curse to the other. Our eyes are many times blinded by the glitter of gold and fail to see it in its true perspective and how it would fit into our lives.

The Torah helps us make the right choice: “The blessing, if ye shall hearken unto the commandments of the Lord your God . . .” (Deuteronomy 11:27-28). Yes! When man performs the commandments of the Lord, that alone makes him happy. “The Lord’s precepts are right, gladdening the heart, the Lord’s commandment is clear, enlightening the eyes” (Psalm 19:8).

The Great Sage of Wilno said: “If for doing a good deed I would be punished and for doing an evil deed I would receive a reward, I would nevertheless do good deeds. For the joy one has in doing the good deeds is a great reward in itself.” As the sages said in Avot 4:2, “The reward of a good deed is a good deed, and the reward of sin is sin (Virtue is its own reward, and sin its own penalty).”

With every MITZVAH we perform, we bring God and His blessings closer to us. The Midrash R. on Parshat Trumah said: “AMAR HAKADOSH BARUCH HU L’YISRAEL, MACHARTI LACHEM TORATI, K’VAYOCHOL NIMKARTI IMA.” “Said God to Israel: When I handed over to you My Torah, I also handed Myself over to you.” Yes, when God is with us we are blessed. When one chooses to live by the precepts of the Torah he is assured of blessing, for God is with him.

Further in the parshah, speaking of the Promised Land, it says: “For ye are not as yet come to the rest and to the inheritance, which the Lord your God gives thee” (Deuteronomy 12:9). Again here, the beginning is in plural and the end is in singular. This is to denote that even the Promised Land does not affect everyone alike. When we hear a tourist complaining about Israel, we easily know that he needs to be straightened out. He is the one who needs to improve so the blessings of the land of Israel, which is God’s choice for His people, may rest upon him also. Yes, God’s choice and blessings for us are always perfect, but we must attune ourselves to them.

The parshah continues with the law of “MASSER,” “tithe,” and here too the Torah speaks to the individual as it is said, “Thou shalt surely tithe all the increase of thy seed, that which is brought forth in the field year by year” (Deuteronomy 14:22-27). Again, when the Torah speaks of the law of “SH’MITAH,” it uses the singular form: “At the end of every seven years thou shalt make a release. And this is the manner of the release: every creditor shall release that which he has lent unto his neighbour, he shall not exact it of his neighbour and his brother; because the Lord’s release has been proclaimed” (Deuteronomy 15:1-2). Also, concerning the law of “TZEDAKAH,” “charity,” the Torah speaks to each individual (Deuteronomy 15:7-8). — When commanding the observance of the three festivals, Passover (Deuteronomy 16:1-8), the Feast of Weeks (Shavuot) (Deuteronomy 16:9-12), and the Feast of Tabernacles, the Torah also speaks to the individual.

In all of the above, the Torah points to every man: “Because that for this thing the Lord thy God will bless thee in all thy work and in all that thou puttest thy hand unto” (Deuteronomy 15:10). — When man learns to share his blessings with others, as in “MAASER,” and “TZEDAKAH,” when he makes the feast days a joyous occasion not only for himself alone, but also for the people about him, then the blessings of God will rest upon him, as it is said: “And thou shalt rejoice before the Lord thy God, thou, and thy son, and thy daughter, and thy man-servant, and the Levite that is within thy gates, and the stranger, and the fatherless, and the widow that are in the midst of thee . . . ” (Deuteronomy 16:11). Rashi comments: “These four are Mine,” says God, “corresponding to the four who are yours, namely, son, daughter, man-servant and maid-servant. If you make My four happy, I will make your four happy.”

All of the above, regarding the sharing of the blessings given him by God, could only be spoken to the individual. No other person can tell in what capacity he is blessed and to what extent he can share it with others.

In summary, the parshah begins with the advice to each and everyone of us to choose the blessings that befit us individually, and then the Torah calls upon everyone of us to share the blessings that we possess with others. Then will God’s blessing be unceasingly upon us.
