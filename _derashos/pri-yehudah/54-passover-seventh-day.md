“Thus the Lord saved Israel . . . And Israel saw the Egyptians dead upon the shore of the sea . . . Then sang Moses and the children of Israel this song unto the Lord” (Exodus XV).

Man, although he is the crown of creation whose mental capacity enables him to delve into the most mysterious and most hidden forces of nature and to attain the heights of the sky, remains nevertheless, no more than a man whose spirit is interwoven with five material traits which subject him together with his spirit, the divine portion within him, to earthly concepts of limitations.

After all the supernatural miracles which the Lord has revealed at the Red Sea, about which our Sages remark: “That which an ordinary servant was able to see at the sea, even the prophet Ezekiel was not able to behold,” one would expect that Moses and the people of Israel would at least convey to us some part, some portion, of those transcendent visions which would reflect for us the greatness of God. Instead, the Torah tells us, after God revealed Himself unlimited in His full Power and Glory, His people saw nothing else, except “The Egyptians dead upon the shore of the sea.” Nothing else brought them close, nothing stirred them to sing the song of redemption except the sight of the dead Egyptians on the banks of the sea. — A people liberated from slavery, a stormy sea suddenly sundered and dried so that six hundred thousand men could march through, and many other great miracles occurred during this great and epoch making era in history, miracles which the imagination can barely understand. But it seems that the Jews neither saw nor comprehended anything so clearly as when they beheld the Egyptians dead on the banks of the sea. Even the song they sang at this wonderful moment was in greatest portion dedicated to the collapse of the enemy. We read expressions such as “The horse and the chariot He cast into the sea.” They saw the magnificence of God expressed by the floundering in the waves of the chariots and legions of Pharaoh.

Our sages remark in SANHEDRIN 94, that it was a disgrace that Moses and the six hundred thousand Jews did not express the word “Blessed be God,” until Jethro came and said: “Blessed be the Lord Who has saved you from the hands of the Egyptians.” Jethro saw the magnitude of God in the redemption of Israel and seeing this great vision and this wonderful turn of events, he praised God. He paid Him tribute. But the Jews, after seeing the miracles, could only speak of and praise the fact that the enemy was drowned in the sea. This is the type of praise which God prohibited the angels from expressing. He did not allow them to sing the song of redemption since His people, his own hand work were being destroyed.

The Jewish people were much too close to those momentous events to be able to understand properly what had really occurred. The quick tempo of the miracles, the rapid change from despair to hope, somehow limited their comprehension and they only felt the things that were very close, that were tangible, that they could see with their own eyes and touch with their own hands.

It seems that our sages wish to emphasize that the song of redemption at the edge of the sea was not in full compliance with the wishes of God. A song which expresses only the appreciation of the death of humans, even if those humans were enemies, is not the true manifestation of the greatness of God.

The wheel which turns constantly, the wheel of fortune which spins rapidly in the life of man, brings moments of great exhilaration which carry one to spontaneous song. But few people will stop and think and consider that much happiness is built at the expense of someone’s loss. Man’s youth is highly praised as long as he goes uphill on the ladder of life. But actually it is at the expense of the previous generations which at that time is already going down hill. For man, though his phantasy reaches the heavens, still stands with his feet on the earth. The gravitation of the earth restrains him not only physically but also spiritually. As long as the spirit is a part of his physical structure and constitution, he is subjected and limited in accordance with the five earthly traits he possesses. His elevation and the decline of his fellow man he sees against his own earthly background. His own happiness he observes in someone else’s failure. It is easier for him to see his own growth through another’s descent. But he will fail to understand that by measuring the shortcomings of others, he will not become greater himself. Conversely, through such a comparison and measure, his aims and strivings become shrunken and reduced. “Speak not in thy heart, . . . saying: For my righteousness hath the Lord brought me in to possess this land, and that for the wickedness of these nations the Lord doth drive them out from before thee” (Deuteronomy 9:4). To measure one’s righteousness against the wickedness of others is not in accordance with the morals and the ethics of the Torah. Only God’s laws and commandments are the form by which one’s virtues must be measured, and in their determination, the aim of life reaches the desire of God.

It is one of the most difficult tasks to build a world with divine justice as it should be, on this earth that contains everything. The world as it is, is realistic, tangible, one which can be measured and calculated. The world which we feel with our flesh and see with our eyes is covered and decorated with external attractions and the human heart responds to it with happiness. The real world, the practical world, is very dear and precious even to the mind. But that is not what God wanted to accomplish through His commandments. As our sages say, “If one observes the beautiful tree or the field that is planted, then it is deemed that he is guilty of destroying his soul” (Avot). — It is not enough to aim for that which is, but rather for that which should be. God’s commandments teach man how he should be and how he should look upon the world. He himself is God’s creature. Nature, too, is the creation of God and God’s righteousness leads him and nature together towards God. — The will of God was not that a song be sung at a time of destruction, even if those destroyed were wicked.

“There was no joy in heaven,” remark our sages, “over the destruction of the wicked.” To the angels God said with rebuke: “Do you sing while My handiwork is drowned in the sea?” And to the Jews God indicated His wish in the words of the Torah, “AZ YASHIR,” using the expression “YASHIR,” “will sing,” instead of “SHAR,” “he sang.” That means the future is to be talked about rather than the present or the past. For the future indicates the time of “T’CHIYAS HAMAISIM,” the resurrection of the dead. — Only at that time will we be able truly to praise the greatness of God. For then, His miracles and the happiness of man will not be at the expense of any others who are downcast. The Divine Power will not be recognized as the power that destroys but rather as the power that gives life. Only such a song and such tribute is pleasing to God.

Our sages remark that when Nebuchadnezzar threw Chananyah, Mishael and Azaryah into the furnace, God said to Ezekiel, “Go and bring the dead back to life in the valley of Durah.” About these dead Rabbi Eliezer remarked: “They stood on their feet and they sang a song.” — Now, after the great miracle when the three had been saved from the fiery furnace, when all recognized that which they had denied before to be really so, that God is the Supreme Ruler of all creation, and they were prepared to express their tribute to God but measured His greatness according to their own concepts, then God said to Ezekiel, “Go out and bring the dead back to life.” Let that, and that alone be the yardstick of the Divine Power. That song, the song of God’s bringing life to the valley of dry bones, had been requested, for it contains the real understanding of the Creator of all life.

When the author of the Haggadah completes the first portion of that magnificent book which we use on the Seder night, he describes for us a word of tribute to God, “We shall say to Him a new song.” Not a song which praises God for the destruction of the enemy, for the collapse of the adversaries of the people of Israel. Not a song of victory or joy which is a product of someone else’s tragedy. But a song of the resurrection of the dead. Of the rebuilding of the world to the proper understanding of the Divine Power. — It is not the will of God that His strength be the result of the destruction of other nations, but the Force which brings life to the dead. As the Builder of the world and mankind. Amen.
