“And Aaron did so: he set up the lamps thereof over against the face of the candelabrum as the Eternal commanded Moses.” Rashi comments that this is stated in order to tell the praise of Aaron, that he did not act differently from what he was bidden (Numbers VIII:3) . . . “And the ark of the Eternal went before them (the camp of Israel) a three days’ journey to search out a resting place for them . . . And when the people complained; it was evil in the eyes of Eternal . . . And destroyed them that were in the extremity of the camp . . .” Rashi comments: “Those amongst them who were extreme in baseness.” But R. Simeon the son of Menassia said: “It means that the fire consumed the most distinguished and prominent ones among them.”

Studying between the lines of this parshah we find a vast amount of proof that the middle of the road is the best way of life for man to choose. The Menorah (candelabrum) which represents light or direction, is made in such a way that the lamps thereof “lit up over against the face of” it, that is, to the middle. Aaron, we are told, was praised because he did not act differently from what he was told. One may ask, why praise Aaron for doing what he was bidden, since any one would have done the same. The answer to this question we will find when we study the original text. The words “EL MUL PNEI HAMNORAH” puzzled Aaron. “MUL” means “against,” “PNEI” means “in front” or “facing up with it.” Aaron then understood the meaning that as a leader he would have to act accordingly. There would be times when he would have to take a stand against the people’s wish and at other times he would be in agreement with them. Our sages in “AVOT” tell us that Aaron was an “OHEV SHALOM” and a “RODEF SHALOM,” which may be translated to mean that sometimes he loved peace and sometimes he chased peace away. Aaron as a leader had to act as the situation demanded. For doing his job correctly, he was praised, as this was not an easy thing to accomplish. To fully carry out his task, a leader must be highly trained to understand his people. One misjudgment on his part may cause very bad results.

The middle of the road is pictured by our sages in Talmud YERUSHALMI thus. On one side of the road is fire and on the other side is ice. If one walks too close to the fire, he is consumed by the flames, and if he walks too close to the ice, he freezes to death. Therefore, he must walk in the exact center. The Torah and the Mitzvot help us in finding the median. “V’ETZ HACHAYIM BETOCH HAGAN” (Genesis II:9), “The tree of life was in the midst of the Garden of Eden,” pointing out that in life the middle of the road is the best. In the Ten Commandments we are also told “LO TAASEH L’CHA PESEL V’CHOL T’MUNAH ASHER BASHAMAYIM MIMAAL V’AL HAARETZ MITACHAT” (Exodus XX:4), “Thou shalt not make unto thee a graven image . . . or anything that is in heaven above or that is in the earth beneath.” Such extremes as what is in heaven or what is on the opposite side on earth, is not what we are recommended to live by. We must learn to combine heaven and earth and find the happy medium.

The Torah has chosen for a special duty the tribe of Levi, as it is written: “V’HIVDALTAH ET HALVIYIM MITOCH B’NAI ISRAEL” (Numbers VIII:14), “Thou shalt separate the Levites from among the children of Israel.” The tribe of Levi, one of the middle tribes, was chosen to teach the people, and not the first tribe of Reuben. Again, this emphasizes that the middle road is most important. The Torah, MISHKON, and BETH HAMIKDOSH had to be in the middle. The Torah says, “LO BASHAMAYIM HE,” “The Torah is not in heaven, but amongst us.” The MISHKAN was in the midst of the camp of Israel, the BETH HAMIKDOSH was in Jerusalem in the middle of the land of Israel.

In social questions, it may sometimes be necessary to go to the extreme, as we find in “AVOT”: “HAOMER SH’LI SH’LI V’SHELOCHA SHELOCH, ZEHU MIDAH BENONIT, V’YESH OMRIM ZEHU MIDAT SEDOM,” “Of one who says, ‘Mine is mine and yours is yours,’ this is considered a normal way of living. Others say, ‘That kind of living is like that in Sodom.’” Yes! Looking at the surface we may agree with the opinion of the first sages. But when we search deeper into the question, we may agree with the second opinion. If a poor man will say, “Mine is mine and yours is yours,” that may be good. But if a rich man will say it, that can be the path to social destruction. — The Priest, being the leader of the people, had to be extraordinarily careful in his decisions. Sometime he had to be “MUL,” against, and at other times “PNEI” for.

At the end of the parshah we are told that when Aaron and Miriam spoke evil about Moses, they were severely punished. — To find fault, even with a man such as Moses, is certainly possible. But if we look at man as a whole, we may find an overwhelming amount of virtues so that the mistakes of the same man are hardly noticeable. When Balaam the Evil went to curse the people of Israel, the Torah tells us: “VAYARE MISHAM ET K’TZE HAAM” (Numbers XXII:41), “He saw the edge of the people.” Had Balaam looked at the whole people, he could not have cursed them. “HEVEH DAN ET KOL HAADAM L’KAF ZECHUT” (Avot 1:6), “Judge the whole man (not a part of him) and you may find him to be very privileged.” This was a great sin, when Aaron and Miriam judged their brother Moses for specifics and not as the whole man.

When the Ark of the Covenant moved a three days’ journey from the camp, it caused complaints. When the so-called Torah Jews make the Sabbath for themselves only and run away from the people to their own extremes, this is dangerous. “VATOCHAL BIKTZEH HAMACHNEH, BEMUKTZIN SHEBAHEM LEGADLUT,” “The pushing fire consumed the most distinguished and prominent among them.”

In summary, we must strive to reach the middle of the road, therefore man should not be judged on cursory observations but on what we see in him in the entirety. “KOL HAOMER DAVID CHATAH ENO ELA TOEH” (Sages), “Whoever says that King David sinned, is making a mistake.” Do not look at only one facet of him, or the bare outlines of him. For there is no man that sinneth not. But, as a king, as a leader, he was perfect.
