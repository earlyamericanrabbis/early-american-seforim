In today’s sidrah, we read of one of the episodes in the life of Jacob our forefather, from which we may learn an important lesson.

Jacob, having left his father’s house for reasons well-known, tarries on the way. He lies down, with the sky for his roof. He dreams. And in his dream he sees placed upon the earth a ladder, the top of which reaches unto heaven, with the angels of the Lord ascending and descending upon it. He sees the Eternal Being in his vision, promising him His protection unending. Jacob awakes from his sleep, and exclaims, “Verily the Lord is in this place, but I knew it not.” And he was afraid and spoke, “How dreadful is this place! This is none other than the House of God and this is the Gate of Heaven.”

This was a vision directing him how to lead his own life and to teach his children, the House of Israel, the same. Jacob being an “ISH TAM YOSHEV OHOLIM,” a perfect man sitting in the tents of Torah, very little realized that just as man cannot live on bread alone, he cannot live with heaven alone. Both are necessary for our well-being. We are not angels who belong to heaven. We originally came from the earth and life must be rooted on the earth, but controlled by the teachings that we receive from heaven. We, above all other creatures of the earth, were endowed with the extra perception which enables us to set up such a ladder fastened to the earth, but on which we may climb higher and higher to the greatest realization of our dreams, thus perfecting our lives on this earth.

Jacob’s brother Esau based his life on the earth alone, “ISH SADEH,” a man of the field was he. As any other human being, he dreamed dreams. He imagined earthly pleasures. Without any bonds, with full freedom from restraint, he indulged himself. Soon we find him in a state of depression, tired of life. “HU OYEF,” nothing worth living for was left for him. He sold his very birthright for a pot of lentils that Jacob cooked. He noticed his brother Jacob joyous and happy so he thought that perhaps in the kind of food Jacob cooked lay the secret for his happiness. But Esau found that he was mistaken. He complained to Isaac, “VAYAKIVEINI ZE PAMAYIM, ES B’CHORATI LOKACH V’ATA LAKACH ES BIRCHATI,” “He held me up twice, he took away my birthright and now he took away my blessings.” — No, Esau! No one took anything away from you. You squandered and wasted life yourself. Nature was very good to you, but you abused it to the point where nature itself revolted against you. But, Esau, you are not alone in this world. There are many fools like you who waste away their lives in nothingness. Whatever joys and pleasures they have are of a fleeting nature. They too realized their error when it was too late.

On the other hand, when Isaac blessed Jacob, he said, “V’YITEN LECHA HOELOKIM,” “And God will give you, etc.” Rashi comments on the word “V’YITEN,” “And He will give.” Why the extra “VAV” which means “and”? To indicate that “YITEN V’YACHZOR V’YITEN,” “He will give you and continue to give you.” Yes, Jacob set up his life with strong roots on earth, realizing all the enjoyments and pleasures the earth had to offer, at the same time accepting obligations to duty or authority as prescribed by heavenly teachings. Therefore Jacob could not abuse nature, for God, the Creator of all, has co-ordinated everything to work in line with his teachings. We too, the children of Jacob, though rooted for almost two thousand years in strange lands under severe conditions, by building our ladder towards heaven, by living in accordance with our religion, preserved and secured our vitality as a people to the wonder and astonishment of the whole world. Judaism as we inherited it consists of two sides, the spiritual and the physical. The two faces of Judaism combined form one homogeneous whole, in which one facet dare not be sacrificed to the other, neither the spirit to the physical, nor the physical to the spirit.

This type of Judaism saved and preserved us throughout our GALUT exile, and now when we are, with the help of God, returning to our homeland, we must with renewed strength uphold, despite all hazards, the fundamental principles of our faith and the traditions of old which have given poetry to our lives and comfort to our souls. We dare not deal lightly with the sanctity of the Sabbath; this is our most precious gift, like a rare perfume, our dearest possession, that helped us and continues to help us as an anchor in the sea of the week’s unrest and toil, as a source of comfort and hope amid the difficulties and trials of life. The same is true of all the commandments of the Torah. The spirit which underlies every command is as fresh and bright now as it was thousands of years ago, and is applicable to every condition of life to this very day. Conditions change, circumstances alter; but we stand as much in need of the counsels and comforts, the teachings and warnings, the light and warmth of the Word of God as when it was revealed on Mount Sinai.

Our people were destined by Providence to bring to the human race the Word of God. The world wants as much light as the Word of God can offer it. The midrash says: “When a man builds a house, he will make the windows narrow from without but opening wide within. Why? In order that the rays of light, converging on the narrow opening, may diverge when entering the room and thus diffuse their light. But the windows in the wall of the Temple were constructed on the opposite plan. Beginning with a narrow opening within the building, they gradually became wider as they reached the outer surface, with this object, that the fullness of light which filled the sanctuary might shed its rays without and illumine the whole world.”

And thus it is with our sacred mission. Not only should we give light within our own land of Israel, but we must help illumine the entire world, for only through the Words of God and living by their direction can the world be saved from total destruction. “KI METZION TETZE TORAH U’DVAR HASHEM M’YIRUSHOLAYIM.” “Out of Zion must come Torah and the words of God out of Jerusalem.”
