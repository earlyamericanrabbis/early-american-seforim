“When Isaac became old his eyes became dim that he could not see,” (Gen. XXVII. 1).

The history of our people never conceals the truth, even when the truth does not meet with our hopes and wishes. In the case of Isaac, the Torah does not hesitate to say that his vision was dimmed, that his judgment was wrong in the choice between his two sons, Jacob and Esau, to one of whom had to be entrusted the traditional blessing of the family upon which rested the welfare of future generations. Our patriarch Isaac was mentally blind to the fact that Jacob was the son deserving of the blessing. He could not see or feel the roughness, deceit and insincerity of his son Esau.

The Torah says: “Do not take bribery, for bribery blinds the eyes of the wise and perverses the words of the righteous.” In a way, Isaac was unknowingly bribed by his dishonest son when Esau provided for his physical needs — “KI TZAID B’FIV.” Esau became his father’s favorite because he knew well how to pamper Isaac. Jacob might have provided for his father as well, and with greater sincerity and devotion, but he was now a “YOSHEV OHOLIM,” a scholar developing and preparing himself mentally and spiritually for the future. The difference in character between the two sons is vividly described in this parsha. “VAYHI ESOV ISH YODEA TZAID, V’YAKOV ISH TAM YOSHEV OHALIM.” Esau chose the life of a hunter, an existence for which creativity was not required; Jacob, on the other hand, showed at the outset that he would never be satisfied with knowledge or crafts that already existed, but would have to help develop and initiate further means of deriving greater joy and happiness for himself and those about him. “V’YODO OCHEZES B’AKEV ESOV,” “He held on to the heel of Esau.” In other words, the heel being the motivation for the forward movement in walking, Jacob’s birth holding on to Esau’s heel indicated that progress and innovation were the aim and goal of his life.

Isaac was blind to these facts. Esau’s savory food when he wanted and needed it most impressed him more than Jacob’s dreams for the future. And Isaac was not alone throughout the ages in his innocent erring, in his blindness. The world is still full of people who consider the successful businessman cleverer, worthier than the man who spends a lifetime broadening his wisdom and knowledge. Imagine a population full of Esaus, whose aim is to acquire all they can today, to whom the mundane things of life are of paramount interest. Such characters, like the Esau of old, would still sell their birthrights, their whole futures for a pot of lentils. The world could not long exist with only men such as these.

Rebekah saw Isaac’s mistaken judgment. As a mother it was her duty to interfere, but it was difficult for her to point out to Isaac the unworthiness of his favorite son. This would have broken the man’s heart. She had to be more tactful. She decided it would be best for Isaac to find out for himself. He, the perfect, righteous man, was unaware that one could be dishonest and deceitful and disguise one’s true character. Therefore Rebekah told Jacob to don the clothes of Esau and bring to Isaac the savory food he so enjoyed. In this way she hoped to make Isaac aware of the difference in character of the two sons. And so it was. Soon Isaac exclaimed: “HAKOL KOL YAKOV, V’HAYADAIM YDEI ESOV,” “The voice is Jacob’s and the hands are Esau’s.” Now Isaac realized that Jacob could do as well with his hands as Esau, but with what a difference! Esau’s voice and manner were rough and discourteous. He did look after his father’s welfare, but only as a matter of duty, and not with love and kindness. He looked forward to the time of his father’s death. “YIKREVU YEMEI EVEL AVI—”, “When my father will die—.” What was on his mind was on his lips. The midrash finds intimations in the parsha pertaining to the rough language Esau used when he brought food to his father. Certainly poor Isaac felt, as do many fathers and mothers of today who are dependent on their children — as if the earth opened up to swallow them alive when they heard unkind words or felt their children’s unfriendliness. And just a few years before these same parents had given up many enjoyments to help their children reach their desired status in life. Yes, Isaac was shocked “VAYECHERAD YITZCHOK CHARADA GEDOLA.” Isaac was frightened by his error in judgment, but he did recognize his mistake. The voice of Jacob was definitely different from the voice of Esau. Jacob too would provide for his father in his old age, but with love and kindness, with refinement that gave a good feeling to the parent when it was most needed.

This parsha is a great lesson for parents to follow. To overcome mental and emotional blindness and to have the right kind of judgment, parents must not wait to be shocked into the truth. They must know their children and let themselves also be known. The children must in their youth be taught the parents’ real values in life. The home must be an educational institution of the highest order, a place to mold the souls of coming manhood and womanhood, a place to cultivate character in the best ways known to us. A child reared along these lines, along the lines of “KOL YAKOV,” the voice of Jacob, and the teachings of the Torah, is the best assurance to the parents of being able in their old age to enjoy the fruits of their labor. Isaac finally entrusted the traditional blessings of the family, and with it the welfare of future generations, into the hands of Jacob, who had spent many years in the yeshivas of Shem-V’Ever, where the “KOL HATORAH,” the learning of Torah, cultivated his “KOL YAKOV,” the refinement of the voice of Jacob, and developed his character to the full expectation of both Isaac and Rebekah.

Only when the lesson of this parsha is followed by parents will the words of the Psalmist come true in their lives, “Your wife shall be as a fruitful vine, encircling your home. Your children shall be like olive plants round your table . . . Yea, you shall see your children’s children and you shall see peace over entire Israel.”
