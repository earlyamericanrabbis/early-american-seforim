“Ye are standing this day all of you before the Lord your God; your heads, your tribes, your elders and your officers, even all the men of Israel. Your little ones, your wives, and thy stranger that is in the midst of thy camp, from the hewer of thy wood unto the drawer of thy water. That thou shouldest enter into the covenant of the Lord thy God — and unto His oath — which the Lord thy God maketh with thee this day . . . Neither with you only do I make this covenant and this oath. But with him that standeth here with us this day before the Lord our God, and also with him that is not here with us this day” (Deuteronomy 29:9-14).

Rashi comments: “Moses assembled all Israel on the last day of his life to initiate them into the covenant with God.”

Twice do we have in the Torah the “TOCHACHAH,” “Threat of Punishment.” The first is in Leviticus 26:14-46, and the second in the preceding Sidrah (Deuteronomy 27:14-26 and 28:1-69). The number of curses for disobeying God’s law proclaimed in Leviticus are forty-nine and those in Deuteronomy are ninety-eight. Another noticeable difference between the two “TOCHACHOT,” “Threats of Punishment,” is that the one in Leviticus speaks in the plural, as it is said: “But if ye will not hearken unto me . . . And if ye shall reject My statutes . . .” But in Deuteronomy it is in singular form, as it is said: “But it shall come to pass, if thou wilt not hearken unto the Voice of the Lord thy God, to observe to do all His commandments and His statutes which I command thee this day . . .”

According to our sages of the Talmud, the Jewish community was not responsible for the sins of the individual (committed openly) until all had crossed the Jordan on their way to the promised land. Only from the time that they accepted the oath on the mountains GRIZIM and EBAL (see Deuteronomy 27:12-26 and 28:1-69) did they accept full responsibility for each other’s actions.

“ACHDUT ISRAEL,” “the togetherness of the people of Israel,” is the greatest factor contributing to Israel’s indestructibility. No other people can boast of true unitedness as Israel does. And its unique position in that respect stems from a deep-rooted belief in One God and in one Torah. Every Jew must recite daily the Thirteen Principles of Faith. To illustrate, here are a few:

(#2) “I believe with perfect faith that the Creator, blessed be His name, is a One, and that there is no Oneness in any manner like unto His, and that He Alone is our God, Who was, is and will be.”

(#9) “I believe with perfect faith that this Torah will not be changed, and that there will never be any other Torah from the Creator, blessed be His Name.”

(#11) “I believe with perfect faith that the Creator, blessed be His Name, rewards those that keep His commandments and punishes those that transgress them.”

(#12) “I believe with perfect faith in the coming of the Messiah, and though he tarry, I daily wait for his coming.”

(#13) “I believe with perfect faith that there will be a resurrection of the dead at the time when it shall please the Creator, blessed be His Name, and exalted be the remembrance of Him forever and ever.” (Morning service.)

In order to be able to perform all the commandments of the Torah, two things had to happen: the perfect unification of the people of Israel, and the possession of the promised land. Many of the commandments of the Torah refer only to certain people. For example, there is the commandment of the redemption of the first-born male. This is the obligation of a man whose wife’s first child is a son. Many of the laws in the Torah can only be performed in the promised land (see preceding Sidrah). Therefore, as soon as they crossed the Jordan, the Israelites were to accept the oath for the observance of the entire Torah. As it is said: “Ye are standing this day all of you before the Lord your God . . . That thou shouldest enter into the covenant of the Lord thy God and into His oath . . . That He may establish thee this day unto Himself for a people, and that He may be unto thee a God . . .” (Deuteronomy 29:9-14). The observance of the entire Torah can only be accomplished when the people of Israel are fully united and responsible one for the other, then rewards as well as punishments are credited to each man. The responsibility that one carries for the people about him entitles him to a share in their rewards. Thus our sages said: “God, the Torah, and Israel and the land of Israel are One.” This oneness was accomplished when they crossed the Jordan, then they unitedly passed over into the covenant of God by taking an accountability for each other and even for all future generations. As it is said: “Neither with you only do I make this covenant and this oath. But with him that standeth here with us this day before the Lord our God, and also with him that is not here with us this day” (Deuteronomy 29:13-14). Rashi comments that this refers to the future generations. The SFORNO adds: “Therefore you have the duty to inform your children that the possession of the land is contingent upon obedience to the covenant.” “ACHDUT,” solidarity of the people of Israel, is a “T’NAY,” condition, to complete the Oneness with God, Torah, Israel and the land of Israel.

The perfect faith of the people of Israel in the coming of the Messiah, the belief in the resurrection of the dead and the belief in reward and punishment are all interwoven with Israel’s acceptance of the covenant of God. The mission of Israel’s Messiah is to be the ingathering of all those who are scattered to the four corners of the world and their return to their homeland. Without Israel’s occupation of his land there can be no oneness. In order to carry responsibility for the future generations, the continuation of generations must be whole, with no break in the line. A dead man carries no responsibility. “MET PATUR MIN HAMITZVOT,” “The dead one is relieved of MITZVOT.” But with the belief in resurrection, the previous onus will be reinstated on each one resurrected. The belief in rewards and punishment is also a part of this oneness and solidarity. Without it, the Torah could not be accomplished in its entirety. By accepting the responsibility for each other for punishments as well as for rewards, we can say that the Torah is obeyed by everyone.

Rashi comments (Deuteronomy 29): “When the children of Israel heard the ninety-eight curses enumerated in the preceding Sidrah, in addition to the forty-nine in Leviticus, their faces turned yellow and they said: ‘MI YACHOL L’AMOD L’AMMOD BIFNEI ILU,’ ‘Who will be able to save himself from all of these?’ Then Moses answered them with the words ‘ATEM NITZAVIM HAYOM KULCHEM LIFNEI HASHEM ELOKECHEM,’ ‘Ye are standing this day all of you before the Lord your God.’” As long as you are together in full “ACHDUT,” you do not have to fear the “TOCHACHA,” the threat of punishment. Abraham asked forgiveness for the wicked city of Sodom should there be within it only ten righteous souls. When people are united, then the good ones will save the bad.

The first TOCHACHAH in Leviticus has only forty-nine curses and is spoken in a plural form. For then, the children of Israel were away from their land and there could not be complete unification, therefore they were considered as individuals. Accordingly, the number of admonitions was only forty-nine. But in Deuteronomy, when they had already crossed the Jordan and had accepted full responsibility for each other, the admonitions were doubled (for the single entity, and for the combined) and were spoken in the singular form to point out that all were now considered as one.
