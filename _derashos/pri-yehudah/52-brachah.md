“And this is the blessing, wherewith Moses, the man of God, blessed the children of Israel before his death. And he said: The Lord came from Sinai, and rose from Seir unto them; He shined forth from Mount Paran, and He came from the myriads holy, at His right hand was a fiery law unto them. Yes, He loveth the peoples, all His holy ones — they are in Thy hand; and they sit down at Thy feet, receiving of Thy words. Moses commanded us a law, an inheritance of the congregation of Jacob.”

The form and contents of this blessing has some similarities to the blessing of Jacob (Genesis 49). Both Parshiyot relate to the blessings Jacob and Moses respectively uttered before they died. But the Parshah that deals with the blessing of Jacob to his children and Jacob’s death is named “VAYCHI,” “He Lived,” whereas this Parshah narrating Moses’ blessing and his death is called “B’RACHAH,” “Blessing.” Also, Jacob blesses his children but Moses blesses a developed nation. Moses, as a leader of the people of Israel, shows also concern for other peoples. He said: “AF CHOVEV AMIM,” “Yea, He loveth the peoples.” Rabbi Sh. R. H. explains the word “CHOVEV,” “Loveth,” is from the present tense of the word “CHOV,” “to be bound to do something, to be owing, to lead one to duty.” It means that God wants the other nations also to be led to duty. Therefore “Thou takest the holy ones (of Israel) in Thy hand.” All the holy ones of Israel are the tools, the instruments for the purpose of teaching the Torah not to Israel alone but to all mankind. At the time of the Revelation of the Torah at Sinai, the people of the world were not mature enough to accept the Torah, nevertheless the Revelation took place in the interest of all the human race. With Israel and through Israel, the way is prepared for the future, when all humanity will recognize their duties to God in accordance with the teachings of the Torah.

Moses was not permitted to enter the Promised Land but as the SIFRI states on the sentence: “And Moses went up from the plains of Moab unto Mount Nebo . . . and the Lord showed him all the land” (Deuteronomy 34), “Moses was shown not only the physical land but he was also given a prophetic vision of the main episodes in the future history of Israel up till the end of the days.” Moses saw in his vision a wayward and disloyal Israel followed by great suffering as God’s punishment, but he also saw the ultimate destiny to be bright and happy.

In this Parshah, as well as in several other places (Josh. XIV.6, Psalm XC), Moses has been given the title “The man of God.” A man of such great Divine inspiration could easily be regarded and accepted as a deity. In Exodus 32 we read: “And when the people saw that Moses delayed to come down from the Mount, the people gathered themselves together unto Aaron, and said unto him: ‘Up, make us a god who shall go before us; for as for this Moses the man that brought us out of the land of Egypt, we know not what is become of him.’” Rashi states: “Satan caused the Israelites to see passing across the skies a bier on which lay a figure resembling Moses.” The man of God (Moses) was mistakenly considered by them to be a Divine Being and they were greatly disappointed when they found out that he was just as mortal as they were. Therefore they asked of Aaron: “Make us a god who shall go before us.” The Hebrew word for “God” in the text is “ELOHIM,” followed by the verb “YELCHU,” “shall go,” in the plural form and that means the Israelites wished to have many gods to go before them and, as Moses had done, show them the way they were to journey.

The Torah stresses the fact: “VAYAMAT SHAM MOSHEH,” “So Moses died there” (Deuteronomy 34:5), to point out that Moses was human in regard to death even as he was to birth. The founders of other religions are considered to be of supernatural birth, but such is not the case in Judaism. Moses is clearly described to be human at birth when the Torah tells us: “And there went a man of the house of Levi and took to wife a daughter of Levi. And the woman conceived, and bore a son; and when she saw him that he was a goodly child, she hid him three months” (Exodus 2:1-2). The Torah also tells us: “And he (Moses) was buried in the valley of the land of Moab . . . and no man knoweth of his sepulchre unto this day.” The Rabbis state the reason he lies in an unknown place is “so that it might not become a place of pilgrimage for those who make god of their heroes.”

Yes, Moses, the man of God who delivered his people out of Egypt, the man whom the Lord knew FACE to face (Deuteronomy 34:10), the man who did all the signs and wonders to Pharaoh and his servants, the man who delivered the Torah to the children of Israel, could easily be mistaken for a deity. Therefore is this Parshah not called “VAYCHI” as in the case of Jacob, in order to remove any doubt as to the mortality of this great man of God.

There is a noticeable difference in the words where the Torah describes the death of Moses and the description of the death of Jacob. In this Parshah we read: “And the children of Israel wept for Moses in the plains of Moab thirty days; ‘VAYITMU Y’MAI B’CHI EVEL MOSHEH,’ so the days of weeping in the mourning for Moses were ended” (Deuteronomy 34:8). The word for “ended” is “VAYITMU.” But pertaining to Jacob, we read: “VAYAVRU Y’MAI B’SHITO,” “And when the days of weeping for him were past . . .” (Genesis 50). The word for “past” is “VAYAVRU,” which means passed from one trouble to another. For as soon as Jacob died the suffering of Bondage began (Rashi Gen. 47:28). But when Moses died, the children of Israel had completely ended their travelling in the desert and were about to enter their Promised Land, therefore the word “VAYITMU,” “ended,” is used.

On Simchat Torah, when we celebrate the concluding and again the beginning of the Torah, we read this Parshah that tells of the death of Moses. One may ask, “Would it not be more appropriate on such occasion, when we celebrate the Law of Moses, to read the Parshah of Moses’ birth?” This is not so. We read the Parshah of Moses’ death to point out that to the Torah Moses is not indispensable. The Torah is not dependent on any single man, even one as great as Moses was. “Moses received the Torah on Sinai and handed it down to Joshua; Joshua to the elders to the Prophets; and the Prophets handed it down to the men of the Great Assembly” (AVOT 1:1). “BINU SH’NOT DOR VADOR,” “Consider the years of many generations” (Deuteronomy 32:7). Every generation has its great men to take over the Torah.

The Torah ends with the letter “Lamed” and begins with the letter “Bet,” and together they make up the word “LEV,” “heart.” The Torah is to perfect the heart of man at all times. Just as the heart pumps blood through the body and thereby keeps it alive, so does the Torah and its teachings function for us. “For they are our life and the length of our days, and we will meditate on them day and night.”

When Moses was born, the Torah tells us that his mother saw that he “was a goodly child . . .” Rashi explains this by saying that when he was born the house was filled with light, pointing to his future greatness (Exodus 2:2). Yes! He was the one who brought the spiritual light of the Torah to the children of Israel and thousands of years after his death is still guiding mankind.

This Parshah is correctly called “B’RACHA,” “Blessing,” for in addition to its being a narrative of the blessing Moses gave to the children of Israel, Moses himself remains as an inspiration for blessing to the end of the days.
