“And the Lord spoke unto Moses and unto Aaron saying: This is the statute of the law which the Lord hath commanded, saying: Speak unto the children of Israel, that they bring thee a red heifer without spot, wherein is no blemish, and upon which never came yoke. And ye shall give her unto Eleazer the priest, that he may bring her forth without the camp, and one shall slaughter her . . . and one shall burn the heifer . . . And the priest shall take cedar wood, and hyssop, and crimson, and cast it into the midst of the burning of the cow . . . And it shall be as a charge for the congregation . . . for a water of separation, it is a purification for sin . . . And it shall be an ordinance forever unto them, he that sprinkleth the water of separation shall be unclean until the evening” (Numbers XIX:1-22). Rashi comments: “Because Satan and the nations of the world taunt Israel, saying, ‘What is this command and what reason is there for it?’ on this account it (Scripture) uses the term “CHUKAH,” “Statute” about it, implying: ‘It is an enactment from before Me; you have no right to criticize it.’”

The lesson that we derive from this parshah is that the acceptance and following of authority is a must on the road to the building and developing of a nation. A society can never exist without a leadership whose authority people will obey. A commander must not always explain why a certain order had to be given. And many times the performance of the order can not wait till all concerned will perceive the reason for it. In defending a country from an enemy, the people must have confidence in their generals and obey unconditionally — “AMAR LO HAKADOSH BARUCH HU L’MOSHE LECHA ANI MEGALEH TAAM PARAH, AVAL L’ACHER CHUKAH” (Midrash Rabah), “Said God, blessed be He, to Moses: To you I will tell the reason of the statute of the red heifer but to others not.” In order to create trust and confidence in God and in Moses, His servant, the people of Israel had to be trained to follow certain orders that had no logical explanations. Things of reason can be disputed and thereby weaken the trust in the good judgment of the leadership. Yes! Moses as the leader of the people was permitted to know the reason for the statutes but others were not. Even King Solomon, the wisest of all men, said: “I said I will get wisdom; but it was far from me,” (Ecclesiastes 7:23). — When he passed beyond these concrete precepts and wandered into the realm of speculation to comprehend the statute of the “PARAH ADUMAH,” he discovered that he was seeking to reach out to the unattainable.

Further in the parshah we read: “Then came the children of Israel, even the whole congregation, into the desert of Sin . . . and Miriam died there . . . and there was no water for the congregation . . . And the Eternal spake unto Moses . . . Take the staff, and assemble thou the assembly together . . . and speak ye unto the rock BEFORE THEIR EYES; and it shall give forth its water . . . And Moses took the staff . . . as He commanded him. And Moses and Aaron assembled the congregation before the rock; and he said unto them, Hear, ye REBELS; must we bring you forth water out of the rock? And Moses lifted up his hand, and with his staff he smote the rock twice; and the water came out abundantly; and the congregation drank and their beasts also. And the Eternal spoke unto Moses and Aaron, because ye believed me not, to sanctify me in the eyes of the children of Israel, therefore ye shall not bring this assembly into the land which I have given them” (Numbers XX:7-13). Rashi comments on the words “Ye Rebels”: the text is using for that the word “MORIM,” “teachers,” such as would teach their teachers. — Here too is a case where Moses and Aaron were severely punished for not following authority as ordered. Moses showed indignation towards the people of Israel and said to them, “Ye are trying to teach me instead of obeying my orders,” but he himself failed to perform the order of God as instructed. Yes! It may be true that Moses had enough reason to misunderstand the order. Firstly, when God said: “Speak ye unto the rock BEFORE THEIR EYES,” this could be interpreted as “Strike the rock so that they may see the act of the striking.” For when instructing on speaking to the rock, it would have been more fitting to say: “— and speak in front of their ears.” Secondly, when God told Moses “Take the staff,” he had good reason to believe that this would be a repetition of a similar case when he was told to hit the rock (Exodus XVII:1-7). Nevertheless, Moses was punished for deviating from the exact instructions of the order. Moses should have known that in order to create full trust in God and confidence in himself as God’s servant, he (Moses) had to set an example of obedience.

Further in the parshah we are told: “And the people spoke against God and against Moses: Wherefore have ye brought us up out of Egypt to die in the wilderness? For there is no bread, and there is no water and our soul loatheth this light bread. And the Lord sent fiery serpents among the people, and they bit the people, and many people of Israel died. Moses prayed for the people. And the Lord said unto Moses: Make thee a fiery serpent, and set him upon a pole, and it shall come to pass, that everyone that is bitten, when he seeth it, shall live” (Numbers XXI:4-10). Here too is a lesson of trust and confidence in God. The mechanical fiery serpent as a cure for those BITTEN by serpents was accepted by Moses as God told him, without a question. Moses had already learned a lesson of perfect obedience to authority.

“AL KEN YOMRU HAMOSHLIM BOU CHESHBON, BOU V’NACHSHOV CHESHBONO SHEL OLAM, ILU HAMOSHLIM BEYITZROM” (Talmud BB. 78b), “They that speak in parables say: Come to Chesbon.” — The word “Chesbon” also means “reckoning,” and the Rabbis of the Talmud call upon every man, “Let us reckon with the world’s problems.” That is, each one for himself is permitted and even urged to learn and understand things logically. But when it comes to “CHESBONO SHEL OLAM,” when it comes to the order of the world, national order, or the order of a given society, one must reckon with it. He, as an individual, must learn to accept and obey orders without any conditions. Because without this, society can not exist. “ILU HAMOSHLIM BEYITZRAM,” “Those who can rule over their desire.” The nature of man is not to do the things the purpose and reason for which he does not fully understand. However, it is important, for the sake of the development and progress of a people, to control one’s inclinations and follow the orders given by the authorities.
