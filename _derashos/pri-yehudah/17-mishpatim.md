“Now these are the judgments which thou shalt set before them: if thou obtain an Hebrew servant, six years he shall serve: and in the seventh year he shall go out free for nothing, etc.” (Exodus XXI:1). Rashi comments: “Wherever ‘ELEH,’ ‘these are,’ is used it cuts off the preceding section from that which it introduces. Where, however, ‘V’ELEH,’ ‘and these,’ is used it adds something to the former subject (i.e., forms a continuation of it). So also here: ‘And these are the judgments’ (i.e., these, also).” What is the case with the former commandments (The Ten Commandments)? They were given at Sinai. So these, too, were given at Sinai. Why is the section dealing with the “civil laws” placed immediately after that commanding the making of the altar? To tell you that you should seat (i.e., provide quarters for) the Sanhedrin in the vicinity of the Temple: “ASHER TASIM LIFNEHEM,” “which thou shalt put before them” — God said to Moses: It should not enter your mind to say, “I shall teach them a section of the Torah or a single Halacha twice or three times until it becomes current in their mouths exactly according to its wording (i.e., until they know the text verbatim), but I shall not take the trouble to make them understand the reason of each thing and its significance.” Therefore scripture says, “which thou shalt set before them,” “like a table fully laid before a person with everything ready for eating” (Rashi).

Two vital suggestions in Jewish jurisprudence are pointed out by Rashi. Firstly, that the civil law, the rules and regulations for society, can not be based on man-made law alone. It must have strong ties with the moral law given by God in His Torah. All the long established countries of the world have their constitutions, laws and regulations made for the benefit of the society which resides within their borders, but have had very little success in rooting out the evil in their midst. Their electric chairs may work day and night, their prison cells may be full, but all of these did not and will not bring the evil-doers to their senses. In order to change their way of life, something other than punishment must be used. Penal institutions of the best kind have never proved to be of any value in providing a cure for bad behavior in society. On the contrary, putting a first-time malefactor into prison, into an environment of social misfits, will result not in his being rehabilitated for life in society, but will make a confirmed criminal out of him. To take away man’s freedom, even for a short time, means to remove from him important human values which he needs for his own development. Once liberty has been lost, it becomes exceedingly difficult to reestablish social equilibrium and initiative action in rehabilitation.

Our parshah begins with the civil law pertaining to slavery. The Torah does not condone thereby the institution of slavery, but the contrary. “For unto Me the children of Israel are servants, servants to Me, but not servants of servants” (of human beings). (Kidd. 22b). — One of the ways that an Hebrew servant can be bought is from the court which sold him for a theft which he had committed, as it is said (XXII.2), “If he (the thief) has nothing, then shall he be sold for his theft.” He can not be sold for more than six years even when the theft is worth more than six years of his service. And if the theft is not worth the full six years of service, then he is not even sold. If the servant does not want to be liberated after six years, then his lord shall bore his ear through with an awl and he shall serve him for ever (until the Jubilee). The boring of the servant’s ear is a sign that he is unworthy, for he did not want his freedom. — The Torah does not place the thief into prison where he completely loses his contact with society, but rather into the care of a good family, where not only will he pay restitution for his theft, but he will also learn how to behave himself in the future. Thus by placing an evildoer in a home where the social and moral conscience is high, the proper influence will be exerted towards restoring him to a normal life.

Further, according to Torah law, one can not be judged by circumstantial evidence (especially in murder cases). There must be two (kosher) witnesses who warned him not to commit the murder and clearly saw the act of murder committed. According to the Talmud, a court which even once in seventy years sentences to death a man for any crime that calls for the death penalty, is branded “BET DIN KATLANIT,” “a murderous court.” But even with this lenient attitude towards those deserving of punishment, our people’s crime rate always was, and still is, the lowest of any other peoples in the world. We never believed that punishment alone would root out evil. We educate our children to the true values of life; we raise not only their material standards, but also their spiritual standards; we teach the Torah way of life. Then, and only then, can we root out the evil from our midst.

The Ten Commandments are placed in two sections. In one section are the commandments that deal between man and God, and in the other are the commandments between man and his friends. One depends largely on the other. A man who believes in God and practices His teachings will be much better regulated in his social life than the one who does not. Very few people have succeeded in raising children devoted to their parents, to their country and to their fellowmen without teaching them any spiritual values, but giving them only the mundane things of life. Naturally, there are those who do not follow the precepts of our Torah, but who have built up a set of values of their own. And if we look carefully into those ideals that they follow, we will find in them a great similarity to the laws of our Torah. Many of the Torah laws were in practice even before the Torah was delivered. Abraham, according to our sages, observed them, as we read: “EKEV ASHER SHAMA AVRAHAM B’KOLI VAYISHMOR MISHMARTI, MITZVOTAY CHUKOTAY V’TOROTAY,” “because that Abraham hearkened to My voice, and kept My charge, My commandments, My statutes, and My laws.” Many of the moral teachings of the Torah can be found even in nature itself, as the sages say: “HISTAKEL B’ORYITA U’BARA ALMA,” “God looked in the Torah, and according to the Torah plan, He created the world.” That is, God has built in nature the moralities of the Torah. Without the Torah at his disposition, Abraham learned many moral observances from the world of creatures (see Proverbs, Chapter XXX:24-31).

The Torah is called “TORAH OR,” “Light.” Just as the light that God created in the first day functioned together with the darkness by day and by night (Rashi), till the fourth day when God set their functions separately, so it is that even in nature we may find the laws of the Torah, but they are not organized in a way that can be seen by everyone. It takes great men such as Abraham to find them. Therefore the written Torah had to be delivered.

The other important idea that Rashi points out from the text is: “Why is this section dealing with the civil laws placed immediately after that commanding the making of the altar? To tell you that you should seat (i.e., provide quarters for) the Sanhedrin in the vicinity of the Temple.”

The altar at the Temple was a place designated to bring up offerings, a place of sacrifice and dedication. A man who sits on the bench of judgment must be dedicated, fear no man, and be willing and ready to sacrifice his life and sometimes the lives of his very near and dear. The honest judge does not wrest judgment out of respect or fear of persons. Many times judges will receive letters of warning or threats from those being judged or their families. But the man who pursues justice will not yield under pressure, but will mete it out impartially to all who come before him.

Rashi further points out as previously stated, that since this parshah deals with the laws pertaining to the actions of man to his friends, then it is very necessary to have them fully explained. One may accept a statute without a clear understanding of it, even at great cost, as long as another does not benefit by one’s loss. But where judgment is involved between man and man, he who loses will want to know why judgment is against him, and this must be made thoroughly clear.

Yes! Both tablets of The Ten Commandments, the whole Torah, even the social order, are all from Sinai, but “V’ELEH HAMISHPATIM ASHER TASIM LIFNEHEM,” “These are the judgments which thou shalt set before them.” Like a table fully laid before a person with everything ready for eating (Rashi). — This parshah teaches us how to safeguard man’s freedom. Just as we are told not to cause servitude to his body, we are also told not to subjugate his mind by making him accept judgments without good reason.
