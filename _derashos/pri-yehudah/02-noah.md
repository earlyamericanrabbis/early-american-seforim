“These are the products of Noah. Noah, a righteous man, was perfect in his generation: with God did Noah walk.”

As the Torah points out that Noah was perfect in his generation, our sages understood it in two ways. One: that in his time, he was a righteous man, but if he had lived in the time of Abraham he would not be considered righteous. And the other: even in his time, he remained a righteous man. On the one hand, the degree of morality and honesty was much higher during the time of Abraham. But on the other hand, morality and honesty attained and kept in such times as during the generations of Noah, weighs incomparably heavier in God’s scales than a greater amount attained and kept in a better time.

There is no question that Noah deserves the title the Torah has given to him, namely: TZADIK TAMIM, Righteous and Perfect. As some of our rabbis pointed out, in his time and generations he was perfect. After all, he did save humanity from total destruction with the Ark that took him one hundred and twenty years to build. But, during his lifetime the world was destroyed by a flood and only Noah and his family were saved. And when he left the Ark, as he was observing the great destruction about him, we would have expected Noah to show some signs of being disturbed, mourning and bewailing the terrible plight of the lost people. He could at least have stayed sober and begun to plan the rebuilding of a world just destroyed, or attempted to create an atmosphere of better moral values than those he knew had brought on the flood and destruction. Instead, we find Noah planting a vineyard and drinking from its wine to the point of intoxication and doing undignified things that certainly reflect against his righteousness.

How would Abraham have dealt under similar circumstances? We find Abraham praying for the wicked cities of Sodom and Gomorrah. He prays even for the wicked King Abimelech. But Noah, instead of urgently entreating God for the entire world, is happy that he and his family were saved. The Torah, at the end of the chronicling of the Flood, no longer calls him TZADIK TAMIN, Righteous and Perfect. VAYISHAER ACH NOACH, he remains only with his name Noah. Although he builds an altar and brings offerings on it, God does not give heed. VAYARACH HASHEM ET REIACH HANICHOACH, HERIACH REICHO SHEL AVRAHAM AVINU OLEH M’KIVSHAN HAESH. God did not notice Noah’s expression of compliance and offerings, but Abraham’s compliance, for which the man was put into a fiery furnace, was noted. This signifies that even Noah’s offerings were not particularly desirous to God.

The kind of righteousness Noah practiced could not be admired by anyone, therefore his preaching had little effect upon his listeners. The belief in and love for God must coincide with the belief in and love for men. At the time of the MABUL, flood, humanity was split asunder. “VATISHACHET HAARETZ LIFNEI HAELOKIM,” “Before they sinned against heaven they corrupted each other.” The DOR HAFLAGAH, the generation of the Tower of Babel, went to the other extreme. They tried to unite humanity into such a great force as to rule even the heavens. Their aim was to usurp God’s authority and knowledge, to make themselves the ultimate judges of right and wrong, good and evil, to make man the sole judge of all human values. When man no longer regards himself subject to any authority higher than his own, he will eventually become selfish and resort to any means to attain his goals. He will even distort the truth and destroy justice and peace.

The builders of the Tower of Babel put too much emphasis on the importance of centralization thereby making the individual subordinate to the wish of the majority.[^1] Clemenceau once said that the voice of the majority can be the voice of the devil. The truth of this was borne out by Nazi Germany. Judaism values the importance of attachment to the community — but does not believe that everything decreed by the majority is best simply because it so willed it. Judaism believes that each man is responsible to God for himself. If necessary one must stand alone with God, when the principle worshipped by the majority is not the true Godly one. One, with God, and the Right, are the true majority.

The DOR HAMABUL was punished because each man lived only for himself, even at the expense of others. Such a system and such a world could not possibly exist. It brought total destruction upon itself. The DOR HAFLAGAH tried to build the Tower of Babel to unite together all forces in a bond of inseparable magnitude, to thus exist and flourish without the authority of God. They were punished with dispersal over all the earth, and thereby it was proved to them that when men have to face the various forces of nature in the divergent places in which they settle, the help of God is required.

And Noah, with his personal righteousness, did not guide either of the lost and fallen peoples to the SHVIL HAZAHAV, the golden path, or the middle of the road, for Noah himself stood at the opposite extreme. “With God did Noah walk,” and left his generations to their destruction.

Abraham was the first to realize that the two extremes are wrong. A divided humanity as in the DOR HAMABUL, Age of the Flood, could not exist. But neither could a strong united society without God. Love for God and love for humanity must go hand in hand. “ET HAELOKIM HITALLECH NOACH,” with God did Noah walk. His righteousness had its roots in that he allowed himself to be led by God’s hand. Whereas of the great men in later times it says that they “went through life before God,” as his messengers. Of Noah it says “with God,” that he paid no attention to the troubles of the people about him, but held fast to God. Noah should have learned a lesson from Chanoch about whom the Torah says: “And Chanoch walked with God, and he was no longer there for God took him” (Genesis 5:24). Chanoch, by choosing to walk with God avoided the people, either out of fear or disdain or contempt. But HITBODDUT, monk-like retirement from the world of men, is un-Jewish. Our TZADIKIM, Righteous Ones, lived among the people, with the people, and for the people. God was not, as in the case of Chanoch, the sum total of all their lives. “V’ENENU,” “He was not there.” To the world such people do not even exist. But the real TZADIKIM kept the course of all their lives with God and with humanity. Such was Abraham’s way of life.

The time may come as in the prophecy of Zephania (III, 9), “For then will I restore to the nations a purified language to call them all in the Name of God to serve Him with one shoulder.” Unification of all the forces of humanity for the betterment of all the people of the world under God’s authority is the call of the Torah.
