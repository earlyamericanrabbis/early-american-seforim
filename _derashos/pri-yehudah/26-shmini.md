“And it came to pass, on the eighth day . . . and he (Moses) said to Aaron, take thee a young calf for a sin offering . . . And Moses said unto Aaron, approach unto the altar, and offer the sin offering.” Rashi comments: “This took place during the installation of the priests into their sacred office . . . The animal for the sin offering was selected to announce to him (Aaron) that God has granted him atonement by means of this calf for the incident of the golden calf which he has made . . . Because Aaron was diffident and feared to go up to the altar, Moses therefore said to him, ‘Wherefore art thou diffident? For this purpose hast thou been selected’ ” (Lev. IX:1-7).

The first part of today’s parshah deals with the installation of Aaron and his children as priests. Then we are told of the great tragedy that befell Aaron in the midst of his extreme rejoicing at the time of the installation. Two of his sons, Nadab and Abihu died, as we read: “And Nadab and Abihu, the sons of Aaron, took each of them his censer, and put fire therein, and put incense thereon, and offered strange fire before the Eternal, which He commanded them not. And there went out a fire from the Eternal, and devoured them; and they died before the Eternal.” Rabbi Eliezer said they died only because they gave decisions on religious matters in the presence of their teacher, Moses. Rabbi Ishmael said they died because they entered the sanctuary intoxicated by wine (See Rashi). The Midrash gives the following reason for their death: “Nadab and Abihu were walking behind Moses and Aaron and all the people of Israel were behind them, and Nadab said then to Abihu: ‘When will these two old men die and you and I will become the leaders of the people . . .’” And in Lev. XVI:1, we read: “And the Eternal spoke unto Moses after the death of the two sons of Aaron, when they approached unto the Eternal and died. And the Eternal said unto Moses, speak unto Aaron . . . that he come not at all times into the holy place . . . that he die not.” We may denote from this that their sin was the approaching much too close to the Eternal. — The cause for the punishment to the two sons of Aaron was a puzzle to the sages and their opinions seem to be too much apart. I shall try to find a common ground where all these opinions will meet.

At the end of today’s parshah the Torah commands not to eat from certain animals and fowls and fishes. The text begins: “Speak unto the children of Israel, saying, these are the animals which ye may eat among all the beasts that are on earth . . .” Rashi comments: “The word ‘CHAYA,’ ‘animal,’ is used here instead of ‘B’HAMAH,’ ‘cattle,’ to express the following idea: because Israel cleave to the omnipresent and therefore deserves to remain in life. ‘ZOS HACHAYA’ is thus taken to mean ‘This, O living nation (or nation that lives), is what ye may eat. He (God) therefore separated them from what is unclean and imposed commandments upon them, whilst to the other nations who do not cleave to Him He prohibited nought.’ ”

What is the connection between “KORBONOT,” offerings, that we find in the beginning of the parshah and the law pertaining to which animals we may eat and which not? In the Talmud (B’RACHOT 55a) it states, “Rabbi Johanan and Rabbi Eleazar both explain that as long as the temple stood, the altar atoned for Israel, but now a man’s table atones for him.”

We note in the Torah where one “KORBON” was accepted by God and the other not. “And there went out a fire from the Eternal and consumed upon the altar the burnt offering . . . which when all the people saw, they uttered cries of exultation.” And in the next sentence, “Nadab and Abihu brought their offerings,” the Torah tells us, “and there went out a fire from the Eternal and devoured them” (Lev. X:1-2). In Genesis IV:4.5, we read: “And the Eternal had regard unto Abel and to his oblation, but unto Cain and his oblation He had not regard.”

The lesson that we learn from today’s parshah is that we must stay away from both extremes, right or left. During the celebration of the installation into the priesthood of Aaron and his children, Moses had to remind Aaron not to be diffident and to perform the service entrusted unto him. Aaron felt ashamed and unworthy for such holy ministration because he made the golden calf. But Moses convinced him that he was chosen for this because his feelings of remorse and shamefulness for the sin he committed was exactly what his ministrations on “KORBONOT” had to produce in the hearts and in the minds of the people who offered it. When Joseph made himself known unto his brethren the Torah tells us: “and his brethren could not answer him; for they were amazed at his presence.” Rashi (Genesis XLV:3) comments that it was out of shame that they could not answer. In Exodus XX:17, we read: “And Moses said unto the people, fear not: for God is come in order to try you, and that His fear may be before your faces, that ye sin not.” The MECHILTA comments: “The word ‘fear’ refers to ‘shame.’ ”

Yes! The best form of repentance is when one shows signs of shame for the wrong he has done. Only then will he try to mend his ways. When a man reaches the point where he is not only ashamed to have men see him doing wrong, but when even in hiding he feels the presence of God who sees what he is doing and knows what he is thinking, then has he attained the highest form of faith. And when man errs and is ashamed of it, then has he attained the highest form of “TESHUVAH,” repentance. — This is exactly what a “KORBON” is expected to accomplish in a man’s life. If the “KORBON” makes the man who offers it recognize his guilt and the ugliness connected with his sin, then it is desirous to the Lord. But when the offering is just another one of the ceremonials to help relieve man of his heavy feeling of guilt, then it has the opposite effect. Then he is not changed by the “KORBON.” On the contrary, he will then feel that he may do what he has done again, and that he can remedy the deed by a “KORBON.” Abel’s offering was accepted because “HEVY GAM HU,” he brought himself; the “KORBON” influenced him to be a better man. But Cain’s offering was not accepted for his was just a ceremonial without any significance to it.

Nadab and Abihu, great as they were, kept themselves too far from the people to be able to understand their frailties. “HEN BIKROVAI EKODESH VAAL PNEI CHOL HAAM EKAVED,” “I will be sanctified in them that approach unto Me, and before all the people I will be glorified.” The Hebrew word for “glorified” used here is “EKAVED,” which may also be translated “I will be heavy” from the word “KAVED,” “heavy.” The meaning is that such a closeness to the Eternal as Nadab and Abihu lived, “SHLO NOSEU LAEM NASHIM,” “they did not marry women,” is not recommendable. Some people may become nazarites, but most will not. The high priest is not permitted to his service in the Holy of Holies if he has not a wife: a leadership that runs to the extreme as far as religion is concerned may become very holy indeed, but it will be difficult to bear. They are then separated from the people as far as heaven from earth. “And Chanoch walked with God; and he was not, for God took him” (Genesis V:24). So it was with Nadab and Abihu. When they came too close to God, they were useless as leaders of their people.

The coming too close to the Holiness produces sometimes contrary effects. One may lose the deep reverence for a holy place if he is there frequently and too much at ease. Then the place no longer gives him the inspiration he ought to receive from it. Again, undue familiarity with the site may result in a false superiority, which is detrimental to good leadership. The sages who said the sin of Nadab and Abihu was that they came into the temple intoxicated by wine, or the others who said they made decisions on religious matters in the presence of their teacher Moses, or even those who said that they discussed how they might take over the leadership, all understood the same truth, which stemmed from a lack of reverence for the holy place and what it represented. Arrogance because of closeness to the sanctuary removed Nadab and Abihu from contact with the people and lessened their reverence for the sanctuary and that which it symbolized. Therefore they did not find favor in the eyes of God, and instead of their offerings being accepted, God removed them.

In the beginning of the parshah the Torah recognizes human frailties and shows a way back from sin to normality by man’s own ability to acknowledge the mistake he makes. By the KORBON that he offers for his sin, he proves that he is ashamed of it. At the end of the parshah the Torah gives us a form of preventive medicine in order to keep ourselves morally and spiritually healthy. Our sages tell us, in three things can a man’s character be noted: “B’KOSO, B’KISO, B’KAASO,” “in the way one drinks or eats, in the way he gives charity, and in the way he displays his wrath.” Food is one of the elements that greatly influences the human character and behaviour. The Torah points out that certain animals, fowls and fishes, when eaten, may cause moral defilement. Food is energy-producing matter. And the Torah knew that we must be careful of the type of energy we produced in our bodies. Hence they forbade us certain food which would yield strange energies, like the strange fires introduced to the altar by Nadab and Abihu.

“As long as the temple stood, the altar atoned for Israel, but now a man’s table atones for him” (Sages). Today the table has a double duty. Firstly, from it we are not to eat of the things forbidden by the Torah. Secondly, it is recognized as the altar for “KORBONOT.” — Eating in general brings us closer and almost to the same level as the animal. Therefore, especially at the table, we must recognize that there is something in us above the bestial plane and we must strive to bring this to the fore.
