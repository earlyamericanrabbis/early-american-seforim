The traditional Passover ceremonies make us profoundly conscious of the great miracles connected with the exodus from Egypt. The Seder itself, as well as the other ceremonies which we observe on the Passover holiday, all serve this purpose.

Nor is it only at Passover time that we are reminded of the exodus from Egypt. Jewish practice and Jewish ritual call the event to our attention every day of the year. The Kiddush which we recite on every Sabbath and every festival contains the phrase “ZECHER L’YTZIAS MITZRAYIM,” a memorial of the exodus from Egypt. Each day when we put on our tefillin we recite a Biblical passage which associates the tefillin with the exodus from Egypt. The Ten Commandments open with the words, “I am the Lord thy God Who took thee out of the land of Egypt.” This is the way in which God identifies Himself. Wherever we turn then in Jewish practice and Jewish tradition, especially on Passover, the Jew is enjoined to dwell on the miracles which were connected with his going forth from Egypt, to reflect on the manner in which God brought him out of the land of bondage, out of centuries of slavery to a noble life of freedom.

How shall we explain this constant and unusual emphasis? A careful analysis will reveal the answer. The events of the exodus from Egypt as they are recorded in the Torah and explained by the rabbis constitute the rare instance in Jewish history in which God’s power and His majesty were exhibited to men in completeness, without restraint and without obscurity. What less than the majesty and power of God could explain the plagues which befell the Egyptians? What less than the strength of God could explain the opening of the sea so that the Jews might pass through in safety? What less than the power and the glory of God could have brought about the salvation of Israel in those dark and difficult days? No one who witnessed the miracles could doubt their Divine Origin. Only God’s might, only His strength, exhibited here in its fullness and completeness, is the true explanation.

But this unrestricted exhibition of divinity is a rare instance, perhaps a unique instance, in Jewish history. Ordinarily, instead of revealing Himself, God conceals Himself. God appears to us, for the most part, through TZIMTSUM, through the concentration of His laws, through a kind of veil of withdrawal. Our sages, in interpreting the MA’ASEH B’REISHIS, the account of the creation of the universe, picture God even there as revealing Himself only partially, as employing only part of His strength and His power. God is depicted as having created the world through the medium of ten expressions, and then, our sages tell us, He turned to the universe and said, “V’AMAR L’OLAM DAI,” “I have done enough.” The rest of creation could now unfold without His immediate participation, without His direct intervention.

Similarly, when God revealed Himself to our Patriarchs, to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, it was always through TSIMTSUM, always concealing more than He revealed. “And I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac and to Jacob B’EL SHADDAI (as God Almighty), but I did not let them know My true Name.” Commenting on this verse, Rashi says: “I did not let them know My true Name” means “I was never known to them completely, I was known to them only partially.” Thus is the usual way of God’s appearance to men. It is only on Passover that, as the composer of the Haggadah tells us, God appeared to the people fully. In the words which are ascribed to the Holy One, Blessed be He, he says: “ANI V’LO HASHALIACH,” “I, and no other agent, no other messenger.” “ANI V’LO ACHER,” “I, God, I alone and no other.” This is the rare, the unusual, the special occurrence in the whole of Jewish history.

How are we to explain this special instance, and why is it that the Torah itself and the rabbinic tradition required us to dwell on it and to remember it constantly at every turn of our lives? If at the beginning of every event we already knew the finale, if at the dawn of history we knew the end, if at each moment of experience the fullness of God’s glory was before us so that we could understand what the future would bring in every detail, then surely all initiative, all striving, all effort would be destroyed.

Let us think of the history of Israel for a moment. Could father Abraham have had the strength to accept the Divine command of “LECH LECHA,” the command to go forth into the world, if he had known in advance the terrible trials and the enormous responsibilities to which his future would bring him? Could he have had the strength to assume willingly the suffering and the burden which were to be his lot? Could our father Isaac have prayed for sons to carry on his tradition, if he had known in advance that he would father not only a Jacob but also an Esau? Would he not rather have suppressed his prayer and have accepted his childlessness with resignation? And could father Jacob have encouraged Joseph as he did if he had known in advance what Joseph’s experiences would lead to? If he had known the trials which awaited the young boy in his exile to Egypt? If he had known that years of bitter slavery lay ahead for all his descendants in Egypt? Could any man face his life, could he face his future, if all that lay ahead were known to him in advance, if the SHECHINAH, the majesty and glory of God, were revealed to him in fullness and completeness?

Even Moses who came closer to God than any man in all history, even Moses who, alone among all the prophets, spoke directly to the Divine Presence, was unable to see far beyond the present moment. He stood before God pleading for Israel and asking bitterly, “LAMAH HAREIOSAH?” “Why have You done evil and caused suffering to this people?” He could not see where the suffering and where the apparent evil would lead. And it is for this reason, because we never know the meaning of the events of the present, that we are commanded to give thanks to God, not only for the obviously good things that happen to us, but also for the seemingly bad. Bound up in every evil and catastrophic event, bound up in all our suffering and all our anguish there may be, for all we know, the seeds of happiness and ultimate fulfilment. Because we are not ordinarily permitted to see the fullness of the Divine Glory and are prevented by God’s very nature from knowing Him completely, we have no knowledge of the future. Whether it seems to us good or evil, we know that our vision is limited and that we must go on with our work.

However, if man had to stop here, if all we could say was that we do not know what the future may bring, where would we find hope and strength in the face of adversity? If there were no indications in history of the almighty power of God, if we had no assurances of His good will as well as of His absolute capacity to control all events, in what would we place our trust? Unless we had some anchor, unless we had some profound assurance, some source of strength, of hope, of trust and ennoblement, how could we ever begin to strive for great things? Man, his life surrounded by darkness and gloom, anguish and destruction, must have in the midst of all of his suffering some great, transcendant, assured power to which he turns constantly and in which he places his abiding trust. This is why we are reminded in our tradition of the exodus from Egypt. In the midst of our own despair the memory of their salvation brings us strength and comfort. As the psalmist puts it: “GAM KEE EILECH B’GAY TSALMOVES LO EERO RO, KEE ATOH EEMODEE,” “Even when I walk in the depths of darkness, in the valley of the shadow of death, even when I am surrounded by the profoundest despair, I shall not be afraid so long as I know that God is with me.”

God removes Himself from creation, so to speak, in order that man might return Him to creation. God withdraws Himself from the world so that man might have the privilege of bringing Him back into the world. It is only in this way that man can be exalted and ennobled above other creatures. It is only in this way that man can truly fulfill the highest possibilities of his nature. And it is only in this way that man can be genuinely secure. Because God made us free, we can never be certain of the consequences of our actions. We do not know the MIDOH AMITIS, we do not know the true nature of God and, hence, do not understand the universe. Since we do not know the true nature of the world, we cannot judge the consequences of what we do at any given moment. We know only that in this world, because we are free, there is much for us to do and much for us to build. There is much to aspire to and much to achieve. God will bring our striving to fulfillment and will protect us as He did Israel at the Red Sea. We know that His will alone prevails.

In the marvelous tale of the exodus from Egypt there is the ultimate expression of the basis of our faith in God’s power. In those memories we find the strength and the confidence with which to face the world. We pray that as on Passover we are mindful of the power with which God drew us forth from Egypt, so may we at each moment of our lives, in every trial, through every struggle and every anxiety, be equally mindful of His presence. May we remember that the God Who saved our people from Pharaoh because their cause was righteous, continues to support all men whose cause is righteous. He Whose strength was sufficient to subdue Pharaoh, is the God Whose strength assures us always that the just, the noble, the good and the merciful will prevail.
