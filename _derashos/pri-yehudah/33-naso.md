This parshah contains the continuation of the counting of the people of Israel. In a way, by counting things, by putting a separate number on each item, we actually bring about a division. When we count people, each one becomes a number of a total community. But at the same time, we thereby designate to each one an individual place in life. — The Torah and its commandments not only preserve society as a whole, but also protect the individual as well.

The various laws given to us in this parshah point in this direction, namely, to protect the “MISHPOCHA,” family, and also the individual man. The Torah gives us the parshah of “SOTAH” (Numbers V:11-31) wherein a woman has been accused by her husband of going astray from him. Not only does the Torah refuse to accept this accusation unless full proof is brought forth, but it tries to find a psychological approach to put her husband’s mind straight and cure him of his jealousy.

Then there is the parshah of the Nazarite (Numbers VI:1-21), “When either man or woman shall distinguish themselves to vow a vow of a Nazarite . . .” By some Rabbis such a one is considered a holy man and he is commanded to bring a “KORBON” when he returns to an everyday way of living. But others consider him sinful for separating from the everyday life. — Both interpretations are right. As for the person himself, he may become a Nazarite. He may become as holy as the High Priest. But to make this a way of life for all the people, that is wrong. A whole society can not live like Nazarites. Here too, the Torah protects the rights of each individual.

We also read in this parshah about the priestly blessings (Number VI:22-27). They were all in a singular form, although the blessings were directed towards all the people, because a blessing is not the same to everyone. To one it may be a blessing, and to another a curse. — When a man is drowning, a blessing for riches has no meaning to him. — There, too, the Torah considers the individual in his needs.

Further, in the parshah of “KORBONOT” (Number VII:12-87), the Torah lists each man’s “KORBON” separately. Although they were all alike and the Torah could have been sparing of space and words by saying that all brought that particular “KORBON,” still, it purposely reiterated each one’s “KORBON” as if there existed a difference between them. Because, even when the “KORBONOT” are physically the same, the “MACHSHAVAH,” the thoughts behind the “KORBONOT” are not the same with all concerned. And the “MACHSHAVAH” is the main purpose of the “KORBON.” Here again the Torah points to the individual’s contribution to the whole society.

The words for counting or numbering used in this text are “NASO,” literally meaning “to lift up,” and “TIFKOD,” literally meaning “missing or empty,” as we find in I. Samuels XX:27, “VAYIPAKED MEKOM DAVID,” “and David’s place was empty.” The Torah does not use the word “SOFOR,” “to count,” because by counting, we separate things and that is not the objective of the Torah. The purpose is to lift each individual up, as “NASO,” and make the missing or the empty place of one single person noticeable in society.

In summary, the law of “SOTOH” was given to safeguard the family life of our people, which is of great importance in the process of creating a wholesome society. Among other things, broken families cause juvenile delinquency. In the case where the husband was jealous of his wife and suspected her of betraying him, not only did the Torah tell the husband that he must have good proof for his accusation and that without proof the wife was declared innocent, but the Torah used a psychological approach in clearing up the problem. A ceremonial test was arranged, and when the accused woman passed the test, the husband was cured of his jealousy. — Thus the Torah prevented a family from being broken up and safeguarded its future.

The Torah counts people “LEMISHPACHOTAM,” by their families. In order to safeguard the MISHPACHOT, the Torah used the word “NASO” for counting, meaning, to lift them up in accordance with what that family is contributing to the general welfare of society.

“LAMAH NISMECHAH PARSHAT NAZIR LEPARSHAT SOTAH, LOMAR LOCH: HAROEH SOTAH BEKILKULAH YAZIR ET ATZMO MIN HAYAYIN” (Sotah 2a). “Why is the section dealing with the Nazarite placed in juxtaposition to the section dealing with the “Sotah”? To tell you that he who has once seen the Sotah in her disgrace should abstain from wine.” — Yes! The husband who suspects his wife of betrayal, must first search his innerself. Perhaps he is responsible for his wife’s failings. Perhaps he is the one who breaks up the family. When a man continuously indulges in pleasures and thereby neglects his family, he has no one to blame but himself. In that case, Nazirot is necessary to protect the MISHPACHAH. When he is finished with the designated time for curbing his uncontrolled appetite for pleasures, the Torah commands that he must bring an offering as an atonement for the extra restriction that he had to take upon himself, namely, that of becoming a Nazarite. For our sages tell us: “LO DAI SHEATORAH ASRAH ALECHA V’ATA BA LEESOR AL NAFSHCHA DVARIM HAMUTARIM.” “Is it not enough that the Torah has many things forbidden to you, and you want to withhold from yourself those things that the Torah allowed?” (Sages) — This man had to become a Nazarite in order to save his family. This was his medicine, this put him straight. But to recommend Nazarition to everyone is not in accordance with the Torah. “KSHOT ATZMECHA V’ACHAR KACH KSHOT ACHERIM,” “Be just yourself before thou demandest it from others.”

The KOHANIM, priests, the leaders of the people, were commanded “KO T’VORCHU ET B’NAI ISRAEL AMOR LAHEM,” “Thus ye shall bless the children of Israel saying unto them,” “YEVARECHCHA HASHEM,” “The Eternal Bless thee.” The word “YEVARECHCHA” is in the singular form to denote that when the leadership of a people makes up the laws and regulations, it must take into consideration how they will affect man in every walk of life. Many leaders think only in terms of the nation as a whole and forget the individual man. Such a nation may be able to save itself from the dangers from without, but may rot from within. On the other hand, the spiritual giants, the KOHANIM, who were trained against over-indulgence in earthly pleasures, had clear and understanding minds for the right needs of the people as a whole, and at the same time safeguarded each and every family as in the case of the “SOTAH” who was brought to the KOHEN for a test to prove her innocence. And so, too, the individual was looked after, as in the case of the Nazarite who was commanded to bring his “KORBON” to the KOHEN.

In today’s HAFTORAH we read that prior to the birth of Samson, who was destined to become the leader of his people, his mother was commanded not to drink strong drinks for her child was destined to be a Nazarite unto God from the womb to the day of his death. That was not a form of asceticism, a rigid complete abstention from everything that is earthly. It was rather advice, that as a leader, he must be able to devote his total attention towards his people. Therefore, he must learn to curb some of his personal pleasures. And that training had to have its beginning with his mother.

The wise men of Greece and especially of Sparta placed all their emphasis on the national life. Towards that goal they geared their educational system. The individual life meant nothing to them. Socrates demanded the rights of the individual and was considered a traitor by his own people. But our people understood him to be correct. Our sages preached three things: “BEN ADAM LAMAKOM, BEN ADAM LACHAVERO UBEN ADAM LEATZMO,” “A perfect man must be correct in his relationship with God, and in his relationship with other men, and to his own self.” Whoever leaves out one of these is not considered an “ADAM HASHALEM,” a perfect man.
