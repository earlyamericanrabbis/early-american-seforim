ASARAH NISYONOS NISNASAH AVRAHAM AVINU, V’AMAD B’KULAM, L’HODIA KAMAH CHIBATO SHEL AVRAHAM AVINU (Pirke Avot).

Our sages enumerate the different tests to which Abraham was put by God and with each of these tests Abraham gave an excellent accounting of himself and proved to be a forward and loyal follower of God’s commandments. But of all the tasks, there is one that occupies an especially prominent position, one that is deemed to be the most exacting of all the ten, that is, the AKEDAH, when God commanded Abraham: “Take thy beloved son Isaac and bring him up to the Mount Moriah, and offer him up there for an offering.” This test of the AKEDAH is so powerfully dramatized in the Torah and in the Medrashic source that we have incorporated it into our most sacred prayer on Rosh Hashanah, when we ask God to forgive our shortcomings and to bless us with a year of joy and happiness, because our father Abraham carried out God’s commandment in connection with the AKEDAH.

Many commentators ask: “Why is it that this test, the test of the AKEDAH, is considered such an unusual and praiseworthy achievement on the part of Abraham? Is it not true that throughout our history, countless thousands of Jewish people, men, women and children, have given away life for the glory of the Torah and for the greatness of the God of Israel?” There are even historic events recorded, where parents actually killed their own children in order to avoid the imminent threat of a forcible acceptance of another faith and the rejection of Judaism. And, mind you, in these cases, the people did not even have a direct call from God to give their lives for Him, and still they did not waiver in their loyalty. Why should we then be so surprised in the case of Abraham who did have a mandate from God concerning the sacrifice of his son Isaac? If any man were to receive a personal command from God, as did Abraham, surely he would answer in the affirmative. No one would reject God’s order. After all, it is within the power of the Almighty at any moment to take the life that He ordered to be sacrificed. It is therefore proper for us to ask: “In what respect was the AKEDAH so far superior to all the other tests which Abraham carried out?”

The answer perhaps can be found in the outstanding characteristic of our father Abraham, namely the trait of kindness, compassion, concern and pity. We find, for instance, in the life of Abraham the quality of concern for the wayfarer, for the stranger, for the friendless. The Torah tells us that Abraham planted a tree, a garden, and our sages comment and tell us that this was an inn, a hotel where one received food, drink and also warmth, sympathy, and understanding. In other words, Abraham gave not only sustenance to still hunger and quench thirst for the moment, but he also gave physical accompaniment to his guests afterwards, walking with them part of the way when they were leaving his hospitality, to make them feel his continuing friendship. And he accompanied them in a deeper sense. He gave them to take along of this quality which he possessed, the quality of love, of mercy, so that with this warmth each would have the strength and courage to continue, and for those who needed it, to strive for self-dependence, self-reliance, without having to resort to the abasement of seeking the help of others. Such an attitude is far superior to ordinary charity, for charity helps the needy at the moment of giving and does not offer respect and dignity. But when an unfortunate one is given not only charity, but guidance, understanding, and love, this is the ultimate in giving.

The great and distinguished Dubner Magid, in interpreting one of the sentences of the Torah concerning our attitude towards the poor, comments on the words “DEI MACHSORO ASHER YECHSAR LO” (Deuteronomy XV-8), “You are to give to the poor man sufficient, which he will lack”: “There seems to be a repetition of the same words “DEI MACHSORO ASHER YECHSAR LO.” Why this apparently superfluous statement? And the Dubner Magid explains that the first part of the verse, “DEI MACHSORO,” means giving to the poor man what he is lacking at the moment. The second part of the text, “ASHER YECHSAR LO,” means what he will lack. In other words, in addition to the immediate assistance, one is also to offer some plan, some constructive aid by which the unfortunate man might avoid having such difficulty and want in the future.

Throughout this parshah we see very clearly Abraham’s compassion for the suffering, for those who are in need, for those who are friendless. When he sees three strangers coming toward him, he does not wait for them to come to the door of his tent to seek his aid. No! “VAYAR VAYARATZ LIKRATAM,” “and when he saw them, he ran to meet them,” making sure they will allow him the privilege of being their host. Then there is the instance of the wicked city of Sodom, a city filled with cruelty and inhumanity to strangers, something which was the exact antithesis to Abraham’s philosophy. Yet, when Sodom was to be destroyed by God for its wickedness, Abraham seeks to save even this corrupt community. He wants to save it for the sake of the righteous people who are in its midst. Mind you, he stresses the word “B’SOCH HAYIR,” “in the midst of the city.” Perhaps there are fifty righteous men within the city. He was not talking about the righteous people who are somehow cloistered in some library, in some study room. Nor the righteous who cannot bear to see the evil that is going on around them and are therefore isolated somewhere in an ivory tower. It is not on behalf of these that Abraham feels the entire city should be saved. No, he refers to those whole-souled ones who are “B’SOCH HAYIR,” who manifest their righteousness and piety by being within the mass of the people and by trying to guide and influence them towards good. Further, there is the incident with Abimelech who committed a terrible wrong against Abraham, having kidnapped Abraham’s wife, Sarah. Surely this was both an appalling moral and personal offense. Yet Abraham prayed to God for Abimelech. Later, Abraham is again hurt by the king, and he criticizes the evil-doer, pointing out his errors. But even though Abraham rebukes Abimelech, the Torah tells us “VAYICHRETU SH’NEHEM BRIT,” “They concluded with a covenant.” There was no severance of relationship, there was no complete irrevocable break.

This trait in Abraham, love for humanity, compassion for the poor and friendless that made it impossible for him to be harsh with anyone, this characteristic was the one which was put to the test in the case of the AKEDAH. God wanted Abraham to understand that all these virtues, wonderful as they may be, must sometimes be curbed for the good of society. The Hebrew word “MIDAH,” “measure,” means just this, to measure when and where to practice these virtues. Abraham was prepared for this test of actually curbing his own natural inclination to be kind and gracious, to be forgiving and merciful. There were two smaller trials which prepared him for this great one, the AKEDAH. The first occurred when Sarah decided to separate Isaac from Ishmael, because she realized the very bad influence that this wild and reckless son of Hagar was exerting upon Isaac. Abraham protested Sarah’s insistence that Ishmael and his mother be forced to leave. But he obeyed God’s command to do as Sarah deemed best, curbed his own merciful tendency, and did what was morally right. We find later in the history of the Jewish people a case where misplaced mercy substituted for righteous judgment brought about a great tragedy. King Saul gave clemency to one of his prisoners of war and as a result Haman appeared on the scene centuries later and all but destroyed the entire Jewish people. Sarah felt that Ishmael would destroy in Isaac those qualities required to become the future standard-bearer of the principles of the Torah, and Abraham, against his personal wish and desire, and because God also endorsed her claim, followed Sarah’s advice.

With these experiences behind him, the lesson of the city of Sodom, and the driving away of Hagar and her son, we read “VAYEHI ACHAR HADVARIM HAELEH,” “and it came to pass after these things,” having already been taught that mercy must be in consonance with the commandments of God, that no consideration can take precedence over God’s wish, Abraham was then prepared to carry out this most exacting of all the ten tests. He realized that the commandments of the Almighty are the controlling factor in human behavior. If there is no fear of God, there is also no bar to the commission of the most cruel and the most brutal crime. So did Abraham say to Abimelech: “If there is no fear of God in this (or in any) place, then they will not stop even at murder.” No matter how merciful, how kind a person may be, if that kindness is not based upon reverence for God, then he will find a way to rationalize any crimes, because only through a belief in Him can one be guaranteed a way of life which would negate such rationalization.

And so, Abraham, with his fundamental trait of mercy and kindness, stands before his greatest and severest test. Through this necessary ordeal he must prove his understanding that his great compassion, virtuous though it may be, must be in accordance with the commandment of God. And Abraham stifles his great goodness of heart, and his natural deep love for his son and prepares to offer him to the Divine Being Whom he loves and reveres even more. So fully was he ready to obey God’s command, so completely did he demonstrate his integration with the will of the Almighty, that we his children to this day ask God to forgive our shortcomings and to bless us with a year of joy and happiness in our most sacred prayer on Rosh Hashanah, because our father Abraham carried out God’s commandment in connection with the “AKEDAH.”
