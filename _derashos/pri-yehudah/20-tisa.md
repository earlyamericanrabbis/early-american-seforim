The story of the “Golden Calf” as it is related in today’s parshah is very perplexing. How could Aaron, the leader, the teacher of his people, be so easily persuaded to make an idol? Scripture does not tell us of Aaron’s resistance to their demand to do so. And how could the people, the very same ones who just a short time before had heard the proclamation of the Ten Commandments, in particular “I am the Eternal, Thy God. Thou shalt have no other gods before My Face. Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image . . .” (Exodus XX:1-5), act as they did? And later we find (Exodus XXIV:7) “and he (Moses) took the Book of the Covenant, and read it in the ears of the people: and they said, all that God has spoken, will we do, and hearken,” then, how could they even take it into their minds to commit such an outrageously disgraceful act as to fashion an idol for a god?

In order to answer this and other puzzling questions pertaining to the golden calf, it will be necessary for us to know somewhat about the spiritual development of man. In the same way that the physical improvement of man comes gradually in degrees, step by step, so is this spiritual attainment a slow process till one reaches the full height. — Adam, who was formed directly by God, did not understand God as One from Whom you cannot hide, when he said: “I heard Thy voice in the garden . . . and I hid myself” (Genesis III:10). Cain had not developed his perception of God when he said: “I know not. Am I my brother’s keeper?” (Genesis IV:9). Even Sarah, who was the prototype helper to Abraham in his One-God idea, failed to understand, we learn from scripture, the fullness of God’s power when God rebuked Abraham by saying: “Is anything too hard for the Eternal?” (Genesis XVIII:14). Man, even at the peak of his highest physical development, is still only flesh and blood, and as such, he could only understand the unknown when it was based on a material foundation. It is hard to believe that the idol worshipper really thought that the carved piece of wood or stone was God. The idol was to him rather a symbol of his concept of the unknown. — In man’s constant struggle against the elements of nature, he searched for some unknown force that created and at present controls nature. At the beginning of his development, he pictured the deity to be found in fire, in water, in the outstanding power of certain animals or other living creatures. He believed that the god demanded from the people he created the things that he as a man physically desired and in such fashion worshipped him. When man became powerful and controlled many people, he declared himself as a god. The scientist who thinks that he can explain somewhat the phenomenon of nature, thinks that nature is the deity. The constant changing from one symbol to another had much to do with man’s physical and mental progress.

At Sinai, we find, the children of Israel said: “Speak thou (Moses) with us, and we will hear: but let not God speak with us, lest we die” (Exodus XX:16). The voice of God was too awesome for men to comprehend. They had to have the constant teaching and reminding of Moses. Had they had then the “Mishkon” (sanctuary), and services held therein, they would not have deviated so fast from the proclamations at Sinai. All the miracles and wonders that they beheld at the departure from Egypt and all the phenomenon at Sinai, made little impression on them. Only their great leader Moses, who gave them the teachings of Sinai, was of importance to them. He was the link between them and the Almighty. And then what happened? “KI ZE MOSHEH HAISH LO YADANU MEH AYA LO” (Exodus XXXII:1). “For the man Moses . . . we do not know what has become of him.” Rashi comments: “That it implies that Satan showed them something that looked like Moses being carried on a bier in the air high above in the skies.”

Living in the desert, where most of the precepts of the Torah had no place and couldn’t be performed, was in itself a hinderance to their faith. The Jew who performs daily hundreds of precepts will not deviate so easily from his accepted faith, for most of the precepts serve as a reminder of that faith.

The children of Israel did proclaim, “NAASEH V’NISHMA,” “We will do and hearken.” If only they had had the “NAASEH,” the precepts to perform, then the “NISHMA,” the things that they heard, would not have been so quickly obliterated from their memories. They asked of Aaron: “Arise, make us gods, which shall go before us” (Exodus XXXII:1). They did not ask for a specific idol, but for gods. In other words, what they looked for was symbols and signs as reminders, for in verse 4 we read “and he (Aaron) made it a molten calf: and they said, these are thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt.” Although Aaron made only one golden calf, they proclaimed it as many gods, as if to say that this golden calf is the physical indication of their gradual development in the understanding of the unknown.

Aaron did not realize that this sudden outburst of the so-called deep religious feeling among the people might lead them to a total denial in the God of their fathers. It is in the nature of man to accept things where there is the least resistance. After all, a god that can be formed in man’s imagination and can serve his whim and fancy, is much easier to follow than one who appeared at Sinai with a long list of precepts to obey.

History repeats itself. We have today movements which win many, even observant Jews, into their ranks by selling them symbolistic Judaism instead of Torah Judaism. Those who follow really begin to believe, that this is all that is demanded from a Jew, namely, to belong to such a temple and once in a while to be present to a service.

Moses pleaded with God to forgive the people of Israel for the sin of making the golden calf by saying: “Wherefore, O Eternal, doth Thy wrath glow against Thy people, whom Thou hast brought forth out of the land of Egypt . . .” (Exodus XXXII:11). Moses emphasized the fact that they came out of Egypt and what they did was what they had learnt from the Egyptians.

The lesson we learn from this parshah is that a belief that is based on “NAASEH,” on deeds, on performances, will also have the “NISHMA,” the remembrance. The words that they heard on Sinai would have remained with the people for all time and it would not have been necessary for Moses to be with them to teach and remind them. On Simchat-Torah when we celebrate the ending and again the beginning of the Torah, we read the parshah which tells us: “VAYAMAT SHAM MOSHEH,” “and Moses died.” One may ask, why read this parshah at a time when we celebrate the Torah that Moses brought to us? One of the rabbis declared: This is to prove that Moses is not indispensible to us. One who observes what the Torah proscribes is safe and sound in his faith.
