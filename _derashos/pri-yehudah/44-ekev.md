“And it shall come to pass, because ye hearken to those ordinances, and keep, and do them, that the Lord thy God shall keep with thee the covenant and the mercy which He sware unto thy fathers” (Deuteronomy VII:12). Rashi comments on the words “EKEV TISHMEUN,” “because ye hearken” (literally the word EKEV means “heel”). “MITZVOT SHEADAM DASH BEAKVO TISHMEUN,” “Commandments which one steps over with his heel (as having little meaning), you will observe, then the blessings enumerated in the Torah will rest on you.”

The Torah must be accepted and kept in its entirety without making distinctions between one law and the other. Our sages said (Pirke Avot), “BE HEEDFUL OF A LIGHT PRECEPT AS OF A GRAVE ONE, FOR THOU KNOWEST NOT THE GRANT OF REWARD FOR EACH PRECEPT.” Such laws that seem to us trivial and petty must be taken as God’s commandments. We must give attention even to commandments which are now accepted as basic in social behavior and seem to us unnecessary to treat as commandments. For all the MITZVOT interlock one to the other, and great results may come from what is apparently the smallest, and seems to us the least important commandment.

The Torah is made up of commandments pertaining to the dealings between man and man. These are the logical and reasonable laws. It is also made up of commandments pertaining to the relationship between man and God. Naturally, it is much easier to accept a law that man’s mind will more or less understand because it is logical, rather than one that has no reason for its observance. On the other hand, one who accepts the authority of God and observes His commandments unquestioningly stands a better chance to remain observant of these commandments than the one who accepted them through logical reasoning only. Logic changes somewhat with time. It can be twisted about and the words of God can not depend on it. — When the Jews stood at Sinai, they exclaimed: “NAASEH V’NISHMA,” “We will perform and hearken.” In other words, they understood the great secret that God revealed only to his angels (Sages), namely, that the understanding will come after the performing of those commandments. The one who lives in accordance with the Torah will not seek logical reasons for his way of life. He finds himself perfected and satisfied in his physical as well as his mental desires. To the observant Jew even the statutes make sense. As the Prophet said: “MIBSARY ECHZEH ELOKIM,” “On my own flesh I can see God.” It may well mean that when one looks upon himself and beholds the tremendous change that living in accordance with the Torah has made on him, this fact alone is the best reason to hearken to the commandments.

Further in the Parshah we read: “And now Israel, what doth God, thy God, demand from thee? Nothing but to fear God, thy God, to walk in all His ways and to love Him and to serve God, thy God, with all thy heart and with all thy soul. To keep the commandments of God and His Statutes which I command thee this day for thy own good” (Deuteronomy X:12-13).

The uniqueness of love and fear together can only be in God, as our sages said (Midrash Tanaim): “There is no love where there is fear and there is no fear where there is love except in God.” The AKEDAT YITZCHAK explains it as follows: “At the time when man observes the creation and the infinite wisdom displayed in it, he can not help but love, praise and desire to know more of God.” As the Psalmist said (Psalm XLII:3): “My soul thirsteth for God, for the living God.” Soon man will step back in reverence, realizing his own limitations and smallness before the One Who knows all. This sort of experience will help him to observe unquestionably the commandments of the Torah which in turn are a blessing to humanity. Our sages say: “God seeks nothing from this world except the fear of Heaven” (Shabbat 31b). Yes, when man’s reverence of God comes through his full recognition of the Creator then he combines in himself love for and fear of his Creator.

The CHOVAT HALVAVOT said: “If one does a MITZVAH in order to gain anything thereby then he serves himself and not God, and that may be considered as worshiping his own idol.” When one gives charity, our sages said, he is allowed to give it for a purpose. “BISHVIL SHYICHYE B’NI . . .,” “that his son’s life may be spared.” Because, when helping one in distress, it makes no difference for what reason the giver has given. As long as the one who was in need was helped, that is all that is required of him. But, when it comes to MITZVOT between man and God, and God does not need our help, then they are to be performed since God has commanded us to perform them and we accept His will. We do it not because we fear His punishments or for any rewards, but purely out of our deep reverence for Him.

The word “EKEV” in the beginning of our text refers to the MITZVOT that one is performing in his normal way of living. The Torah does not demand of man to do only the MITZVOT which cause him to fight resistance in order to fulfill them. But even pertaining to those MITZVOT that are a daily part of his life as a man among people, as a normal social behavior, if man does these “LESHEM SHAMAIM,” “because God so commanded him,” all the blessings of the Torah will rest upon him. For, the performance of all MITZVOT “LESHEM SHAMAIM,” assures that even the ones difficult to carry out, or those of the statute type for which there is no logical explanation, will come more naturally.
