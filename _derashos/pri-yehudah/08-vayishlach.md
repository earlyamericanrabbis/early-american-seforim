“And Jacob sent messengers before him to Esau his brother unto the land of Seir, the field of Edom. And he commanded them, saying: Thus shall ye say unto my lord Esau: Thus sayeth thy servant Jacob: I have sojourned with Laban, and stayed until now. And I have oxen, and asses and flocks, men-servants and maid-servants, and I have sent to tell my lord, that I may find favor in thy sight.”

This episode according to Nachmanides is narrated to illustrate how God saved his servant and redeemed him from an enemy stronger than himself, by sending his angel and delivering him. We also learn that Jacob did not rely upon his righteousness, but took all practical measures to meet the situation. It contains the further lesson that whatever happened to the Patriarchs happened to their offspring, and we should follow Jacob’s example by making a three-fold preparation in our fight against Esau’s descendants, with prayer, gifts (appeasement), and war.

The YALKUT SHIMONI comments: that at the time when Jacob called his brother Esau “My lord,” God said to him. “Thou hast humbled thyself before your brother and called him lord for eight times. I’ll therefore establish for Esau eight kings before the time thy children will have a king.”

Our Patriarch Jacob, though he humbled himself out of necessity to protect the lives of his family and himself from Esau and his approaching army, was nevertheless punished severely. Since he was the one into whose trust was given the establishment of the House of Israel which was commissioned by God to carry His moral code to the world, Jacob had not the right to relinquish his honor before his brother Esau. Furthermore, Jacob considered Esau unworthy to receive the blessings of their father, as Esau was unworthy of the birthright which he had himself sold for a pot of lentils. Our sages say: “A king is not permitted to forsake his honor, because it belongs to the people under his reign and not to himself.” Jacob, who was now the head of the Children of Israel, erred by dishonoring himself when he called his brother “master” and himself “servant.”

Yes, whatever happened to our patriarch Jacob happened to his children. The one factor that kept our people for almost two thousand years in GALUT, although the “CHAVLEI SHEL MOSHIACH,” “the birth pains of Messiah” had been going on continuously, is that we humbled ourselves before our enemies. We allowed ourselves to be thrown and trampled and we even kissed the whip that beat us. God refers to us as “B’NI B’CHORI ISRAEL,” “My oldest son is Israel,” but we did not behave accordingly. The chapter of “V’SHUVU BANIM LIGVULAM,” “My children shall return to their own borders” has appeared to us in various forms, but we managed to discredit any of the movements for “SHIVAS TZION,” “Return to Zion,” with slogans, both religious in nature and irreligious. Some spiritual leaders erroneously interpreted the words of the psalmist, “If God will not build a house, in vain will be the toil of those that build.” In other words, they were waiting for Messiah to return us to our homeland. According to them, any movement toward “SHIVAS TZION” was considered a breach in our religion. In the opposite direction, a great many of our people were looking for an opportunity to assimilate themselves with those among whom they lived. If it were not for the Torah and Rabbinical teachings, we would have disintegrated a long time ago. Only our truly religious and our great literature kept us mindful of our great “YICHUS,” that our forefathers brought spiritual life to the world at a time when humanity as a whole was spiritually in total darkness.

In the Talmud (Chagigah 5) says Rabbi Samuel in the name of Rav: “There is a place for God that is called “MISTARIM,” “Secret,” from out of which we hear a constant cry bemoaning the loss of our self-respect in GALUT and how we handed that respect over to the peoples of the world.” The “MISTARIM” where God is found is our subconscious mind. Within the subconsciousness of every one of us, we can always find a divine spark that awakens in us some feelings towards the pride that we have lost. When we wake up from our spiritual lethargy and look at ourselves in the right perspective, we see the difference between ourselves and many a people that we have admired. Our beautiful family life, and the great minds that we produced from the past to the present in every field of knowledge, are envied everywhere. In ancient times, when humanity as a whole had hardly advanced into morality from the world of beast, we had already produced the Patriarchs, the Law-giver Moses, and others. At a time when we were denied admission into places of learning, we produced the greatest lore of all times, the Talmud. To the envy of our adversaries, we produce today learned men and women well beyond the ratio in proportion to our population. Then, is there any reason to humble ourselves before other peoples? Experience has proved that when a man lost his fortune but kept his courage and pride, he eventually made a comeback. The same is true about a people. When they are subdued by an enemy, but still have pride in themselves, in their country, and in their achievements, they will regain their independence.

In the Talmud (Sanhedrin 20), Reish Lakish says: “King Solomon first ruled over all Israel and then only over Jerusalem, and later only over his bed, and later over his staff and bag. But in the end he became again king over all Israel.” King Solomon was privileged to regain his throne after he had been cast away by the ASHMEDAI (Asmodeus) to a distant land where no one knew him, where he was forced to go about begging from house to house. But even then, when all he possessed were his staff and bag, he considered himself a king. He constantly proclaimed “ANI SHLOMOH,” “I am Solomon.”

Messiah, according to our sages, is waiting at the gates of Rome for the call to redeem our people from GALUT. They call it the “CHEVLEI SHEL MOSHIACH,” “The Pain of Messiah.” This may mean that Messiah is in great pain at finding our people not yet ready for the fulfillment of his mission. Through all the years of our GALUT, he found us apathetic, accepting our plight, and with our will power lost to fight for our honor by redeeming our homeland out of the hands of our enemies.

In today’s SIDRA we also find that when our forefather Jacob fought for his right, he surely won. The Torah tells us that while Jacob was returning to his father’s house, his way was blocked by an angel who was aligned with Esau. They fought and Jacob was victorious to the point where the angel begged to be set free. Jacob did release the angel, but only after the angel blessed him. The angel told him: “Your name will not be YAAKOV (which means “holding on to the heel” — as was the case when he held on to Esau’s heel at the time of their birth). But Israel will be your name, which means a master, a ruler, for you fought against an angel of God and against people (the army of Esau) and prevailed.” The Torah also tells us that Jacob fought the angel till it became morning, “AD ALOS HASHACHAR,” “till the morning star came out.” That is to say, only through the night, or through the time of our GALUT, do we have to fight for our rights and for our honor. Only during the time when we were scattered over all the world without an address of our own, was credit for our achievements given not to us but to the countries in which we lived. During the time we held on to the heel of other people, we lost our own honor and with it one third of our people. Then there awoke in us the “CHEVLO SHEL MOSHIACH,” “the birth pangs of the Messiah,” the strong willpower to return to our homeland. In the last seventeen years since the independence of the State of Israel, when the sun began to shine somewhat for our people, in that country, and in every land where Jews are found, there was and is a feeling of pride in belonging to our people. The world began to recognize our accomplishments and our amazing ability to govern ourselves.

The Zohar points out that when Avram’s name became Abraham, it was never changed back to Avram, but when Yaakov’s name became Israel, we find it later changed back to Yaakov. The Zohar says that concerning Abraham, the Torah states “V’HAYAH SHIMCHA AVRAHAM.” Instead of using the regular future tense, “YHIYEH,” “will be,” the Torah used the past tense “HAYAH” placing in front of it the letter “VAV,” making “V’HAYAH,” thus changing the past to the future. But concerning Jacob, we find the regular future tense used in changing his name from Yaakov to Israel. This may indicate that Israel is his name only under certain conditions. Whenever Jacob shows weakness he is again Yaakov. Only when he shows strength is he called Israel. We too, the B’nai Israel, the Children of Israel, can only be worthy of that name when we awaken to the footsteps of the Messiah and help in the mission for the redemption of our people and the entire world.
