Says Rabbi Isaac: “The Torah ought to begin with “Hachodesh Hazeh Lochem” (Ex. XII) which is the first mitzvah given to Israel. But it began with B’REISHIT as proof against the hostile claim that we “stole” the land of Israel (Rashi Genesis 1).

The Book of books, The Torah, which is the highest moral code, and the best way of life ever devised for man, could not have chosen a more powerful introduction, a more effective slogan than the word “B’REISHIT.”

As in the realm of technology it is essential to find the “MOKOR,” “the root and origin,” in order to make further progress, so too, and in even greater degree does the origin, the initial point in a Torah morality, play a crucial role.

In technical endeavor, the B’REISHIT, “the beginning,” is the tool without which no development is possible. If one desires to further technical progress, he must first become thoroughly familiar with that technical field. For example, he who wishes to create a new machine must first be well-versed in the fundamental laws of physics, so that his invention might be a success. His efforts cannot begin midway, that is, he cannot commence with the motion stage of the machine and from that point hope to add something new. No, first he must master that which has been developed up to this point, and only then can he proceed intelligently to think of improvement.

The development of man also has its B’REISHIT. The child is born, weak, helpless and gradually he or she becomes a profound thinker, an inventor. It is this quality of gradual growth which distinguishes man from animal. The latter has little or no B’REISHIT. “An ox which is a day old,” say our sages, “is called an ox” (Baba Kama 65b). There is no gradual process among animals, for their creation is not from the B’REISHIT, from the beginning, but a continuation.

He who admires a painting is not by virtue of that fact either a connoisseur or an artist. Only he who observes and understands the fundamental artistic features of the painting and sees the beginning, the first strokes of the brush, can grasp the artistic ingenuity needed to produce a work of art. The inventors of television had to go back to the original principles of electronics in order to achieve one of the greatest creations of our time. To understand divinity, one must delve deep in the B’REISHIT and when one reaches a point beyond understanding, that is already divinity itself.

“And the earth was desolate, barren, and darkness was over the deep” (Genesis 1). When man gets to the bottom of B’REISHIT, back to a point where he sees beyond the beginnings of development but only “darkness over the deep,” when the mind runs up against that which is dark and past comprehension, at that point we are told “the spirit of God hovers over the waters.” Yes, when man comes up against the mystery of the dark and the unknown, the spirit of God enters.

So it is also with the psychiatrist, when he seeks to heal the disturbed. He must first go down deep into the subconscious of the patient. In the subconscious, can one find the B’REISHIT, the root, that lies at the bottom of the mental disorder.

God warns Adam not to eat of the Tree of Knowledge because to do so would mean that the law of B’REISHIT, of gradual process, is violated. Man, of course, would like to gain understanding by eating a fruit, by beginning not at the bottom but somewhere midway. The result would be catastrophic. For he would know only how to operate the mechanism of life as is, but that is all. He would deteriorate to the level of the animal without the God-given faculty of free choice and self-development.

The serpent has his own plan. He does not want man better cultivated than his own status quo. He urges man, therefore, to eat of the forbidden fruit (Gen. III). But God’s warning is clear — “and from the fruit in the center of the garden do not eat” (Gen. III, 3). Man must not begin at the center, he must start from the bottom, his growth must be gradual. Only then can he be master over his world, only then can he be a partner to God in the scheme of creation.

This same lesson is driven home to us in the story of Cain and Abel (Gen. IV). We read “and Cain brought from the fruit of the earth an offering to God.” Rashi, the great commentator, adds: “from the most inferior products,” and Abel offered from the first of his sheep and God turned toward Abel and his offering but to Cain and his offering “He did not turn.” Abel’s offering was preferred not only because of its superior grade, but because it had the additional quality of being the B’REISHIT, the first-born. He thus showed his understanding of the lesson of B’REISHIT, and that he would not repeat the sin of his parents who violated that all-important principle.

The Torah, we have said before, is the world’s highest moral code and as such it proclaims the principle of B’REISHIT, because in human relations that principle is paramount. Often we condemn angrily those who do wrong and we are ready to tear them apart in righteous indignation. But when we go beneath the surface, to the B’REISHIT of the incident, we may find that with that misdeed a great misfortune was averted. “Do not judge your fellowman till you get to his position,” teach our sages (Pirke Avot).

Many times we see someone terribly angry over something and immediately we label him hot-tempered, quarrelsome, etc., but when we get down to the B’REISHIT of what caused his outburst, we may well find that plenty of inflammable material accumulated in his heart and now with even a slight spark, the full explosion was inevitable.

Many a family tragedy would have been avoided if husband and wife cooly looked into the B’REISHIT of their discontent. The world would not now be under the yoke of the so-called cold war, and the Vietnam entanglement with so many casualties, if the nations had searched the B’REISHIT of their conflict and distrust. Perhaps, then, peace might have been attained.

The Torah did not begin with HACHODESH HAZEH LOCHEM, though that is the first MITZVAH given to Israel, because when it comes to Mitzvot there is no priority. “Ye shall be as careful with the less important as with the more important Mitzvot” says the sages (Pirke Avot). All Mitzvot are alike. But the Torah did give priority to the concept of B’REISHIT —because that is our strongest document that the Holy Land is ours, a land upon which the greater part of our Torah is based.

The B’REISHIT rejects the hostile claim that we “stole” the land from anyone, because in the beginning, it was ours. The patriarchs lived there. — True, we were forced out of the land, but as long as in the B’REISHIT it was ours, it is they and not we who are the usurpers. Now the land of Israel is back with us because of that B’REISHIT.

To us Jews, too, the B’REISHIT teaches a lesson. We had great difficulty in getting back the land of our fathers, because, as Gedaliah Bublick, the great religious Zionist said: “We based our claims for Eretz-Israel on the Balfour Declaration.” “That is an insult,” said he. Such a claim is not even intelligent because the answer is flung back in our faces. The Arabs tell us they were there all these centuries, they also fought to preserve their land, they too brought sacrifices. Therefore do they call us the aggressors. Our best and only claim is B’REISHIT, for that cannot be paralleled by any other people.

It is with the B’REISHIT approach that Rabbi Gold and Dr. Silver made the most effective argument before the U. N. Together with the Torah, which is called REISHIT, beginning, we can make our Torah and our land and our people the pride of all nations. Amen.
