Among the many lessons that we may derive from today’s parshah, one deserves priority for discussion, namely, “The word and its significance.”

Not all people realize how much damage is done to a person of whom one talks evil. “LASHON HARA,” “evil talk” about someone is considered by our sages as one of the greatest sins. It is classed as even worse than murder, for a murderer kills one, but with evil talk, a man destroys the one he is talking about, and to some extent also the one he is talking to, for now he too thinks badly of the one about whom evil was spoken. And also, the evil-talker kills himself as friend to the subject of the conversation and his family. — Very few people ever stop to think how important our words may be in our affairs with the people about us. There was a time when Jews trusted each other on the spoken word. Many great contracts were concluded on just the oral agreements. People knew whom to trust. The criterion if a man was genuine in his religion or not, was whether or not he kept his word. If a man’s words were so cheap to him that he uttered them meaninglessly, then very few took him seriously. But if he was careful with his utterances, such a one’s words were considered wise and were accepted.

With words, a man is able to mask his true identity. He can speak them in such a manner as to give them two widely different meanings, and thereby deceive people. The dishonest one will try to find refuge from criticism in interpreting his promises in a way that may justify him in the minds of his listeners. But eventually, the truth will become evident and the one whose words can not be trusted will reap the fruit of his own creation.

Our parshah begins: “And Moses spoke unto the heads of the tribes of children of Israel saying: This is the thing which the Lord has commanded. When a man voweth a vow unto the Lord, or sweareth an oath to bind his soul with a bond, he shall not break his word, he shall do according to all that proceeded out of his mouth . . .” (Numbers XXX:2-17). The “KLEY YAKAR” explains wherefore is the law of vow preceded with the words “ZE HADAVAR,” “this is the thing which the Lord has commanded,” and why did he have to speak to the heads of the tribes. That was so we may understand the law of vows. The Torah tells us that when a woman makes a vow, her husband or her father may nullify it. If any man makes a vow, he can go to a “CHACHAM,” “wise man,” and have his vow revoked. One may ask, who gave the husband or father or even the “CHACHAM” the power to make nought of one’s wish to bind himself to something or restrict himself from something by a vow? The answer is given in the Torah’s distinctiveness in directing this law to the heads of the tribes, thus pointing out that even a restriction that a man takes on himself has to receive the sanction of our leaders. The simplest words that a man utters may involve other people, therefore the decision as to whether or not his restraints are in line with the moral teachings of the Torah, lies with the findings of the “CHACHAM.” If, as in the case of a husband or father, the “CHACHAM” will feel that a certain vow may become a disturbing factor in the normalities of life or the lives of the people with whom he who vowed is in contact, he will hereby receive the authority from providence to nullify or to revoke the vow. One should not think for a minute that because he makes a vow or any kind of restriction on himself as an offering, that no other has the right to forbid his making a certain sacrifice. Even a man’s risking his own life for a good cause must have the sanction of the CHACHAMIM. As in the case of “KORBONOT,” if it is not done in accordance with the best judgment of our interpreters of the Torah, then, not only is the offering worthless as far as an offering is concerned, but it may even be considered as a sin, as if one would bring an offering outside the Bet-Hamikdash.

God’s Torah is called “TEMIMAH,” perfect. No further restraints are necessary on the part of man to make him more ideal than those imposed by the laws and regulations of the Torah. If one takes on himself further restrictions, he may be considered defective in his faith in the ability of the Torah to make the perfect man. And thus he may be considered as esteeming lightly the wisdom of the Torah teaching.

The Torah commands: “LO YACHEL DEVARO,” “he shall not break his word.” Rashi comments: “This is the same as ‘LO YECHALEL DEVORO’ (the root of YACHEL being CHOLOL), he shall not make his words to bear a secular character” (CHULIN). In other words, even the simplest utterances a man makes must be kept within the sphere of holiness. “KOL HAYOTZE MEPIV YAASEH,” “He shall do according to all that goeth forth from his mouth.” But one must not try to interpret his own words so as to say that they were not meant as they sounded. Therefore the Torah said “LO YACHEL DEVARO,” “Do not make your words unholy,” to be understood in different ways. They must be words in which your fellowman may have confidence.

In today’s parshah we also read: “The children of Reuben and the children of Gad had a very great multitude of cattle and when they saw the land of Gazer and the land of Gilead that, behold, the place was a place for cattle, they said (to Moses): Let this land be given to us, bring us not over the Jordan . . .” Moses, after a lengthy discussion agreed to that, when they promised to help in the war of liberating the land west of the Jordan (Numbers XXXII:1-42). Here, too, we find the importance of words, when Moses said to them: “Build ye cities for your little ones, and fields for your flock, and do that which hath gone out of your mouth.” — Moses made no contract with them, but only a verbal agreement. And how they kept that agreement! Moses demanded of them only “Let the land be subdued and afterwards ye may return,” but they undertook (v. 18) “We will not return to our homes until the children of Israel have inherited” (Joshua XXII), and that meant to stay on the western side the seven years during which they were dividing the land. And so, indeed, they did.

In summary, we must not claim sole ownership to the words that come out of our mouths nor must we reserve the right to interpret them as they best suit us. They must be considered holy even as the holy script for which we depend on the CHACHAMIN for interpretation. And when we utter words of restriction as in the form of a vow or the swearing of an oath, again our CHACHAMIN must make the decision if it is in line with the moral teachings of the Torah.

Our main introduction to the holy day of Yom Kippur is Kol Nidre, which nullifies all the vows and restrictions that each of us made during the year. Because in this “YOM HAKADOSH,” “Holy Day,” we must fully identify ourselves as having the complete confidence in the teachings of the Torah as the perfect guide in our way of life. And under such conditions, no other restrictions are necessary to make us good men in the eyes of God and in the eyes of the people of the world.
