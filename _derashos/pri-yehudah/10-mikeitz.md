This sidrah reveals the approach to the drama of GALUT MITZRAYIM, our exile in Egypt. We are told that there was a famine in Canaan and “VAYAR YAAKOV KI YESH SHEVER B’MITZRAYIM VAYOMER ALEHEM R’DU SHAMAH V’SHIVRU LANU OCHEL,” “Jacob saw that there was food in Egypt and he said to them, go and purchase for us food.”

Our father Jacob knew very well what God said to Abraham, “KI GER VHIYEH ZARACHO B’ERETZ LO LOHEM,” that “Thy children will be strangers in a land that is not theirs,” and “V’AAVADUM V’INU OTAM ARBA MEOT SHANAH,” “They will enslave them, afflict them for four hundred years.” Jacob certainly understood that this famine in his land was God’s way to lead him and his family into that strange land. He could have chosen another land besides Egypt, for God did not specify to which land they would be exiled. The choice might have been Aram, where his uncle Laban lived and where most of his children were born. But the Torah tells us: “ARAMI OVED AVI VAYERED MITZRAYMAH,” Laban was about to destroy our father Jacob and he went to Egypt. When Laban said to Jacob: “The daughters and the sons are mine and all that you have are mine,” Jacob felt that under such circumstances he had no chance to survive as a separate entity, whereas in Egypt he had hopes that his children would depart, as God’s promise was to Abraham, “V’ACHREI CHEN YETZU BIRCHUSH GADOL,” “That they will depart with great substance,” spiritual as well as material. In the text we read: “Jacob saw that there is food in Egypt.” The Hebrew word for food used here is “SHEVER” and it has three different meanings. “SHEVER” is from the root “BAR,” corn, crops. It is also “SHAVOR,” to break, to destroy, and also “SABEIR,” to hope (Zohar).

Jacob felt that in order to save his family from starvation, Egypt would be better than Aram, where Laban lived. Although Jacob knew that his children would suffer in Egypt, and they might even reach a breaking point, “SHAVOR,” but he had hopes “SABEIR,” that at least there his children would not disintegrate, might in fact be strengthened through their sufferings. As it was: “V’CHAASHER YAANU OSO KEN YIRBEH V’CHEN YIFROTZ,” “ the more they afflicted them the stronger they became.” Jacob’s hopes were expressed in prophetic words when he told his children: “Go down there and purchase food.” The Hebrew word for “go down” is “R’DU” and the numerical value of that word is two hundred and ten. That is exactly the time the children of Israel were in Egypt. God took one hundred and ninety years off from the promised exile because of the extra hard labor enforced on them by the Egyptians.

The Midrash says: We find in the Torah that when Jacob was about to escape from his brother Esau by going into “GALUT,” exile, “VAYIKRAH YITZCHAK EL YAAKOV VAYVORECH OSO,” “Isaac called in Jacob and blessed him.” The Midrash asks, “Why did he again bless him after he had already previously blessed him?” “ELA SHERAH YITZCHAK B’RUACH HAKODESH SHEASIDIM BANAV LEHIGALOT, AMAR LO, BO V’AVERECHCHA BRACHAH SHEL GALUT, UMAH HEN HABRACHOT, B’SHESH TZAROT YATZILCHA UB’SHEVA LO YIGA BACH,” “Isaac saw in his vision that his children will be in GALUT, therefore he called in Jacob and blessed him with special blessings for the GALUT. What were the blessings? That God will save you from six afflictions and the seventh will not even reach up to trouble you.”

As mortals, we are unable to foresee the ways of God. Joseph suffers in an Egyptian prison and doesn’t know that it will lead him on the way to becoming a great leader in Egypt. Pharoah causes great sufferings to the children of Israel and doesn’t know that this will shorten their sojourn in Egypt, and that he and his people will dearly pay for that suffering. “ES TZARH HI L’YAAKOV U’MIMENAH YIVASHEA” (Jeremiah 9), “Out of Jacob’s sufferings comes his salvation.”

The GALUT MITZRAYIM, Egyptian exile, brought the children of Israel closer to each other. In their own country there was envy and hatred between the children of Jacob, but as soon as they came to Egypt we find “VAYAKER YOSEPH ES ECHAV V’HEM LO HIKIRUHU,” “Joseph recognizes his brethren but they did not recognize him.” Rashi comments: “K’SHENIMSRU L’YODO HIKIR SHEM ECHOV V’RICHAM ALEHEM V’HEM, LO HIKIRUHU, K’SHENAFAL B’YADAM.” “When Joseph’s brothers came to Egypt, Joseph forgot all that they had done to him and he had pity on them and showed them mercy. But in their homeland, when Joseph fell into their hands, they did not show any love for him.” — Joseph felt that in GALUT, they must be perfectly united against the common enemy. That was one of the blessings that the GALUT brought to us. Our ACHDUT, closeness to one another, the unifying of our forces for the common cause, is the envy of the world.

When Jacob left his father’s house on his way to exile, his father, who was known to be a prophet, did not console him with what he, Isaac, could foresee, that Jacob’s son Joseph would be an interpreter of Pharoah’s dreams, and that he would be second to the king of Egypt. No! These were not considered as blessings either by Isaac or by Jacob. Such favours extended to our people were always detrimental to our salvation. Joseph, also, was not too proud of his position. He soon learned that Egypt was using him so long as it could benefit from his leadership. “HANIMTZA KAZEH ISH ASHER RUACH ELOKIM BO. AMAR LAHEM SHELO YIMTZU MITZRI KOMOHU” (Rambam). Pharaoh said to the Egyptian officers: “You can not find among the Egyptians anyone who has the spirit of God in him as has Joseph.” Even Pharaoh’s CHARTUMIM, magicians, could not interpret his dreams, but Joseph did. The same Joseph who on every turn could hear the Egyptians ridiculing him with the words “NAAR,” boy, “EVED,” slave, “IVRI,” Hebrew. But when they needed him to relieve them from the famine, to put their country on a strong economic basis, they put him up next to their king. — Such has been the fate of our people through our long GALUT. Not only did we interpret the dreams of kings and all sorts of leaders in the world, but we also brought culture and great wealth to them. And as soon as we were no longer needed, we were persecuted and driven out. The butlers and the bakers and many other petty officials of Pharaoh whose dreams Joseph interpreted held on to their positions in Egypt, but Joseph in a very short time was forgotten and his children soon enslaved.

We suffered more than any other generation. One third of our best people were destroyed by the worst beasts in the history of the world. But we in our generation had the privilege of seeing the beginning of the rebuilding of our homeland, where our Josephs can use their ingenuities for our own benefits. With God’s help, in the very few years of existence of the state of Israel, our people have accomplished miracles. This rebirth of our country was not an unexpected happening. The Jew, as one of the principles of his faith, believes in the coming of the Messiah to take the children of Israel out of the GALUT. The dispersion of Israel among the nations of the earth was a necessary element in the scheme of Divine workings, in order to carry the principles and moral teachings of the Torah to the four corners of the world. But, just as in Egypt, when it became evident that to keep the children of Israel there any longer was too dangerous and they were taken out of that country, so too, this generation had suffered so much in our long GALUT, that the prophecy of Hosea had to come true, “And the children of Judah and the children of Israel shall be gathered together” (Hosea I - 11).

Although Israel is surrounded on all sides by enemies who are set to destroy it, and even the great powers are not too friendly to it, with the help of God, that country will be safe. The rebirth of the State of Israel is only an “HASCHALTA D’GEULAH,” a beginning of our people’s redemption during the final stage of which there will be a total redemption of the whole world. A change of thought and action by the nations will take place. Since the dawn of history nations attempted to become great by enslaving other people, but this was never lasting. Pharaoh of Egypt was one of the very few whose dreams and thoughts were to strengthen his power from within his own land. In today’s sidrah we find that Pharaoh dreamt. “Behold, there came up out of the river seven kine, well-favoured and fat-fleshed; and they fed in the reedgrass, and behold seven other kine came up after them out of the river, ill favoured and lean fleshed, and stood by the other kine upon the brink of the river, and the ill favoured and lean fleshed kine did eat up the seven well favoured and well fleshed kine.” But when Pharaoh told the dream to Joseph, he added: “And when they had eaten them up, it could not be known that they had eaten them, but they were still ill favoured as at the beginning.” Yes! Pharaoh of old learned from experience that one cannot get fat by swallowing up other peoples, that subduing and enslaving other nations will not make him permanently strong. Pharaoh searched for a wise and capable man to help him strengthen his country from within, and he found that man in Joseph.

In the dream of Pharaoh, he saw the fat kine feeding in the reedgrass. The Hebrew word for reedgrass is “ACHU,” that comes from the word, “ACH,” brother. This was to indicate that the land where the fat kine were feeding was the same as where the ill favoured kine were feeding. The difference was in their behavior. The kine that lived brotherly-close, united with each other, benefited. The others, who fought among themselves, even after totally engulfing their rivals, did not change their evil appearance.

Sooner or later, with God’s help, the nations of the world will come to the realization that only through cooperation with other peoples will all of us benefit from the blessings of God to humanity.
