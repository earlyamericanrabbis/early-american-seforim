The drama of “YETZIAT MITZRAIM,” the departure from Egypt, comes to a close in today’s parshah. But the drama of the ever-changing human character takes up a great part of the parshah.

The B’nai Israel have already crossed the Red Sea that separates them from the Egyptian influence and they are now treading on the free sand of the desert on their way to the Promised Land. On one occasion we find the B’nai Israel celebrating and singing together with Moses when they behold the great miracles at the Red Sea. The Torah tells us that they believed in God and in his servant Moses. But not long after, we find them complaining bitterly. When they noticed that they were travelling through the desert where very little water was found, though they still had water in their utensils, they already began murmuring: “Wherefore hast thou brought us out of Egypt, to kill us and our children and our cattle with thirst?” (Exodus XVII:3).

One would expect that a people who had just departed from a land that caused them tremendous sufferings, hardened to the most inhuman conditions, would now love and enjoy their freedom and independence, that no hardship in the world would disturb them. Certainly, these complaints were not called for.

“VAYOVOU MOROTO V’LO YOCHLU LISHTOT MAYIM MIMARAH KI MARIM HEM, VAYOREHU HASHEM ETZ VAYASHLECH EL HAMAIM VAYIMTKU HAMAIM, SHAM SAM CHOK UMISHPOT V’SHAM NISAU,” (Exodus XV:23-25). “And when they came to Marah, they could not drink of the waters of Marah for they were bitter. Therefore the name of it was called Marah (that is bitterness) etc. And the Lord showed him a tree and he cast it into the waters, and the waters were made sweet. There He made for them a statute, and an ordinance, and there He proved them.” “AMAR RABBI LEVI: U’MAH KI MARIM HEM? HADOR HAYAH MAR B’MAASAV.” Says Rabbi Levi, “What does it mean that they were bitter? It does not refer to the water but to the people of that generation.” Yes! The people were not content, therefore everything tasted bad to them, and nothing suited them. Whatever you do for a sick man hurts him. The sufferings in Egypt did condition them against physical and mental mistreatments, but in Egypt they did not have to worry about food, water, and shelter. All these minimum necessities were provided for them by their Egyptian masters. For the first time, here in the desert, they were faced with the problem of providing for themselves. They did not cease complaining until they were assured by Moses that God promised He would bring water out of the flint rock and bread out of the heavens. Normally, man has the faculties and ability to cope with any situation which may face him. Man should never expect things to adjust to his whim and fancy. “EN SOMCHIN AL HANES,” “We must not depend on miracles.” Man must learn to adjust to the unexpected however disappointing it may be.

Most of the conditions and situations, good or bad, man creates for himself. “ADAM CHOFSHI B’DIMYONO OSUR B’MUSKALO, DIMYONO MOLICHO SHOVOV B’DERECH LIBO”; “Man is free in his imagination, but restricted, limited in his thoughts.” His imagination leads him wildly and uncontrolled down the path of his heart’s desires. If he would only use his mind to control his imaginative power, man would be the most happy creature in the world. Unfortunately, some people want to live life as they imagine it, and as life is not always a bed of roses, they encounter one disappointment after another. Instead of blaming themselves for not facing reality, they blame all about them.

The B’nai Israel, after so many heavenly signs and wonders on their behalf to get them out of Egypt, imagined far different conditions under freedom and now were imagining that their life in Egypt had been more secure than their desert existence, and voiced much resentment at their departure.

The “DOR HAMIDBOR,” that generation who departed from Egypt, was not privileged to enter the Promised Land. One must come to the Promised Land with joy and with love. The land of Israel was given by God to his chosen people, and by finding fault with the land we criticize God’s choice.

At the very beginning of the sidrah the Torah tells us: “VLO NACHAM ELOKIM DERECH ERETZ PLISHTIM KI KAROV HU, KI OMAR ELOKIM PEN YINACHEM HAAM BIROTAM MILCHAMAH V’SHUVU MITZRAYMAH” (Exodus XIII:17), that God led them not by the way of the land of the Philistines, although that was near, for God said: “lest peradventure the people repent when they see war and they return to Egypt.” The words in the text “BIROTAM MILCHAMAH” do not necessarily mean “when they see war.” The meaning may well be that the people would build up in their imagination things that are not there, for if the intent had been to state that war would befall them, then the word in the text ought to be “TIKRENAH.” The interpretation is that they would imagine all sorts of troubles and would finally fear fear itself. — Instead of gaining from their newly acquired independence a strong willpower to face any kind of situation and solve any problem independently, through imagining the worst they were even destroying their inborn instinct of self-preservation.

The Torah tells us that God taught Moses a remedy for such a state of mind. “VAYOREHU HASHEM ETZ VAYASHLECH EL HAMAYIM VAYIMTIKU HAMAYIM SHAM SAM CHOK UMISHPAT V’SHAM NISAHU.” “And the Lord showed him a tree, and he cast it into the waters and the waters were made sweet. There He made for them a statute and an ordinance, and there He proved them.” The word “VAYOREHU” literally means “He taught him.” God taught Moses the importance to the freed people of laws and regulations to govern life. “SHAM NISAHU,” there was the testing ground where it was discovered and proved that with faith alone, without a Torah to live by, the Israelites would never satisfy their spiritual longings; that the observance of the “MITZVOT” sweetened the disposition and made clear minds with which to look around at life which was lived, then, with full trust in God. It is well known that the observant Jew has a much healthier disposition than the non-observant. The former practices and lives his faith so his religion and belief in God becomes a part of him. Even in a moment of great danger he will say, “God is with me, I will fear no evil”; but the latter can be veered off his course by the slightest of winds.

This sidrah ends with the war of Amalek. The Torah tells us: “V’HAYAH KAASHER YARIM MOSHE ET YADO V’GAVAR ISRAEL V’CHAASHER YANIACH YADO V’GAVAR AMALEK” (Exodus XVII:11). “And it came to pass, when Moses held up his hand, that Israel prevailed, and when he let down his hand, Amalek prevailed.” Moses’ hands raised heavenward served to direct the hearts of the Israelites towards God as the ally who could assure them of victory (Rashi).

To continue further, three times we find in the Torah that the B’nai Israel came into the desert and each time their thoughts were directed to a want. First: “And they went out into the desert of Shur; and they went three days in the desert, and found no water” (Exodus XV:22). Second: “And they journeyed from Elim, and all the congregation of the children of Israel came unto the desert of Sin, etc. and the whole congregation of the children of Israel murmured against Moses and Aaron in the desert; and the children of Israel said unto them etc., for ye have brought us forth into the desert, to put to death this whole assembly by famine” (Exodus XVI:1-3). Third: “And all the congregation of the children of Israel journeyed from the desert of Sin, after their journeys, according to the Commandment of the Eternal, and encamped in Refidim; and there was no water for the people to drink. Wherefore the people did quarrel with Moses, and said, give us water that we may drink” (Exodus XVII:1-2). But one time we find that they came into the desert of Sinai and had no complaints. “For they journeyed from Refidim, and came to the desert of Sinai, and encamped in the desert, and there Israel encamped before the mountain.” The text uses the singular form “VAYICHAN” instead of “VAYACHNU,” “and they encamped.” That is to say, they encamped as one man and with one mind (Mechiltah, Exodus XIX:2). Here, in this place called Sinai, they were about to receive the Torah that regulates life, that gives meaning to life, that fills in all the voids one faces in life. It is as the words of the psalmist, who when speaking of the righteous people, says: “But his delight is in the law of the Lord; and in his law does he meditate day and night. And he shall be like a tree planted by the streams of water, that bringeth forth its fruit in its season, and whose leaf does not wither. And in whatsoever he doeth he shall prosper. Not so the wicked; but they are like the chaff which the wind driveth away.”
