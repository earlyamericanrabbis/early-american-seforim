Today’s two parshayot bring to a close the drama of the forty years wandering of the children of Israel in the desert.

When we study the drama in its entirety, from the time the curtain opened up on the departure from Egypt to the closing of the curtain as they were standing at the gates of the Promised Land, we find it to be a combination of happy and joyous experiences as well as sorrows and total disappointments. Today, as when we contemplate the happenings of those days and we ponder upon the different characters who made up this great drama, our feelings are varied.

The prophet Jeremiah (Chapter II:2) praises the “DOR HAMIDBAR,” the generation of the desert: “Go and cry out in the ears of Jerusalem, saying: Thus saith the Lord: I remember for thee the affection of thy youth, the love of thine espousals, how thou wentest after Me in the wilderness, in a land that was not sown.” — After all, a people whose entire foundation as a nation was organized in bondage under compulsion, managed to develop themselves into a “MAMLECHET KOHANIM V’GOI KADOSH,” “into a kingdom of priests and a holy nation.” In other words, even when they wandered from place to place in the hot sun of the desert and many times suffered hunger and thirst, they managed to educate themselves to realize and understand the outstanding values while adjusting their lives in accordance with the teachings of the Torah. To the amazement of their contemporaries and the many generations since that time, the children of Israel were the prototypes for those who accepted the moral code of Sinai. At the same time we find them to be quite rebellious. They rejected God’s choice of the land of Canaan as their land. As punishment, the whole generation was to die out in the desert and never be privileged to see the land. Even Moses was permitted merely to behold it, and only Joshua and Kalev were privileged to enter the land.

Now, as they are standing at the gates of their promised land, scripture reiterates all the locations in the desert that they passed during their long journey. The naming of each station “VAYISU VAYACHNU,” “and they journeyed and encamped,” served as a reminder to them of all that had occurred during their travels. Rashi quotes R. Tanchuma explaining it with a parable: It may be compared to the case of a king whose son was ill and whom he took to a distant place for a cure. When they returned home the father began to enumerate all the stages, saying to him “here we slept, here we caught cold, here you had this headache, etc.” (Numbers XXXIII:1).

History repeats itself and there is very much that we can learn from historical background. We too experienced a “VAYISU VAYACHNU.” For almost two thousand years we were scattered to all the corners of the world, in the “MIDBAR HOAMIM,” “wilderness of the nations.” Our “VAYISU” and “VAYACHNU” also contained a mixture of happy and joyous periods, as well as sad and very disappointing experiences. We too, managed to become spiritual giants even at times when the odds were against us. We completed the greatest lore of all times, the Talmud, at the station of Babylon. During the dark middle ages, we produced numberless men of culture whose wisdom still illuminates many perplexing questions of today.

As in those days, when the children of Reuben and the children of Gad asked of Moses to be allowed to remain east of the Jordan, and Moses granted their request provided they would help in the war of liberation on the west side of the Jordan, so today there is a similar situation. We too, at a time when the footsteps of the “MOSHIACH” can be heard, have a great number of our people who desire to remain in their adopted lands in Galut. And paralleling the children of Reuben and of Gad, these same people of today are displaying great courage in helping in the liberation and upbuilding of the land of their fathers.

The experiences of those days serve us as a perfect guide for the problems that we face today. The Torah tells us, during the war with Midian, of the cleansing of the utensils from the forbidden food which they had absorbed. Vessels used for cooking hot water were to be purified by means of hot water. Those used for roasting in fire must be cleansed by fire, and others used for cold foodstuffs had to be scoured with cold water (Rashi, Numbers XXXI:23). The Rambam says: “Why was not such a procedure ordered after the campaign against the Amorite kings, Sichon and Og, though there too the Israelites had taken the spoil of the cities (Deut. II:35, III:7)? Because the territory of these kings was part of the Holy Land, and whatever was found in it, even forbidden things, was permitted for use.”

The Torah so ordered, that in the war of liberating the Holy Land, their promised land, the warriors should not have the slightest discouragement. The land of Israel was the choice of God for the people of Israel, and even the slightest doubt as to the wisdom of that choice defects our faith. During the war, the major restrictions of the Torah covering forbidden food was withdrawn and any food was permitted (as long as there was not available the same in kosher food). In our day, too, there is a parallel. The forerunners of our forces of liberation were the “CHALUTZIM.” They came at a time when they had little choice in the matter of food, for there was very scant fare in this land which had lain desolate for almost two thousand years. With little nourishment in their stomachs but with the greatest of enthusiasm, they risked their lives for the re-establishment and liberation of the land. Today, we are faced with the problem of re-educating them to live a life of Torah and MITZVOT. However, we must be careful not to diminish their enthusiasm, nor belittle their contribution to the Holy Land through criticism. We must approach the problem with a great amount of love and understanding, and only then will we win them back into our fold.

In another part of our Parshah, scripture tells us: “And the Lord spoke unto Moses saying: Speak unto the children of Israel and say unto them: When ye pass over the Jordan into the Land of Canaan. Then ye shall appoint you cities to be cities of refuge for you, that the manslayer that killed any person through error may flee thither. And the cities shall be unto you for refuge from avengers, that the manslayer die not, until he stands before the congregation for judgment. Ye shall make three cities beyond the Jordan, and three cities in the Land of Canaan” (Numbers XXXV:9-34). Rashi quoting (Makkot 9b) says: “The three cities on the east side of the Jordan did not function until the three on the western side of the Jordan were appointed.”

Yes! The countries of refuge that we used through all the centuries of our being in exile, proved to be of very little avail to us. Sooner or later, we were expelled and in many places we were almost annihilated. As long as we did not have an address of our own, we were outcasts. With the establishment of Medinat Israel, where any Jew from any place in the world is unquestionably accepted, many countries opened wide their doors for us. The Jew, wherever he is now, feels more proud that he is a Jew than at any time in the past two thousand years. He cannot avoid hearing the great chorus of praise for this little country called Israel. The Jewish heart wherever it is found, melts with joy when the Jew sees, reads or hears about the miraculous accomplishments this small country has shown in the short time of its existence.

“AD SHENICHNESU L’ARETZ HUCHSHERU KOL HOAROTZOT LOMAR SHIRAH, U’MESHENICHNESU, LO HUCHSHERAH ELA ERETZ ISRAEL BILVAD.” “Before the children of Israel entered the land, of every other country could they sing. But after they entered into the Promised Land, only of that land may they sing” (Sages).

Every country has its songs expressing joy and pride in its accomplishments as an organized nation. We too joined in singing these songs in the countries where we lived. We were a great factor in their developments. We gave to every country of our refuge all our genius and energy. All our inventions, all our innovations were considered a product of and bore the labels of the lands in which we were found at that time. Yes, we helped them to sing their songs. But the song of God, which is ours, we could not sing on alien soil. As the psalmist said: “How shall we sing the Lord’s song in a foreign land?” (Psalm CXXXVII:4). But now, when with the help of God, our people have reclaimed their homeland, we are told to sing our own song. We must see to it that our great genius shall produce to the credit of Israel. For then and only then will we receive the full respect for our endeavors and accomplishments and we may again become the light of the nations, which prophecy is seemingly being fulfilled.
