“Judges and officers shalt thou make thee in all thy gates, which the Lord thy God giveth thee, tribe by tribe, and they shall judge the people with righteous judgment” (Deuteronomy 16:18).

Until this time, the dispensation of justice as well as the entire government had been handled by Moses himself and those he chose to help him. In Exodus 18:25-26 we are told: “And Moses chose able men out of Israel, and made them heads over the people, rulers of thousands, rulers of hundreds, rulers of fifties, and rulers of tens, and they judged the people at all seasons, the hard causes they brought unto Moses, but every small matter they judged themselves.”

Now, when they were about to enter their promised land, God told them to establish there a democratic system in all branches of government. — This Sidrah, from the beginning to the end, points to the excellence of function of a democracy when based upon the laws and regulations of the Torah.

The Parshah first calls for the establishment of the justice department. Every community must have their own courts and the judges must be selected from their own people (Rashi). This is well understood, that a judge from the same community knows and understands much better the nature and position of those to be judged and thereby may be able to come up with a more righteous decision. “Do not judge your fellowman until you have been in his position” (AVOT).

The Parshah continues: “When thou art come unto the land which the Lord thy God gives thee, and shall possess it, and shall dwell therein, and shalt say: I will set a king over me, like all the nations that are round about me; thou shalt in any wise set him king over thee, whom the Lord thy God shall choose. One from among thy brethren shalt thou set king over thee . . . And it shall be, when he sitteth upon the throne of his kingdom, that he shall write him a copy of the law in a book out of that which is before the Priests and Levites. And it shall be with him, and he shall read therein all the days of his life, that he may learn to fear the Lord his God, to keep all the words of this law and these statutes to do them. That his heart be not lifted up above his brethren, and that he turn not aside from the commandment to the right, or to the left . . .” (Deuteronomy 17:14-20).

A king reigned among the nations in those days either by subduing his subjects on the battlefield, or by purchasing his position with money. Once he ascended the throne he became the absolute ruler and even a divine being with no restrictions upon him whatsoever.

The Hebrew king had to be chosen by the people from the people and for the people. He was by no means on the receiving end. He was not permitted to amass a great deal of wealth or to have many wives. He was not permitted to use his position for personal gain or pleasures. He was selected by the people to serve them to the best of his ability, with the warning “that his heart be not lifted up above his brethren.” The Torah demands of every man that he write for himself the Torah (Sages based on Deuteronomy 31:19), but of the king is asked: “to have two scrolls, one to deposit in his treasury and the other to be always with him wherever he goes” (Rashi). In other words, the scroll that directs his ways when he appears among people, a copy of the same must remain treasured in his very heart. Our sages say that he must be “TOCHO KEBARO,” “within his inner-self the same as he appears on the surface.”

The leaders of the people of Israel were ordered by the Torah to carry full responsibility for their people. In the law pertaining to the “Cities of Refuge,” the Torah commanded “to operate three cities in the midst of the land, that in the case of a manslayer whoso killeth his neighbor unawares, and hated him not in time past; he shall flee unto one of these cities and live, lest the avenger of blood pursue the manslayer, while his heart is hot and overtake him, because the way is long, and smite him mortally; whereas he was not deserving of death, insomuch as he hated him not in time past. And if the Lord God will enlarge the borders of Israel, three more cities shall be added. That innocent blood be not shed in the midst of the land which the Lord God has given to Israel” (Deuteronomy 19:1-14). In Numbers 35:9-34, we are told that the manslayer must remain in the city of refuge until the death of the High Priest; but after the death of the High Priest the manslayer may return into the land of his possession. Rashi quoting Makkoth 11a, says: “The exiled man gains freedom through the death of the High Priest, because the latter should have prevented this calamity in Israel by virtue of prayer. It is therefore only his death that expiates the sin of the homicide.”

At the end of this Sidrah we are commanded: “If one is found slain, lying in the field, and it be not known who has smitten him. Then the elders and the judges shall come forth and they shall measure unto the cities which are round about him that is slain. And it shall be that the city which is nearest unto the slain man, even the elders of the city shall take a heifer of the herd, which has not been wrought with, and which hath not drawn in the yoke. And the elders of the city shall bring down the heifer unto a rough valley . . . which may neither be plowed nor sown, and shall break the heifer’s neck there in the valley . . . And all the elders of that city, who are nearest unto the slain man, shall wash their hands over the heifer . . . And they shall say: Our hands have not shed this blood, neither have our eyes seen it. Forgive, O Lord, Thy people Israel . . . And suffer not innocent blood to remain in the midst of Thy people of Israel. And the blood shall be forgiven them” (Deuteronomy 21:1-9).

In summary, the whole Sidrah serves as an historical document of the democratic form of government the people of Israel were ordered to establish as soon as they entered their promised land. The leadership, including the king, being selected by the people, usually reflects the character of the people. As in Isaiah I.4-23, “Woe! Sinful nation, people laden with iniquity . . . Thy princes are rebels, and companions of thieves, every one loves bribes, and runneth after rewards . . .” Yes! The prophet Isaiah sees in the leaders a reflection of the people’s misdeeds. It was demanded of the leaders of Israel to lead exemplary lives not only in public, but also in their personal lives lest they be a bad mirror of the people. They were responsible when a fellowman was killed even through an unpremeditated action, as illustrated in the case of a man who chopped down a tree in the forest unaware that someone was passing behind him. During the chopping the axe flew off the handle, killing the passerby. The leadership was accountable because it had not enacted laws and regulations in places of work to safeguard all from injury. Therefore the innocent manslayer had to remain in the city of refuge till the death of the High Priest, who in his position was answerable for the innocent blood shed. And in the case of the man found slain and there was no knowledge of who committed the crime, the elders and leaders again were liable. The Torah prescribes a ceremonial that besides being a good publicizing medium through which the guilty might be found, also points out to the heads of the community where they failed in their duties. — The heifer, “EGLAH ARUFAH,” used in the ceremony had to be one “which hath not been wrought with, and which hath not drawn in the yoke.” Also, the ritual had to take place in a rough valley which had been neither plowed nor sown. In other words, the leadership failed in its duty to create work for everyone, and idleness is the greatest cause of crime. Juvenile delinquency can in great measure be blamed on the government. When unemployed youths are put to work, crime diminishes. The ceremony of the “EGLAH ARUFAH” was an excellent reminder of that fact.

“Judges and officers shalt thou make thee . . . and they shall judge the people with righteous judgment.” With the selection of ethical judges and honest heads of the government, the nation will be looked upon as also being righteous. The leaders of the people, in their behavior, reflect upon the people for good and for bad.
