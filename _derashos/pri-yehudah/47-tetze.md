The way of life set up by the teachings of the Torah entails everything and anything that man encounters during the years allotted to him. The commandments of the Torah direct man how to live and how to behave under any kind of circumstances.

This Sidrah in particular contains more commandments than any other Sidrah in the entire five Books of Moses. Although they are diversified and seemingly have no connection one with the other, the very fact that they are grouped into one Sidrah proves that there is a correlation between the various commandments, and that itself carries an important message to us.

As the Torah is “not in heaven” “LO BASHAMAIM HE” (Sages), therefore the greatest part of it deals with the earthly problems as they pertain to man’s well being. The Torah knows of man’s frailties but considers them an important ingredient of his make-up. Man’s ambition to attain higher and ever higher goals has brought on the tremendous progress we behold today. And this ambition has its roots in the restlessness caused by the contrasting and varied desires in mortals. The “YETZER TOV” and the “YETZER HARAH,” the inclination to do good and the one to do evil, are both for man’s welfare. Each, when controlled and used in the proper direction and proportion, helps make the “ADAM HASHALEM,” the perfect man. “BARATI LO YETZER HARAH, NATATI LO TORAH TAVLIN” (Sages), “I created in him (man) the evil inclination, therefore I gave him the Torah as a remedy to help him.” The Torah and its commandments direct in the proper use of these tendencies. “And thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might” (Deuteronomy 6:5). The Hebrew word for “heart” as it is used in the text is “LEVAV” in the plural, instead of “LEV” in singular, to indicate that God has to be loved with both the good and evil inclinations (Rashi).

Today’s Parshah begins with “When thou goest forth to battle against thine enemies, and the Lord thy God delivereth them into thy hands, and thou carriest them away captive . . .” This certainly does not mean an unprovoked attack on the enemies. Such is not envisaged by the Torah. It speaks of war against those who have shown themselves to be enemies in their designs to conquer (SIFRI). Not only does the Torah point to and give solutions to the social and personal problems caused by war, but from the words “when thou goest forth” used in the text, we also learn that it provides some strategy for conducting a successful campaign. Israel has always been a small country and could not survive in a defensive war. Therefore, instead of saying “KI TILCHOM,” “when thou makest war,” the text declares “KI TETZE LAMILCHAMAH,” “when thou goest forth to battle,” advising thereby not to wait for an attack by the provoking enemies, for that would be fatal for a land so little that there is no place to withdraw for regrouping of forces. Its only chance for survival is in offensive tactics, to carry the battle into the land of the foe.

The Parshah then discusses one of the problems brought on by war, a problem which in the outset was only personal in nature but soon became a social dilemma. “He sees among those in captivity a woman of beauty and desires for her . . .” The Torah suggests that he should take her to wife. — Scripture here makes concession to human weakness, knowing that in the heat of conquest man will be unable to control his passion, therefore the Torah prescribes either marriage or freedom for the captive (Kiddushin 21b). This marriage does not carry with it God’s blessing. Rashi comments on the words of the text: “‘And it shall be if thou have no delight in her . . .’ denotes that in the end he will hate her.” After all, she is mourning for parents and other members of her family, how then could she have true love for her captor? Such a union can hardly produce a good family life and children with normal behavior. Then the Parshah warns not to differentiate between the children of the wife who is beloved and those of the one he hates. If his first-born son is of the wife who is disliked, still the law of the first-born applies, and that son is to receive a double portion when inheriting his father’s possessions. — Then the text continues to show the results of such an alliance. “If a man have a stubborn and rebellious son, that will not harken to the voice of his father, or his mother, and though they chasten him will not harken unto them . . . Then shall his father and his mother lay hold on him, and bring him out unto the elders of the city . . .”

Today, with all the advances in the field of psychology, we are still groping in the dark about whom to blame for the increase in juvenile delinquency. Many law enforcement agencies like to blame the parents for neglecting the proper upbringing of their children. The Torah does not always share this opinion. In the case of the rebellious son, the Torah points out that not only was he “SORER,” rebellious, but also “UMOREH,” literally, a teacher. A rebellious son was indeed a parental problem that developed from abnormal conditions in the home. A marriage union based on passions created by war could not result in children of whom much could be expected. But when one of such offspring grows up and becomes a menace not only to his parents, but to society as well, when he becomes a “MOREH,” a teacher, and his evil sways others, then it is the duty of the parents to bring him to the proper authorities and permit their assistance in coping with the problem. Parents are at fault when they know their son is spreading his evil and they try to hide the truth. But parents of children led astray by such wickedness as that spread by the “SORER U’MOREH” are not to be blamed since no child can be locked in an ivory tower to protect him from outside forces. Then it is the duty of society to rid itself of bad influences and the wrongdoers. The Torah mentions the results brought on by concealment of the culprit by the parents. In the next lines the Torah says: “And if a man have committed a sin worthy of death, and he be put to death, and thou hang him on a tree. His body shall not remain all night upon the tree, but thou shalt surely bury him the same day; for he that hangs is a reproach unto God . . .” “The degradation of the human body is an affront to God in Whose Image man is made” (Rashi). Rashi also comments: “The connection between the last lines of the text with the text of the rebellious son is to point out that if the parents will try to hide the true facts about their son through a feeling of mercy for him, they may expect very grave results.”

The same Parshah that up till now dealt with war and taught us not to be merciful even to one’s own child when he becomes a menace to society, now introduces laws in which we are told to be merciful even to dumb creatures. “If a bird’s nest chance to be before thee in the way, in any tree or on the ground, with young ones or eggs, and the dam sitting upon the young, or the eggs, thou shalt not take the dam with the young, thou shalt in any wise let the dam go, but the young thou mayest take unto thyself that it may be well with thee and that thou mayest prolong thy days.” “The reason for this law is to implant in man the virtue of mercy” (N).

“Thou shalt not plow with an ox and an ass together” (Deuteronomy 22:10). This law applies to any two different kinds of animals, and not only for ploughing but also for carrying a load (Rashi). The principle involved is the prevention of cruelty, since the ass which is weaker than the ox would suffer in such a combination (Abraham Ibn Ezra).

“When a man takes a new wife, he shall not go out in the host, neither shall he be charged with any business; he shall be free (from all services imposed by the state) for one year, and shall cheer his wife whom he hath taken” (Deuteronomy 24:5).

“Thou shalt not muzzle the ox when he treadeth out the corn” (Deuteronomy 25:4). This law applies to any animal that is engaged in work in connection with food (Rashi).

“If brethren dwell together, and one of them die, and have no child, the wife of the dead shall not be married abroad unto one not of his kin; her husband’s brother shall go in unto her, and take her to him for a wife, and perform the duty of a husband’s brother unto her. And it shall be, that the first-born that she beareth shall succeed in the name of his brother that is dead, that his name be not blotted out of Israel” (Deuteronomy 25:5-10). By this marriage he receives the deceased brother’s share of their father’s estate (Rashi). The principle of this law is also an act of pity to a dying man with no children. At least he is assured that his possessions (including his beloved wife) will not be transferred to a total stranger.

At the end of this Sidrah we are told: “Remember what Amalek did unto thee by the way ye came forth out of Egypt” (Deuteronomy 25:17-19). The history of Amalek’s deed should be passed on from one generation to the next, for he was guilty of an unprovoked attack (Rashi and Nachmanides).

In summary, the Sidrah begins with war and deals with the side effects of war and ends with war. The Torah realized the evil inclination in man, and that there would always be ambitious people who would provoke wars. Therefore it even advised some good strategy. It also pointed out the necessity of being prepared to face attacks, referring to that of Amalek which took Israel by surprise. But the Torah was also aware of the disturbances war brings to normal human behavior, which disturbances permit wicked tendencies to get the upper hand. A nation triumphant in war finds its young men and women returning from the battle field in a grave state of confusion and it takes years for the process of rehabilitation to return them to normalcy. Therefore all the other portions of this Sidrah deal with acts of mercy which serve as appropriate lessons in order to lead the man of war back to a life of peace, that is, the middle of the road, the “SHVIL HAZAHAV,” the golden path.
