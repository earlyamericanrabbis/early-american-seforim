In today’s sidrah we are told that Moses, after being asked many times by God to accept the mission of taking the B’NAI ISRAEL out of Egypt, has finally accepted it. We find Moses asking many questions, first pertaining to his own unfitness for such a mission and second, the unworthiness of the B’NAI ISRAEL of redemption (Exodus III, 11).

Moses knew of the promise God made to Jacob on his way to Egypt: “I will go down with you unto Egypt and I will bring you up from there,” (Genesis 46.4). The word for “bring up” used here is “ALOH,” which means to bring up something that does not put up resistance and when the object to be brought up is a living thing, it even helps in the process of coming up. But the word “L’HOTZI” used in connection with the mission given to Moses means “to pull out, as to pull something out of the mud.” Yes! The promise to Jacob was “ALOH,” signifying that the B’NAI ISRAEL would be helping themselves on the way to redemption from Egypt, that they would always feel like strangers in Egypt even under the best of conditions and their aim would be to depart from there. But Moses finds to the contrary, that the B’NAI ISRAEL have adopted the Egyptian way of living, and are not even interested in departure. We also read that God said: “And I will come down to deliver them out of the hand of the Egyptians and to bring them up out of that land,” (Exodus 3.8). The word for “bring them up” used here is “UL’ALOTO,” from the word “ALOH,” but when God gives the mission to Moses, the word “L’HOTZI” is used. Also in verse 9, God said to Moses: “Now therefore behold the cry of the children of Israel is come unto Me, etc.” The word for “cry” used here is “TZAAKAT” which has the same meaning as “ZAAKAT SDOM V’AMORAH,” referring to the evil doings of Sodom and Gomorrah (Genesis XVIII). Moses quickly learns that the people, instead of being united by their sufferings to a common cause for redemption, quarrel with each other. It was not a cry of pain, of suffering, that rose to the heavens, but a cry of jealousy and hatred. Such was the degrading state of living into which they had fallen. And Moses asked: “What merits have the B’NAI ISRAEL that they should go forth from Egypt?” What good would it do them if they carried the Egyptian way of life into their promised land? God answered him: “I have a great purpose in this, My bringing them forth, for they are destined to accept the Torah upon this mountain at the expiration of three months after they leave Egypt,” (Rashi - Exodus III.12). If they could only shake off the sand of the GALUT, they would be ready to accept the moral code, the Torah. As we are told by our sages, referring to the words “and God saw the children of Israel, and God knew,” (Exodus II.25) that God saw that the Israelites would say of the molten calf, “This is your god, O Israel,” but God also knew that they would say: “All that the Eternal has spoken we will do and hearken,” that they would perform God’s commandments without any questions.

Moses did not realize that the Israelites, contrary to surface appearances, still had in them a great spark for a higher mode of life inherited from their forefathers; that it was only necessary to awaken that spark for a complete change to occur. “And they will hearken to thy voice,” — as soon as the words “POKOD YIFKOD” would be uttered, they would listen, for from the days of Jacob and Joseph it was known to them that these words would usher in the deliverance (Rashi - Exodus III.16-18). What is in that phrase that will bring about the movement for redemption? In the double use of the same expression “POKOD” and “YIFKOD,” “God will visit you,” “POKOD” may refer to the deliverance of the body and “YIFKOD” to the deliverance of the soul. The B’NAI ISRAEL inherited from the patriarchs the belief in One God and trust in His unlimited power, and because of this they upheld the commandments given by God to the Patriarchs and later in the Torah. These heavenly-descended ideals are not limited to the development of our spirit alone, but help also in the perfection of the body. In the same way that the soul of man has to have a body wherein to transcend itself, so too must the materialistic requirements of man be combined with his spiritual values and ideas. By the same token, material achievements alone will never satisfy our people. “That man does not live by bread alone, but by whatever the Mouth of the Eternal bringeth forth does man live,” (Deuteronomy VIII 3). Such was the promise of God to Jacob: “I will go down with thee into Egypt and I will also come up,” (literally translated) (Genesis XLVI.4). That is to say, both the redemption of the body and the redemption of the soul will take place.

Moses could not perceive that the Israelites, after being enslaved in Egypt for such a long time, would still have the belief and trust in the God of their fathers, as it is said: “V’AMRU LI MAH SH’MO MAH OMAR ALEHEM,” “And they will say what is His Name, what shall I tell them?” And God said unto Moses, “I am what I am.” And He said, “Thus shalt thou say unto the children of Israel, the Eternal, the God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, has sent me unto you. This is My Name forever, and this is My Memorial unto all generations,” (Exodus III-13). The words in the Hebrew texts for “I am what I am,” are “EHYEH ASHER EHYEH,” and in a literary Hebrew translation may mean “EHYEH LACHEM KASHER EHYEH ETZLECHEM,” “I will be to you as much as you think of Me.” As much as man believes in and trusts what God can do, so much can he expect God to do for him. Under bodily enslavement the imaginative power of man is limited. A slave can hardly think of becoming a king. He may only hope for an improvement in his life. “B’MIDAH SHADAM MODED MODDIM LO.” “Whatever is man’s measure or standard in God’s ability to do, so much can he expect God will do for him.” — The B’NAI ISRAEL in Egypt, though in general appearance not much different from the people about them, still carried within themselves the ingredients of royalty, “B’NAI M’LOCHIM,” children of kings, of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

“And the Eternal said: I have surely seen the affliction of My people who are in Egypt etc.,” (Exodus 3:7). The words “I have surely seen” in the text are “RAOH RAITI” and the sages ask why the double expression. And they answer that God said: “You, Moses, see only one thing, their outward appearance. But I see two things. I also see that deep in their hearts glows a divine spark that will inspire them to higher standards of values in life.” “KOL MAKOM SHEGALU, GALTAH SH’CHINAH EMAHEM.” “Wherever the Israelites were exiled the Divine Presence went with them,” (Sages). — Their standard of values may have been temporarily lowered when influenced by a strange environment, but as soon as they will be able to shake off the dust of the GALUT, they will rise again to their original height.

What happened with the people of Israel in our time bears testimony that Moses was very wrong in his judgment of his people. The world is still astonished and barely able to believe what it sees — that an exiled persecuted people which was scattered for almost two thousand years over all the world among different nationalities, whose languages it adopted and whose customs made visible impressions, is now rebuilding its own homeland with precision that even centuries-old nations have been unable to achieve. The melting of so many nationalities into one single force, working for the general welfare of the nation is amazing, and the high standard of living developed in the short time of Israel’s existence is the admiration of all the peoples of the world. Educators and leaders from everywhere come to these shores to learn the secret of our success. This they will never find if they look only at outer appearances. On the surface, the various environments under which the people of Israel lived through the centuries have left noticeable marks, but within their hearts and spirits still glows a strong spark for the high standard of values taught by our forefathers. This is the secret factor in our success.

“And God spoke unto Moses . . .” — The text uses “ELOKIM” instead of the usual “HASHEM” as the Name of God, because it signifies the divine attribute of justice. Moses is rebuked for his question, “Lord, wherefore hast Thou dealt ill with the people” (Rashi). The parshah continues: “And I appeared unto Abraham, unto Isaac, and unto Jacob as ‘God Almighty,’ but My Name ‘Hashem’ (that refers to “EHEYEH ASHEER EHEYEH,” “I will be what I will be” \[lit.\]), I made not known to them.” The Torah did not say, “I did not make My Name known,” but “I was not known” (lit.), therefore, God did not reveal Himself to the patriarchs by the name “HASHEM,” which signifies keeping faith, because the promises were not fulfilled in their lifetime (Rashi). But to Moses He did so reveal Himself when He declared, “I am that I am,” since His word was about to be accomplished (Rashbam).

In the last sidrah we delved on the drama with the unhappy theme, that of Moses, the destined leader of his people, having neither understanding of, nor trust in these people. In today’s sidrah we read that when rebuking Moses, God said, “I appeared unto Abraham, unto Isaac, and unto Jacob, etc.” and Rashi comments: “V’ERA EL HAAVOT,” — “And I appeared to the fathers.” What did Rashi add to what has already been said in the text? Whenever we refer to “the fathers” we mean “Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob,” and that is in the text. But Rashi expressed here in one word the difference between the great leader Moses and the Patriarchs. — The Midrash tells us: “B’SHNEI MEKOMOT DIMAH MOSHEH ET ATZMO L’AVRAHAM, OMAR LO HAKADOSH BARUCH HU, AL TITHADER LIFNEI MELECH, U’BIMKOM G’DOLIM AL TAAMOD. AVRAHAM AMAR HINENI L’KEHUNAH HINENI L’MALCHUS, ZACHAH L’KEHUNAH V’ZACHAH L’MALCHUS. MOSHE AMAR HINENI L’KEHUNAH HINENI L’MALCHUS, AMAR LO HAKADOSH BARUCH HU: AL TIKRAV HALOM.” “In two places Moses tried to compare himself to Abraham, but God said to him, “Do not approach here and do not pride yourself in front of kings and do not stand in the places of great people.” Abraham said: “I am ready for the priesthood and for the kingdom.” He was privileged to be a king and a priest. But when Moses said: “I am ready to be a king and a priest,” God said, “Do not approach it.”

The difference between Moses and Abraham Rashi stated in one word, “AVOT,” “Fathers.” Abraham was a father of his people. A parent does not see faults in his children. Abraham never discredited even the lowest of his people. He found virtuous ones even in Sodom and pleaded with God to save the city on account of the few “TZADIKIM,” “righteous people.” Abraham accepted his mission to become an “AV HAMON GOIM,” “the father of nations,” and as a father he blamed himself for failing in his mission when he found some of the community did not live up to his teachings. Instead of being “MATZDIK ET HADIN,” finding fault with his people and upholding their deserved punishments, he pleaded with and beseeched God to save them. — Abraham had confidence and trust in his people. Therefore he became king and priest. A priest is one who has the ability to listen to people confess transgressions against laws and regulations, and to lead these transgressors back to the straight path. The knowledge of their sins or guilt does not cause him to declare the transgressors as unworthy and lost to our people. On the contrary, he blames himself since he, the teacher of his people, has failed. The same is true of a king. He must be as a father to his people. He must know and feel their needs and have full trust in their abilities. Abraham was father and teacher to his people, therefore he was privileged to be a king and a priest. But Moses found fault with them and this was not the make-up of a father, therefore he was never a king or a priest.

Love and trust in God must go hand in hand with love and trust in people. “V’AHAVTA ET HASHEM ELOKECHA” and “V’AHAVTA L’REACHA K’MOCHA,” “Thou shalt love thy God” and “Thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself,” are two MITZVOT depending on each other. If one fails in his love and trust in God, he also fails in his love and trust in men. When he fails in his love and trust in men, he also fails in his love and trust in God. The Torah is divided into MITZVOT SH’BEIN ADAM L’CHAVERO, between man and his fellowman, and into MITZVOT SH’BEIN ADAM L’MAKOM, between man and God. One is dependent on the other and together they make up the Torah.

The psalmist said: (Psalm XXVII:11) “HORENI HASHEM DARKECHA U’N’CHENI B’ORACH MISHOR L’MAAN SHORROY,” “Teach me Thy way, O Lord and lead me in an even path, because of them that lie in wait for me.” S.R.H. explains it: “DERECH,” “road” in the text, refers to the moral way between man and God. “ORACH” on the other hand refers to the ethical path between men and men. What the psalmist means is that the moral way between men and God and the righteousness between men and men must not be separated. A perfect man must be equal in his devotion and trust in both and only then can he pray to God.

God has already told Moses: “And I know that the king of Egypt will not let you go, not even by strong hand. And I will stretch out My hand, and smite the Egyptians with all My wonders which I will do among them; and after that he will let you go” (Exodus III:19-20). Wherefore did Moses complain by saying: “Lord, wherefore hast Thou done so evil to this people? Wherefore is it that Thou has sent me? For since I came to Pharaoh to speak in Thy Name, he has done evil to this people, neither hast Thou delivered Thy people at all” (Exodus V:22-23). God rebuked Moses for that, by saying “And I appeared unto the patriarchs as ‘EL SHADDAI’” (God Almighty, that is, in a limited form of the attributes of God). “SHADDAI” is related to the word “DAI,” “enough,” as it is said: “MI SHEAMAR L’OLOM DAI,” “He is the One Who said to the world that it is enough.” When God created the world, the world continued in its creation ceaselessly, till God stopped it by saying “DAI,” “enough.” From then on, it was given to man to perfect the creation. By doing so, God limited Himself in His powers. But when God told Moses His real name, “EHEYEH ASHER EHEYEH,” “I will be what I will be” (lit.), that may mean God will be to men what men think God can do. Then there is no limit to the power of God, for it depends on what men want God to be.

After the remarks made by God to him, Moses was unique for all time. He was the only prophet with whom God made direct contact. “And no prophet arose again in Israel like Moses, whom God allowed to know Him face to face” (Deuteronomy 34:10). He was raised above the rest of mankind for the greatest of missions as the leader and teacher of the children of Israel.
