“And Moses went and spoke these words unto all Israel. And he said unto them: I am a hundred and twenty years old this day; I can no more go out and come in; and the Lord hath said unto me: thou shalt not go over the Jordan” (Deuteronomy 31:1-2).

Nachmanides comments: “After Moses concluded his exhortations to the whole assembly which already dispersed, he went from tribe to tribe to announce his approaching end, to comfort the people and encourage them to put their faith in his successor Joshua.” Rashi comments on the words “I can no more go out and come in,” “not because of declining strength (cf. 34:7) but for the reason that the leadership is being transferred to Joshua.”

Very few men would accept so graciously a decree to relinquish leadership as Moses did. Once a man has tasted the power of being head of a people, he will not willingly give it up even in his declining years. It is not easy to recognize that just as age deteriorates one’s body, so has it the same effect on the mind. Even more so is it true that a man who has been the commander for many years will not easily give up his position, especially if he is physically and mentally sound. The humbleness and humility displayed by Moses as recorded in this Sidrah was never found in any other human being. “Moses was very meek, above all the men that were upon the face of the earth” (Numbers 12:3).

At the beginning of Moses’ life, the Torah tells us: “VAYIGDAL MOSHEH VAYETZE EL ECHAYV,” “When Moses grew up he went out unto his brethren” (Exodus 2:11). Yes, he left the Egyptian palace where he was raised and came to help his brothers. Not yet had he received the mission for this task, but he did so voluntarily. So now too, after the burden and yoke of leadership was removed from him and given to Joshua, Moses went from tribe to tribe, the Torah tells us, and spoke to the people. The fact that for a very long time he was in command and it was they who came to him, not he to them, had in no wise changed his character, and he remained as meek and as humble as always.

Moses, who was chosen by God to deliver and to teach the Torah to the children of Israel, had to be himself a perfect example of what a man of Torah must be. “Now, write ye this song (Torah) for you, and teach thou it to the children of Israel, put it in their mouths . . .” (Deuteronomy 31:19). The words “put it in their mouths” mean that the Torah shall not serve as an ornamental object placed into an Ark for show. Its purpose is to teach the children of Israel, to change and to mold them to be a perfect people. Even put the ways of Torah in their mouths, so their speaking may be more gentle and refined. Words spoken with refinement are more impressive. Studying the Torah is not enough. It is important to study it in order to know how to fulfil its teachings. In the Talmud (Megilah 28b) we are told: “— that all who study daily the traditional laws are sure of the future world, for it is written: ‘HALICHOT OLAM LO’ (Habakkuk 3:6), ‘His ways are eternal.’” Do not read “HALICHOT,” ways, but “HALACHOT,” traditional laws. For these “HALACHOT,” laws, practiced in the ways of the world, in daily life, become the accomplishment of the Torah. The business man, the employer and the employee, can become very efficient in the “HALACHOT,” laws, when putting them into use in their day to day affairs. “When ye reap the harvest of your land, thou shalt not wholly reap the grain of the field, neither shalt thou gather the gleaning of thy harvest . . . thou shalt leave them for the poor and for the stranger . . . Ye shall not steal; neither shall ye deal falsely, nor lie to another . . . The wages of a hire servant shall not abide with thee all night until the morning” (Leviticus 19:9-13). Just as when one studies diligently the traditional laws he can become a scholar, even a “GAON,” so too, in practicing those laws in every human endeavor, can one become greatly improved in excellence of character and reach to the highest places in the world.

Our sages said: “ASHREI MI SHEBA L’KAAN V’TALMUDO B’YADO” (Sages), “Happy is the one who comes here (in the hereafter) and his knowledge (of the Torah) is in his hand.” It ought to say “and his knowledge is in his head.” What does it mean, “his knowledge is in his hand?” — A man may have much Torah knowledge in his head but if he does not practice it with his hands, whose function is to put into effect the good knowledge accumulated in the head, then he accomplished nothing. Only he who enters into the life to come, with hands that executed the laws of the Torah, is happy. — Since deeds count more than knowledge, Moses recognized and respected sincerely everyone from the hewer of wood to the drawer of water (see Deuteronomy 29:10). He went not only to those recognized as great scholars of “HALACHAH,” traditional law, but also to those who were great in “HALICHOT OLAM,” in the ways of the world, people who put into practice what the “HALACHAH” called for. This characteristic, and many other outstanding traits made Moses the most beloved leader of Israel of all times.

Not easy was the task to get Moses to take the leadership of the children of Israel (Exodus 4:13), for in his meekness, he considered everyone else more suitable for the great mission than he. But when he finally accepted the burden he became the greatest of the leaders of all times. His prophetic spirit was shown in all the wonderful acts which he performed both among the Egyptians and Israel. And even when he knew he would not enter the promised land, he did not relinquish any of his duties. Courageously, at the end of his mission, and without envy or dismay, he handed over the leadership to his pupil Joshua. Now again as a private citizen, as in the beginning of his life, he humbly went from tribe to tribe to exhort them to be brave and of good cheer, for God would not fail them (Deuteronomy 31:6).

Moses, who delivered God’s Torah to the people of Israel, also presented himself as an example of what the teachings of the Torah, when diligently observed and made a way of life, do to a man. There are many men who reveal excellent traits during their youth, but somehow change as soon as the pressures of life’s demands fall upon them, or when they are suddenly exalted to a high position. The Torah puts men on the “DERECH HAYASHAR,” the right course, and keeps them there from the beginning of life to the end.
