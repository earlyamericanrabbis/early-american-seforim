“And thou shalt command the children of Israel that they bring unto thee pure olive oil beaten for the light, to cause the lamp to burn always. In the tabernacle of the congregation outside the curtain which is before the testimony, Aaron and his sons shall order it from evening till morning before the Lord, it shall be a statute for ever unto their generations on behalf of the children of Israel” (Exodus XXVII:20-21).

This parshah describes in detail some of the very important objects and ornamentations that became a part of the sanctuary and the priesthood.

The candlelabrum with its seven branches was not made to light up the sanctuary; for, we may ask: (Midrash) “O Lord, Who givest light to the whole universe and yet commandest the burning of the perpetual light?” — Rashi tells us: “Moses was puzzled about the workmanship of the Menorah until the Holy One, blessed be He, showed him the pattern of it in a candlestick of fire, as it is said: “and see and make” — “see here, in the mountain, the pattern which I shew thee.”

Moses certainly knew that the candelabra as well as the other furnishings of the sanctuary were not ordered to be there for their physical use and purpose, but rather for their symbolism, to teach us something. Therefore was he puzzled with the meaning of all the intricate parts of the candlestick, until God showed him one of fire. Then Moses understood.

Wherever we find the word “Light” in the Torah, it may not always refer to physical light. It may mean the Torah itself. “TORAH OR,” the Torah is light because it enlightens, it teaches us, it opens our minds to understand things around us. For the same reason, general education, culture and science can also be called light. Through general knowledge, man is the highest product of God’s creation. He well superseded the work of evolution. He invented tools and machines to do the work which he himself could not do.

The candelabra with its seven branches represents the many branches of learning. God commanded: “Towards the face of the candlestick shall the light of the branches be directed.” — The shaft of the Menorah represents the knowledge of the Torah and the branches represent the other forms of knowledge.

The difference between the teachings of the Torah and other teachings is this: all the other wisdoms cultivate the mind of man, whereas the Torah reaches to enoble his heart. Yes! It is the ennobled heart of man that separates him from and sets him above the animal. Animals also have minds that display a high degree of intelligence. It is also known that they share our feelings and emotions, that they have memories, and also learn from experience. Man, however, holds the monopoly on rational judgment. The ability to distinguish truth from falsehood is a faculty that belongs only to man. But without putting the desires of man’s heart on the right path, his mind, lacking the teachings of the Torah, may be bribed and not be able to distinguish between right and wrong. The Greeks worshipped the beauty of form. The highest accomplishment to the Romans was the power of force. But the truth that we find in the Torah gives us more beauty and more power reflected from the inner spirit to the outer life. And for this the Jew lived and died.

We also read in today’s parshah: “And thou shalt make a bright plate of pure gold and engrave upon it, like the engraving of a signet, Holiness to the Eternal . . . and it shall be on Aaron’s forehead . . . and it shall be continually upon his forehead, that they may be accepted before the Eternal” (Exodus XXVIII:36-38).

“And Aaron shall bear the names of the children of Israel on the breastplate of judgment upon his heart when he cometh in unto the holy place, for a memorial before the Eternal continually. And thou shalt put in the breastplate of judgment the URIM and the TUMMIM. And they shall be upon Aaron’s heart when he cometh in before the Eternal continually” (Exodus XXVIII: 29.30).

Moses’ name does not appear in today’s sidrah. When Moses pleaded with God to forgive the children for the making of the golden calf, he said: “Yet now, if Thou wilt forgive their sin . . . and if not, blot me, I pray Thee, out of Thy book which Thou has written” (Exodus XXXII:32). Moses preferred death, rather than face the evil that might come to his people. This sounded a bit selfish, although Moses is known to be the very opposite. What he really meant was that if God was to destroy the people of Israel, he did not want to live without them and start the nation all over again by himself, as God said to Moses: “Now therefore let Me alone, that My wrath may wax hot against them, and that I may consume them, and I will make of thee a great nation” (Exodus XXXII:10). Moses did not wish to become great upon the destruction of his people. In Exodus XXXIII:14-15, we find Moses pleading with God. “And He said: My Presence shall go with thee and I will give thee rest. And he (Moses) said unto Him: If Thy Presence go not with me, carry us not hence. For wherein now shall it be known that I found grace in Thy sight, I and Thy people? Is it not in that Thou goest with us . . .” Again Moses was not satisfied for God to go with him alone. But our sages tell us: “CHACHAMIM HIZAHARU B’DIVREICHEM” (Avot), “The wise man must be careful with his words.” Words that can be misinterpreted by any human being should not come from a wise man’s mouth. Therefore, Moses’ name does not appear in this parshah.

The high priest who is the teacher of his people must always combine in him mind and heart. Regarding the coverings for both, on the plate of the forehead and on the breastplate, God said: “He must wear it continually.” Rashi comments: “It informs us that he must continually touch it while it was on his forehead, so that he should not divert his attention from it.” That is to say, the high priest had to have his mind and heart directed towards his people at all times. Even his contacts with the Divine Being had to be through the URIM AND TUMMIM which were placed upon his breastplate where the names of the tribes of Israel were inserted. Only when his attention, either in mind or in heart, was undiverted from his people could he attain his highest degree of leadership.

A leader must be trained and able to stay and suffer with his people. He must not take the easy way out. Yes! This parshah teaches us, through the lesson of the Menorah, the importance of knowledge both for the mind and heart of man, that priority must be given to cultivate the heart of man through the teachings of the Torah. And when this parshah speaks of the high priest, it is demanded of him to direct his full attention to the needs of his people. His mind and heart, both, had reminders in the form of the forehead plate and the breastplate, that he should at no time divert his attention from his people and become somewhat self-centered. Therefore Aaron was chosen for the mission of priesthood. It was Aaron who had the outstanding qualities of a good teacher, who, when the pupils fail in their learning, does not blame them but himself.

On Yom Kippur, when the high priest made atonement, we read in the Torah that first of all he made atonement for himself and for his house (Leviticus XVI:6), and then again he made atonement for himself and for his household, and for all the assembly of Israel (Leviticus XVI:17). Here too, is good proof of what a “KOHEN,” a priest, has to be, that before he finds fault with others, he first searches himself.

It is the Torah that teaches us not to be selfish and self-centered. That our joy, comfort and luxury must be shared with others. This alone separates us from the world of creatures, and this alone will save the world from destruction. “L’HALOT NER TAMID,” “to cause a lamp to burn continuously,” the lesson that we learn from the MENORAH is forever to enlighten us at all times.
