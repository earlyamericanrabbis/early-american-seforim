“Give ear, ye heavens, and I will speak; and let the earth hear the words of my mouth. My doctrine shall drop as the rain, my speech shall distil as the dew; as the small rain upon the tender grass, and as the showers upon the herb” (Deuteronomy 32:1-2). “Moses called heaven and earth to witness his last request because they are eternal. He told them that the Torah is life to Israel as rain is to the world; but whereas rain is troublesome to wayfarers and others, Moses adds ‘as the dew’ which is acceptable to all. Just as rain and dew do not fail in their effect of fertilizing the soil so, prays Moses, may his words find receptive minds among Israel” (Rashi and Ibn Ezrah).

This Parshah is the farewell song of Moses, wherein he made a comprehensive survey of Israel’s destiny from infancy to final deliverance from exile. Beginning with the mercies which God had shown to Israel since that nation became His people, it predicts Israel’s betrayal while residing in a land of plenty, the sufferings in that land and banishment from it. Then it continues to predict that in the end God will pay homage to Israel.

In today’s Haftorah the prophet Hosea (Hosea 14) says: “Return, O Israel, unto the Lord thy God, for thou has stumbled in thine iniquity. Take with you words, and return unto the Lord . . .” — In the Hebrew text we find the prophet begins in singular form “SHUVAH ISRAEL,” “Return O, Israel,” and in the next sentence he speaks in plural form, “V’SHUV EL HASHEM,” “and return ye unto the Lord.”

The belief in God as the Creator of the universe is accepted by almost all the peoples of the world. But the channels through which one comes to such a conclusion are diversified. One can just glance at nature and, like the Psalmist, become overwhelmed with what he beholds, and exclaim: “How manifold are thy works, O Lord! In wisdom hast Thou made them all: the earth is full of Thy possessions” (Psalm 104). Another one looks at nature but sees nothing of Godliness in it. In PEREK SHIRAH we are told that every creature sings songs glorifying God. If one would only divert his attention from the deafening noises about him, he would hear millions of creatures sing in a huge chorus their praise to God. If one would only open his eyes, he would see and even feel God’s presence everywhere in nature. Our sages tell us (Avot 6.2) that there is a “BAS KOL,” an echo, coming every day from the Mount Chorev saying: “Woe is to the people for their disregard of the Torah.” There are numerous signals directed to us by the Creator but many of us fail to turn on the receiver of our inner being to be able to see and hear God’s message to us. The Psalmist enumerates many voices of the Lord: “The voice of the Lord is upon the waters; the God of Glory thundereth, even the Lord upon the great waters. The voice of the Lord is powerful; the voice of the Lord is full of majesty. The voice of the Lord breaketh the cedars . . .” (Psalm 29).

Why does not man see and hear these? Because man’s mind is not fully developed at the time of birth. That comes later and by the time full maturity of the brain is reached, he has already seen again and again the functions of nature, and now he takes all that for granted. The first man, we are told (Sages), when he beheld the setting of the sun, exclaimed: “Woe is to me! The world is coming to an end!” Yes, he was frightened. But when the next morning came and he beheld the sun again, he said: “MINHAGO SHEL OLAM HU,” “It is the nature of the world.” Things that we see day in and day out do not tend to impress or inspire us to thought. We see miracles in the change of nature’s course, as in the dividing of the Red Sea. But we do not see much in the “MIKOLOT MAYIM RABIM ADIRIM MISHBREI YAM,” “The voices of many waters, mighty waters, breakers of the sea” (Psalm 29). We see a miracle in “SHEMESH B’GIVON DOM,” “Sun, stand thou still upon Gib’on” (Joshua 10:12), but we do not see a miracle in “V’ZORACH HASHEMESH UBA HASHEMESH,” “The sun also riseth, and the sun goeth down.” Or in “HASHOMAYIM MESAPRIM K’VOD KEL,” “The heavens recount the glory of God” (Psalm 19).

On Rosh Hashonah, the Torah commands: “Ye shall make (prepare) a burnt offering for a sweet savour unto the Lord . . .” (Numbers 29.2). The text uses the Hebrew word “VAASITEM,” meaning “Ye shall prepare” and the sages comment: “Wherefore is the word “VAASITEM” used for the Rosh Hashonah offering when for the other offerings the word “VEHIKRAVTEM,” “Ye shall bring near,” is used? They answer that this is to denote “KEILU ASITEM ET ATZMECHEM B’RIYAH CHADASHAH,” “It shall be considered as though you have been transformed into a new creature.” What the sages mean is that on the New Year, one must feel that he has just been born, but with a fully developed mind, and he looks at things of nature as if he has never seen them before. Then will he be inspired by the normal course nature takes and he will see and hear therein the wonders of the Lord.

On the words: “V’ATA PEN YISHLACH YADO V’LAKACH GAM MEITZ HACHAYIM . . .” (Genesis 3:22), “And now lest he put forth his hand, and take also of the tree of life . . .,” comments the sage R. Aba, “This is to denote that God opened for Adam a door for repentance.” A similar word, “V’ATA,” “And now,” is used in conjunction with the demands of God from man. “And now, Israel, what doth the Lord thy God require of thee, but to fear the Lord thy God, to walk in His ways and to love Him . . .” In the word “V’ATA,” “And now,” lies man’s ability to repent and return to God, beholding nature with a fully developed mind and observing it “now” for the first time. Constantly seeing the same thing causes one to lose interest. The repetition no longer attracts attention. But “TESHUVAH,” repentance, has the function of returning to man, through the “V’ATA,” the feeling of being newly born and changed.

The Rabbis ask: “How does prayer help to change a decree of God when the Torah says: ‘HAHU AMAR V’LO YAASEH V’DIBER V’LO YIKIMENAH,’ ‘When He hath said will He not do it? Or when He hath spoken, will He not make good?’ (Numbers 23:19)?” They answer that God does not change the decree against the sinner, and when a man prays and repents, the man is changed and the decree does not cover this man for he is not the same. But when Moses prayed to be forgiven for his sin and to be permitted to enter the promised land, it was not granted. Because Moses could never change into another and more perfected person. He was the one of whom the Torah said: “And there has not risen a prophet in Israel like unto Moses” (Deuteronomy 34:10).

“SHUVA ISRAEL AD HASHEM ELOKECHA,” “Return O Israel to the Lord your God.” The Hebrew word “AD,” “till,” is “AD V’LO AD BICHLAL” (Sages), meaning “Return till God but there shall be a certain distance from God.” “U’BIKASHTEM MISHOM ET HASHEM ELOKECHA UMATZATA,” “But you will seek there the Lord thy God and you will find Him” (Deuteronomy 4:29). When one is close to nature through the stages of life during which his mind is not yet ready to think, he will not observe the wonders with which God has endowed nature as well as would he who has been totally excluded from seeing or observing nature till his mind began to function. Such a one, exposed to these great wonders for the first time, is inspired to think of their Creator. The search for God must be “MISHAM,” “from there,” nothing through closeness, through “HERGEL,” “familiarity.” But from a distance, through the unknown. However, this is true only about an individual where closeness and familiarity cause the loss of interest, but is not so when it comes to a group of people. They too may be dulled by the constant intimacy with nature, but not as much as an individual. For among a group, there are always some who are moved to inspiration by the very same familiarity and they may be able to transmit this motivation to the others. Therefore when the prophet speaks of the people of Israel as a group, he says, “V’SHUVU EL HASHEM,” “Ye return to God.” “EL HASHEM” means close to God. Because in a group the danger of closeness is lessened.

In the words of the prophet: “Whoso is wise, let him understand these things; whoso is prudent, let him know them for the ways of the Lord are right, and the just walk in them; but the transgressors do stumble therein” (Hosea 14:10).
