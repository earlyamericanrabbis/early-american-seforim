Today’s parshah and Haftorah teach us a very important lesson pertaining to the behavior of men, in particular, the telling and rebuking others for the mistakes they made. In order that one shall not again make the same error, it is important that he know and recognize that what he has done is a mistake. But to teach this to a man, we must find the right time and the right place. While a person is suffering as a consequence of his misdeeds, he is not able to grasp what he is being told. In a place where he beholds destruction and disillusionment, any type of rebuke will act like acid on an open wound. When the pain is unbearable, one can not think.

In today’s parshah we find Moses rebuking the people of Israel for the wrongs they have committed since their departure from Egypt. The Torah points out “ACHREI AKOTO ET SICHON . . .” “after he has smitten the nations bordering the land of Israel.” He did not rebuke them during the time that they were marching in the hot sun of the desert, when they could hardly see an end to their wanderings. But, after they were already at the gates of their promised land and had met with success against their enemies, Moses confronted them. The Torah also teaches us the proper approach in reminding one of his errors. Moses first pointed out to the people of Israel the different places where the unhappy events took place. This served as a reminder to start them thinking. Then he lashed out in detail about everything that happened. The ability to tell one about the mistakes he made is an art. In order that it shall bring the proper results, one must do so carefully and intelligently. Man’s mind is not properly receptive to admonishment when he is under any kind of stress. So that he shall fully perceive what he is told, he must be more or less in a relaxed state of mind.

In today’s Haftorah, we find that Isaiah tells his people that they are about to bring a “CHURBAN,” a total destruction, upon themselves because of the wrong standards by which they were living. He tries to save them by making them realize their misdeeds. — In order to find a remedy to a given problem, one must know what brought on the problem. Only then can one look for a solution. Isaiah warns them against their insincerity to each other. He also warns them against their complete loss of a sense of values. “And when ye spread forth your hands, I will hide Mine eyes from you. Yea when ye make many prayers, I will not hear, your hands are full of blood” (Isaiah I:15). “When ye come to appear before Me, who hath required this at your hand to trample My courts” (Isaiah I:12). Speaking of trampling the courts, Isaiah uses the word “hand.”[^1] But hands do not trample. It is the feet that do. However, Isaiah properly meant that the hands were trampling in the holy places. When he says “Your hands are full of blood,” the word for “blood” is “DAMIM” which has a twofold meaning, “blood,” and “money.” Isaiah pointed to the cardinal sin of placing too much value on money. Unfortunately also in our own time, it is money that gives one a high position in society too often. We also practice a most ridiculous way of deciding matters of great importance, by counting a majority of up-lifted hands. Even problems pertaining to matters of “KEDUSHAH,” of interpreting our Torah law, are decided by people who have no feelings for that. The leaderships of synagogues and other holy institutions are taken over by people who can display wealth and their hands are influencing decisions. Therefore Isaiah rebukes them by saying: “Who hath required this at your hand to trample My courts?”

Jeremiah in the book of Lamentations (I:15) says: “He hath called a solemn assembly against me, to crush my young men.” Our sages placing importance on these words, call “TISHAH B’OV” a holiday and many laws referring to holidays are practiced on “TISHAH B’OV.” But this is contrary to the usual joyful and happy purpose of a holiday. Our sages say: “B’TISHAH B’OV NOLAD MOSHIACH,” “Messiah our redeemer was born on TISHAH B’OV.” “ATID HAKADOSH BARUCH HU L’HAPOCH TISHAH B’OV LESIMCHAH,” “God will in the future make “TISHAH B’OV” into a joyous time.” How does lamentation mix with happiness?

In Tractat Gitin 58a, we find that R. Joshua b. Hananiah once happened to go to the great city of Rome, and he was told there of the imprisonment of a child with beautiful eyes and face and curly locks. He went and stood at the doorway of the prison and said: “Who gave Jacob for spoil and Israel to the robbers?” The child answered, “Is it not the Lord, He against whom we have sinned and in Whose ways they would not walk, neither were they obedient unto His law.” R. Joshua said: “I feel sure that this one will be a teacher in Israel. I swear that I will not move from this place before I ransom him, whatever the price may be.” It is reported that he did not leave before he had ransomed the child at high cost. Nor did many days pass before the child became a teacher in Israel. Who was he? He was R. Ishmael b. Elisha.

“HAKARAT HACHET,” “recognizing an error,” is the most important factor for real repentance. After the destruction of our homeland the leaders of our people were greatly perplexed as to how we would rise again as a nation, out of such physical destruction and disillusionment. Our sages saw that most of the people did not know why they suffered. Even those who witnessed the “CHURBAN.” And the learned among them asked “AL MAH AVDAH HARETZ,” “Wherefore was the land destroyed?” And they gave different answers. And more so, the young generation which grew up in exile little realized what had happened. Like Jeremiah, the prophet, they bewailed the “CHURBAN,” but how to make a comeback, how to rise up again out of the ashes of destruction, they did not know. Because most of them did not recognize the germ that caused the disease that brought on the “CHURBAN.” Therefore, when R. Joshua b. Hananiah found a child who was raised in a modern environment, but still answered correctly to the general question that so perplexed the leaders of those days, he excitedly exclaimed, “I am certain that he will be a leader in Israel.”

The belief in the coming of the Messiah meant a constant hope and striving for the complete redemption of humanity. If through the lamenting for the “CHURBAN” came a birth of “MOSHIACH,” then it calls for celebration. If the “CHURBANOT” of all kinds brought us to the realization of our past mistakes and benefited us into making a comeback, then “TISHAH B’OV” can be considered as a holiday.

May it be the will of God, as we have already witnessed the footsteps of our Messiah, and our exiled people have already made a return to their homeland, that we may speedily, in our days, be privileged to witness the complete redemption of our people and the whole world. Amen.
