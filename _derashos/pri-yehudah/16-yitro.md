This sidrah that contains the Ten Commandments, the essence of the whole Torah, is named Parshah Yitro.

What is so great in this man Jethro and what is his contribution to the spiritual wealth of our people, that his name heads this most important parshah? After all, Jethro is not only a non-Jew, he is the spiritual leader of an idol-worshipping sect, the Midianites. Certainly, his being the father-in-law of Moses should not give him priority over Moses himself. Would it not be more fitting to name this parshah after Moses who delivered the Ten Commandments? In today’s sidrah we find several important happenings in which Jethro transcends even Moses.

The sidrah begins: “Now Jethro, the Priest of Midian, Moses’ father-in-law, heard of all that God had done for Moses and for Israel, his people, how that the Lord had brought Israel out of Egypt, etc.” Rashi comments: “What was the most important thing that made Jethro come? The division of the Red Sea and the war with Amalek were the stirring factors for Jethro to come.” What did Jethro behold when he came upon the Israelites? He knew very well that the children of Israel had to be almost pulled out of Egypt. They were not very willing to leave, as it is said “because they were thrust out of Egypt” (Exodus XII:39), and “How that the Lord had brought Israel out of Egypt,” the supreme act of God and therefore specially recorded (Rashi - Exodus XVIII.1). Yes, thus had they become free! But how could they be made into an organized people in such a short time? How could they, in a matter of days, do away with the character which hundreds of years of subjugation had stamped upon them? Jethro was well aware that the children of Israel had made peace with their plight in Egypt and were not even anxious to leave the land, that it took a miracle to get them out of there. Yet he finds them well organized in their war effort against the Amalekites and in this instance, not waiting for miracles. That alone is the greatest single thing that could happen to a people who had sunk so low that they no longer felt their sufferings. The Torah calls it “SIVLOT MITZRAYIM” (Exodus VI:6), “The burdens of the Egyptians.” The word “SIVLOT” is derived from the word “SAVOL,” to bear or to endure. Yes, they had learned to endure their lot, and were not looking for redemption. But suddenly this same people is seen by Jethro heroically waging a successful war against the mighty army of the Amalekites. Jethro, the non-Jew, had he wanted to find fault with the people who had only recently been emancipated from Egypt, could have had enough material on which to base his criticism. Instead, he found them praiseworthy. — In Verse 10 we read, “And Jethro said blessed be the Eternal Who hath delivered you out of the hand of Egypt and out of the hand of Pharaoh, Who hath delivered the people from under the hand of Egypt.” The repetition of almost the same words, “Who hath delivered the people from under the hand of Egypt,” may mean their sinking low to the point where they did not want to be delivered out of Egypt.

Referring further to Jethro’s statement, in the Talmud (Sanhedrin 94a) A Tanna reported in the name of R. Pappias: “It is a reproach to Moses and the six hundred thousand people that did not say the words ‘BARUCH . . .,’ ‘Blessed is God for their departure from Egypt,’ until Jethro came and said: ‘Blessed is God Who saved you, etc.’ ” Although they did sing a song of praise to God when they crossed the Red Sea, still, because they did not say the words “Blessed is God,” they were reprimanded by the Talmudic sages. Why?

“KEDUSHAH” and “B’RACHAH,” holiness and blessing combined, is an axiom in our belief. When God created man, Scripture tells us, “VAYVARECH OTOM ELOKIM,” “God blessed them.” “V’HASHEM BERACH ET AVRAHAM BAKOL,” “And the Lord blessed Abraham in all things.” The Torah describes in great length the blessings that Isaac gave to his son Jacob and the blessings Jacob bestowed on his children. The children of Israel are commanded, “K’DOSHIM THIYU KI KADOSH ANI,” “Ye shall be holy, for I the Lord Your God am holy.” “REHE NATATI L’FANECHA HAYOM ET HACHAYIM V’ET HATOV VET HAMAVET V’ET HARA,” “See, I have set before thee life and good, and death and evil” (Deuteronomy XXX:15). And in verse 19 we read: “I have set before thee life and death, the blessing and the curse, therefore choose life.” It does not say to choose “good,” but to choose life, because the blessing of “good” man cannot choose for himself. Man does not even know what is good for him. That has to be decided by God Himself, for He knows what is best for man. “U’METZ HADATH TOV V’RA LO TOCHAL MIMENU,” “From the Tree of Knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat.” The choice of life does not mean life itself, for in that too we are dependant on God’s will. It means the “KEDUSHAT HACHAYIM,” “The Holiness of Life,” which depends on what we make of our lives. Life is holy, but not death. One can be dead or unholy even when alive. “RESHAYIM B’CHAYEHEM KRUYIM METIM,” “The evil ones, though they are alive are called dead” (Sages). And one can be alive and holy even when he is dead. “TZADIKIM BEMITATAN KRUYIM CHAYIM,” “The righteous though they are dead, are considered to be alive” (Sages). In other words, holiness is within our own hands to acquire. Also, the Sabbath given to the children of Israel by God as a sign between God and His chosen people (Exodus XXXI:17) is a combination of blessings and holiness. “VAYVARECH ELOKIM ET YOM HASHVIHI VYKADESH OTO,” “And God blessed the seventh day, and hallowed it.”

Thus, in order to have our belief complete, we must understand that God is the source not only of holiness but also of all blessings. But Moses and the children of Israel thought that blessings are earthly in character and only holiness should be ascribed to heaven. Asceticism to them meant holiness, and the blessings of life had no place in it. — When Moses accepted the mission to lead the children of Israel out of Egypt, he separated from his wife and children, for they were a part of the blessings of life. But, we find in today’s parshah that Jethro did not share with Moses in such a thought. “VAYIKACH YITRO CHOTEN MOSHE ET TZIPORAH ESHET MOSHE ACHAR SHLUCHEAH” (Exodus XVIII:2), “And Jethro, Moses’ father-in-law, took Ziporah, Moses’ wife, after Moses sent her away and brought her back to Moses.”

The Jew must believe that not only is God the Creator of the universe, but He is also a “MASHGIACH AL HAKOL,” that He watches over everything. Anything that occurs comes through His will and is not just coincidence. Believing that God is the starting point of all blessings means that we believe in His “HASHGACHAH PROTIT,” that God cares for every individual and He rewards the one who does good and punishes the one who does evil. When we utter a blessing to God for anything we have, we thereby identify ourselves with God as He is the one Who gave it to us.

Some still believe that praise to God must be only for the solemnity but not for the proximity. God, to them, is the ALLHIGHEST POWER, and though earthly gifts come from that source, God must not be praised directly for that. A distal practice is followed by them. Jethro, with his praising God for all He has done to Egypt and for the deliverance of the children of Israel from bondage, has shown that he believes that the proximity of God is to be praised (S.R.H.).

We find in today’s parshah another important lesson that Jethro taught Moses. “And it came to pass in the morning that Moses sat to judge the people. And the people stood by Moses from morning unto the evening. And when Moses’ father-in-law saw all that he did to the people, he said, what is this thing that thou doest to the people. Why sittest thou thyself alone and the people are placed near thee from the morning unto the evening.” Yes, Jethro instructed Moses in the proper way of administering justice to the people of Israel by the least amount of “TIRCHAH DETZIBURAH,” the trouble, of waiting for a long time to be heard in court.

Jethro’s lesson as a prelude to KABALAT HATORAH, the receiving of the Torah, serves also as an answer to those who think that Moses wrote the Torah himself. From this it is easy to see that Moses had little talent for legislation and organization, so little that he had to learn this from his father-in-law. Such a man could never have given a constitution and laws to his people. But through Moses, God gave the Torah to the children of Israel (S.R.H.).
