“And the Eternal said unto Moses, Say unto the priests the sons of Aaron, and thou shalt say unto them, there shall none be defiled by the dead among the people . . .” (Lev. XXI:1-3). Rashi comments: “The repetition of the words ‘say’ and again ‘thou shalt say’ is intended to admonish the adults about their children also — that they should avoid defilement.” — The Midrash (Lev. Rabah) says: “Because Aaron feared God, he was privileged that this parshah, dealing with the laws pertaining to the dead, was given to him and to his children till the end of the generations.”

The average man does not see things for what they are, but only through comparison. He does not realize light until he has seen darkness. He does not appreciate good health till he experiences sickness in himself or in the people about him. He does not appreciate the freshness of the air on a farm till he spends some time in a large congested city. For the same reason, he will not see or feel the peace of mind he, as an observant Jew, has until he comes in contact with the very restless and sometimes disturbed unobservant Jew. — “It is better to go to the house of mourning, than to the house of feasting; for that is the end of all men, and the living will lay it to his heart” (Ecclesiastes VII:2). There at the house of mourning one will learn to appreciate life as it is. A visit to such a house is a reminder to reflect upon the three things suggested by our sages, to protect us from coming within the power of Sin. “Know whence thou comest, and whither thou are going, and before whom thou wilt in future have to give account and reckoning” (Avot).

Man engrossed only in pleasures is not conditioned to the adversities of life. Such a man, when the trials of life catch up with him, becomes shocked and disturbed to a point beyond cure. — Yes! The Torah permits pleasures, but the regulations of the Torah safeguard that man shall get the pleasures without paying a high price for it.

In today’s parshah (Lev. XXIII:37) we read: “ELEH MOADEI HASHEM . . .” “These are the appointed festivals of the Eternal . . .” The Midrash comments: “ILU Z’CHITEM HAYITEM KOR’IM ELEH MOADEI HASHEM, V’ACHSHAV SHELO Z’CHITEM ATEM KOR’IM AL ELEH ANI BOCHIYAH.” “If you would have been privileged you would have read, ‘These are the appointed festivals of the Eternal,’ but now when you are not privileged you read ‘For these things I weep’ ” (Lamentations 1:16). — If the “MOADEI” “festivals,” are from the category of “HASHEM,” through the regulations of the Torah, then it is full of pleasures and undisturbed enjoyments. The kind of enjoyment that does not leave a bad effect. But if it is of the kind that is uncontrolled, one will certainly pay for it afterwards.

Our sages understood well the frailties of man. They knew very well that man is being constantly pulled down by his earthly desires. They advised him “If the repulsive wretch (evil inclination) meets thee, drag him to the BETH HAMIDRASH; if he is thereby subdued it is fine, but if not, read ‘KRIAT SHEMA,’ and if this does not help, remind yourself of the day of death” (Sages). First, they advised to take the evil inclination into the BETH HAMIDRASH, the place of learning of the Torah, where one can learn the truth of right and wrong and not be influenced by the “YETZER HARA.” As it is told of a pious Jew, when someone brought him to the point of anger, he would run to the “SHULCHAN ORUCH” to look up if one is permitted to become angry for such a thing. Naturally, by that time his rage cooled off. Secondly, our sages further advised that if the BETH HAMIDRASH did not help, to recite “SHEMA.” When doing so, one takes upon himself the yoke of the Kingdom of God, which may save him from committing a foolish deed. But if this too does not help, he should remind himself of his end and that will put him straight in his way of thinking. — That is what Ecclesiastes meant by saying: “It is better to go to the house of mourning, than to the house of feasting.” And the psalmist (Psalm CXXVI) also said: “They that sow in tears shall reap in joy. Though he goeth on his way weeping that beareth the measure of seed, he shall come back with joy bearing his sheaves.”

The “ADAM HASHALEM,” the man of perfection, does not need to use the last resort, namely the reminder of his end, or even put himself in a position that will bring him to a state of tears, in order to be set straight on his way of life. This sort of man sees things as they are. He does not require comparisons to further his understanding. He has such a degree of faith that he does not have to subject himself to see the end of man, in order to satisfy the demands of his life. The priests were not only teachers in the ways of the Torah, but had to act as examples of what they taught. The people had to see in the priests the real benefits of following this sort of teaching. In the same way that one has greater confidence in a physician who is and looks healthy, so it is with a teacher and a priest. He must have the perfection of body and soul, in order to be able to influence the people to his preaching of the morality of the Torah. He must be a vivid, living example.

The Torah orders: “Speak unto Aaron, saying, Whosoever he be of thy seed in their generations that hath any blemish, let him not approach to offer the bread of God” (Lev. XXI:17). — Yes! Even the slightest blemish mars the priest’s effectiveness in teaching and in the influencing of the people. But even more so, if there is an imperfection, a weakness in his character. When the priest too has to resort to the thought of death in order not to be seduced by evil inclinations, then he can not be a teacher of the people. “Serve the Lord with joy!” orders the psalmist. It is the aim of the Torah that one learn to live a life that will not necessitate its being marred with thoughts that become melancholy. — Therefore, Aaron who was already stable in his ways and feared God, was told not to come in contact with the dead. Because his position was to teach, he had to be extraordinarily perfect physically and mentally.

There is another way to help man rid himself of the “YETZER HARA.” The sages (Pirke Avot 4) suggests: “Run quickly towards a little MITZVAH and run away from sin.” The question is, why do they say “Run towards a small MITZVAH”? Why not say: “Run to a MITZVAH”? Also why do they suggest doing the MITZVAH first and then running from sin, when the suggestion of the psalmist is the opposite: “SUR MERA VAASEH TOV,” “Turn away from evil and do good”? The sages, in their advice on how to get rid of the “YETZER HARA,” meant that doing any little MITZVAH will help you and protect you from doing evil. In that way you strengthen the “YETZER TOV” and thereby acquire an ally in your war against the “YETZER HARA.” — This is what the Rabbis call “YIRAH ELOHO,” “the higher type of faith.” Instead of trying to weaken the “YETZER HARA” by the last resort — the contemplation of one’s end, a man must instead try to secure for himself the effective ally, the “YETZER TOV.”

Aaron had the “YETZER TOV” developed in him. Therefore he did not need to defile himself with the dead.

Aaron and his children, the priests, who had to atone for the sins, were to teach the people that in order to receive atonement for committing a sin, one must show real repentance by doing a MITZVAH with the same enthusiasm with which the sin was performed. In Exodus 30:12 we read that God suggested that every one should give half of a sheckel to atone for his sin. The sages said that God showed Moses a coin of fire and said: “Give one like this one.” What it means is that if you want atonement for your sins, the MITZVAH that you perform must have the same fire or fervor you had when you committed a sin.

Yes! The Kohen had to show not a diminishment of his courage, not a dark outlook, but rather great strength and a bright and sunny approach to life. “Serve the Lord happily,” is the call of the Torah.
