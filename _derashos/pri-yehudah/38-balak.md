“And Balak . . . saw all that Israel has done to the Amorites . . . And Moab was sore afraid of the people, because they were many. And Moab was overcome with dread because of the children of Israel. And Moab said unto the elders of Midian: Now will the multitude lick up all that is round about us . . . and Balak . . . was king of Moab at that time. And he sent messengers unto Balaam . . . to call him saying . . . come now therefore I pray thee, curse me this people, for they are too mighty for me, peradventure I shall prevail, that we may smite them, and that I may drive them out of the land” (Numbers XXII:2-12).

This parshah unveils for us and brings into the open the character of the world’s greatest prophet except for Moses, Balaam. The Torah tells us: “V’LO KAM NAVI OD B’YISRAEL K’MOSHE,” “And there has not arisen a prophet since, in Israel like Moses” (Deut. XXXIV:10), and our sages comment: “B’YISRAEL LO KAM, AVAL B’UMOT HAOLAM KAM, U’MIHU BALAAM,” “Only in Israel did not another arise, but among the nations arose, and that was Balaam.”

The earnest student of the Bible may ask a qualified question: “Where do the sages see the comparison, when their characters are so widely separated?” Moses was honest and sincere. Neither honors, nor gold or silver could prevail upon him to do wrong. Moses, who was the emancipator of his people, who was the acclaimed of heaven and of the people of Israel, was nevertheless recognized as the meekest man of all the men of the earth. Moses could not do a thing that was tricky or underhanded. We find that even in a war against his enemies, he could not use any kind of false diplomacy or battle deception to put the enemy off guard. The Torah tells us that during the war with the Amalekites, when Moses lifted up his hands, Israel prevailed, and when he let his hands down, Amalek prevailed. Yes, Moses was open in his tactics even on the battlefield. We find Joshua readily using battle campaign strategy, but not Moses.

Balaam on the other hand was very greedy for gold and silver, and sold out his own honor and the honor of his people (Numbers XXII:17). “‘I (Balak) will promote thee unto very great honor, and whatsoever thou sayest unto me I will do; come therefore I pray thee, curse me this people.’ And Balaam answered and said . . . ‘If Balak would give me his house full of silver and gold.’ ” — It is interesting to point out that he did not reply regarding the honors he was offered by Balak, but only about the silver and gold. He was so dishonest and immoral that even his ass noticed it and miraculously found words to reprimand him (Numbers XXII:21-35, see Rashi). “And the Lord opened the mouth of the ass and she said unto Balaam: What have I done unto thee, that thou hast smitten me . . . Am I not thy ass upon which thou hast ridden all thy life . . . Was I ever wont to do so unto thee . . . Then the Lord opened the eyes of Balaam and he saw the angel of the Lord . . . And the Angel said to him: Wherefore hast thou smitten thine ass . . . I came as an adversary, because thy way is contrary unto me. And the ass saw me and turned aside before me . . .”

When Balaam could find no other way to succeed in his paid mission, he advised Balak to permit the tragic defilement of the daughters of Moab and of Midian in order to cause deterioration in the ranks of Israel (Numbers XXV:1-9). “And Israel abode in Shitim, and the people began to commit harlotry with the daughters of Moab.” The Talmud (Sanh. 106a) says that Balaam suggested the plan to corrupt Israel through the lure of these women. Being a good prophet, he could not help but praise the people of Israel (Numbers XXIV:5-25), “How goodly are thy tents O Jacob, thy dwelling places O Israel.” He even predicted a great future for them, “Come and I will announce to thee what this people will do to thy people in the end of the days.” This prophecy relates to the events of the Messianic Era (N). — But, all of this did not stop him from advising Balak to commit the most disgraceful act!

The parshah concludes with the story: “And behold, one of the children of Israel came and brought unto his brethren a Midianitish woman in the sight of Moses and in the sight of the congregation of Israel, while they were weeping (praying fervently) (E) . . . And when Pinchas . . . saw it . . . took a spear . . . and thrust both of them through, the man of Israel and the woman through her belly, so the plague was stayed from the children of Israel.” — This again proves the difference in the character of Balaam, who was considered a great prophet, and Moses, and a man like Pinchas. Balaam disgraced and put at stake the life of a princess of Moab, just so he might prevail in his evil mission. But Pinchas risked his life in order to save his people from being a part of this very shameful act. He murdered a prince of the tribe of Simeon.

Even with all the artifices Balaam used, he did not prevail over the children of Israel. We find in the end that he and his people of Midian were totally destroyed.

Balaam as a great prophet should very well have known that the B’nai Israel were indestructible. They were blessed by God. “No weapon that is formed against thee shall prosper, and every tongue that shall rise against thee in judgment thou shalt condemn” (Isaiah LIV:17).

The Torah tells us of the blessings that Isaac handed over to Jacob (Genesis XXVII:23). Jacob in his last testimonial points out to his children the weak links in their midst. Meaning: that in spite of their inherited blessings, our people would be subject to enemies who might use the weaknesses in order to hurt them. — Jacob spoke in general terms, but bearing them in mind, we may find that at all times in our history when we suffered, the enemies were wise enough to uncover our frailties. Only by doing so were they successful in penetrating our ranks.

Foes, like Egypt, tried in a direct way to destroy us and failed (Exodus I:12). “The more they afflicted them the more they multiplied and the more they spread about.” Amalak failed. All the other nations of the Biblical days and after failed. Only those nations who succeeded in drawing us away from our heritage managed to hurt us severely.

Our shield, our protection in keeping us safe from our enemies, is our heritage. Through it we are blessed. As God said to Balaam (Numbers XXII:12) “Thou shalt not curse the people for they are blessed.” Once we break with our heritage we become an open target for those who would destroy us.

The world at all times has had many prophets like Balaam, wise enough to find our weaklings, and with them, begin to draw us away from our blessed way of living. Balaam tries “ET KTZEH HAAM.” “And he saw from thence the utmost part of his people” (Numbers XXII:41). When Balaam saw that he could not prevail over the whole congregation of Israel, he tried a part of them, and through them to hurt the whole. Yes! it would have been propitious for him to succeed in his mission were it not for the heroic act of Pinchas who saved the situation.

In every generation, we were fortunate in having these brave men whom our people produced, men who in time noticed when our enemies breached somewhere the walls of our defenses and unhesitatingly took action until they succeeded in closing the gap.

Balaam said (Numbers XXIII:9), “EN AM LEVADAD YISHKON U’VAGOIM LO YITCHASHAV,” “Lo, it is a people that shall dwell alone and shall not be reckoned among the nations.” “They will always remain a separate entity, unlike any other nation and as such invincible and imperishable” (N). Our protection does not depend on making alliances with other peoples, but, with the help of God, on our own efforts. Our prophets warned us against relying on the cooperation of other nations. “HAKADOSH BARUCH HU HERAH L’MOSHEH DOR DOR V’DORSHOV” (Sages), “God has shown to Moses each generation and its leaders.” Moses wanted to know if in generations to come the people of Israel would produce a man with vision to match the Balaam of those days, and the Lord showed him that each generation produces its proper leaders, who would be of value to that generation. Yes, it is the people that create what their leaders are. Our people created a Moses, a David and many others who used their wisdom and abilities, not for trickery or putting their people to shame for selfish reasons. But the nations of the world, as in the days of Balaam, produced their wise men, their prophets to use their abilities to create evil. In the outset, Balaam was a prototype of Moses, but the people of Israel made Moses what he was and the other nations made Balaam what he was.
