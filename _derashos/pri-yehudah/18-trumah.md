“And the Eternal spoke unto Moses, saying, speak unto the children of Israel, that they take Me a heave offering: of every man whose heart impelled him ye shall take My heave offering.” “And let them make Me a sanctuary; that I may dwell in the midst of them” (Exodus XXV:1-8). This parshah teaches us the fundamental approach to Godliness and holiness. In the last sidrah we read: “and he (Moses) took the Book of the Covenant, and read in the ears of the people: and they said, all that the Eternal hath spoken will we do, and hearken” (Exodus XXIV:7). The point was made that to perform the Commandments is all that is required. And also that until Moses read to them the Book of Covenant which includes the social order too, then and only then did they accept it with the exclamation, “All that the Eternal hath spoken will we do, and hearken,” “NAASEH V’NISHMA.” Previously they said only “NAASEH,” “We will do.” But in today’s sidrah the Torah goes a step farther, namely, that deeds alone are not enough. It is also very important that the heart of the one who does a good deed should impel him to do so. As it is said: “Of every man whose heart impelleth him ye shall take My heave offering.”

After the “sidroth” where the Torah speaks of the Ten Commandments and the social order, comes today’s sidrah with the Commandment to build the sanctuary. The Bible student, when he reads of the plan of the sanctuary and of the precious furniture and utensils, bedecked with gold and silver and copper, may wonder why all of these mundane, material affectations? In a sanctuary, one would expect to see four empty walls, a separation from the outside world; and no ceiling, but a clear view of the sky, to give a oneness with God. But why all these decorations and sensuous things? Our sages give the answer to this perplexing question. They point out that scripture has it: “and let them make Me a sanctuary that I may dwell ‘B’TOCHAM’ among them. ‘B’TOCHO LO NEMAR ELA B’TOCHAM,’ the Torah does not use in the text the singular form ‘B’TOCHO,’ but ‘B’TOCHAM,’ ‘among them’; that is, ‘B’TOCH KOL ECHAD V’ECHAD,’ that God will dwell in the midst of everyone of them” (Sages).

By performing a Mitzvah, a Jew does not only obey God’s commandment, but he actually builds a place for God within his own self. The Torah and its precepts train and develop man’s character, man’s feeling, to a point where it may become difficult for him finally to do a wrong for the remainder of his life. In several places in the Torah we find the expressions “LO TUCHAL” or “LO YUCHAL,” “you will not be able” or “he will not be able.” For example, we read, “One from among thy brethren shalt thou set a king over thee, thou ‘LO TUCHAL’ will not be able to put a foreigner over thee” (Deuteronomy XVII:15). And again, “LO TUCHAL,” “Thou will not be able to sacrifice the Passover offering within any of the gates which the Lord thy God gives thee, but at the place which the Lord thy God choose to cause His Name to dwell in, there thou shalt sacrifice the Passover offering, etc.” (Deut. XVI:5-6). In other words, being trained to the importance of belonging to your people and of keeping that union strong and eternal, how then can you ever allow a stranger, not one from among your own people, to rule over you? And, how then can you separate yourselves from your people when they are celebrating in Jerusalem their day of independence, the holiday of Passover? We further read (Deut. XXI:15-17), “If a man have two wives, the one beloved, and the other hated, and they have borne him children both the beloved and the hated; and if the first-born son is hers that was hated. Then shall it be in the day that he causeth his sons to inherit that which he hath, that “LO YUCHAL,” he will not be able to make the son of the beloved the first born before the son of the hated, who is the first born.” Also in Deut. XXII:1-3, “Thou shalt not see thy brother’s ox or his sheep driven away and hide thyself from them. Thou shalt surely bring them back unto thy brother, etc. and so shalt thou do with every lost thing of thy brother’s which he has lost and thou has found.” “LO TUCHAL,” thou canst not hide thyself.” In Deut. XXII:13-19, “If any man take a wife, and go in into her and hate her, and lay wanton charges against her and bring up an evil name upon her, etc.,” when it was found that he did not tell the truth, the Torah says: and they shall fine him, etc. because he has brought out an evil name, etc., and “LO YUCHAL,” he will not be able to put her away all his days. All of these are excellent proof that one who trains his character through the teachings of the Torah can not possibly do or commit any wrong. Another example is “LO TITCHATEN BAM,” “thou canst not make marriage with them (speaking of the Cannanites),” for it does not say “LO TCHATEN BAM,” “thou shall not make marriage,” but “TITCHATEN” (BINYAN HITPAEL, SFAT EMETH).

The “MISHKON,” sanctuary, was also meant to be a training place for holiness. It was not a separate entity of holiness. Rather to the contrary. It is the people who keep the sanctuary holy. The Godliness that each one develops in his day-to-day living, reflects upon the sanctuary. In the meeting place of the sanctuary, people of different character get together and in time get to know each other. After that, wherever they have contact, in the street or in the home, they can easily observe what real holiness does to man. They can observe to what extent the righteous man lives in accordance with the teachings of the Torah; how much is such a man’s share to the sanctuary. Yes! One may give a great deal of money to the sanctuary, but that is not what makes it sanctified.

The Torah did not want to separate the sanctuary from everyday living, but the contrary. Its purpose was to make every material thing in life sanctified. Man’s physical necessities, even man’s pleasures, must beclothe themselves with “KEDUSHAH,” holiness. Therefore the “MISHKON,” the sanctuary, whose holiness is also derived from the holiness of the people who worship therein, had to have furnishings and utensils symbolic of what sanctification means to us. It is not the separation from life that makes one holy, but rather life itself. And everything connected with life must be sanctified. — Therefore the Torah points out: “ASHER YIDVENU LIBO,” “His share for the sanctuary must be of such, that his heart impels him to do it.” — The heart of man is the organ that desires all the material and physical pleasures of life. Yes! The HEART has to be trained for sanctification, and to give its share to the sanctuary. The mind of man has developed progressively since the time of Adam. But the heart of man has not changed.
