“This section is beautifully expounded in the Midrash of Rabbi Tanchuma” (Rashi) — “‘And Korach took’ — He betook himself to one side with the view of separating himself from out of the community so that he might raise a protest regarding the appointment which Moses had made of his brother to the priesthood. Thus is what Onkelos means when he renders it by ‘VEETPALAG’ — he separated himself from the community in order to maintain dissension” (Rashi).

Three kinds of tests were given in this Parshah in order to prove that Moses and Aaron were the right leaders chosen by God. One was the test with the censers. This rite of offering the incense was dearer to God than all the sacrifices (TANCHUMA TETZAVEH), but there was death in it, for through it Nadab and Avihu were burnt (Lev. X:2). The people had been warned of this — “And it shall come to pass that the man whom the Lord will choose is the holy one” — he is already in his state of holiness (not he will become holy), thus they were warned not to imperil their lives, for only he whom He has chosen will come out alive, but the others will perish (Tanch.).

The second test was God’s new creation as a means to destroy them, as it is said (Numbers XVI:30), “If a creation — an entirely new one the Lord will create, to slay them by a kind of death by which no man has hitherto died — that the earth open wide her mouth and swallow them ye shall know that they have provoked the Holy One, blessed be He.” The Rabbis gave the following explanation: “If there has been a creation, that is, if there was already a mouth to the earth from the six days of creation, well and good. If not, the Lord will create one now” (Sanh. 110a; Ned. 39b).

The third test was that of the twelve staves. As it is said (Numbers XVII:16-26), “And the Eternal spoke unto Moses, saying: Speak unto the children of Israel, and take everyone of them a staff, according to the house of their fathers, of all their princes . . . twelve staves. Write thou every man’s name upon his staff. And thou shalt write Aaron’s name upon the staff of Levi . . . And thou shalt place them in the appointed tent, before the testimony . . . And it shall come to pass that the man’s staff whom I shall choose, shall blossom . . . And it came to pass, that, on the morrow, Moses came into the tent . . . and behold the staff of Aaron, for the house of Levi, yielded a flower, and brought forth buds, and bloomed blossoms, and ripened into almonds.”

This parshah teaches us a lesson as to what must be the qualities of a man to become a leader. The wealth that a man possesses, is of no avail. Korah displayed a tremendous amount of wealth and that was of no help in making him the leader of Israel. Even cleverness is not of the greatest importance towards becoming a captain among men. Korah was also very clever, as Rashi said (Numbers XVI:7), but neither did that help him in his ambition. The real tests of leadership are those described in our parshah. Firstly he must be acceptable by heaven to be a man with perfect, pure thoughts. He must have no other motives but the welfare of his people. In the case of “KETORETH,” incense, when Nadab and Abihu brought the offering of incense they were burnt “BEHAKRIVAM ESH ZARAH,” they offered strange fire, that is, strange thoughts, and that was not acceptable to heaven. Also in our parshah, we note that those who followed Korah and brought incense were consumed by fire, as it is said (Numbers XVI:35), “And it came out a fire from the Eternal, and consumed the two hundred and fifty men that offered incense,” for they too had selfish motives in their quarrel against Moses and Aaron. But the same dangerous incense, when used by Aaron, helped to stop a plague. As it is said (Numbers XVII:11-15), “And Moses said unto Aaron, take the censer and put fire therein from off the Altar, and put on incense, and go quickly unto the congregation and make an expiation for them, for there is anger gone out from the Eternal; and the plague has begun.” Rashi comments: “This secret the Angel of Death communicated to him (Moses) when he went up into heaven to receive the Torah,” namely that the incense checks the plague.

Secondly, a shepherd of the people must be a normal person who does not reject nature and therefore is better equipped to understand the needs of his people. The second test, when the earth swallowed up Korah and his followers, proved that nature itself is made to revolt against leaders who are apart from nature. Yes, man was taken from the ground and shall return to the ground and this is natural. But for the earth to swallow men alive is not a natural thing. This is a new creation, apart from earth’s natural course. It is simply nature’s revolt against man’s rejection.

The third test, that of the staves, teaches us that a leader, even if he is acceptable to heaven and to earth, must be productive. His guidance must alleviate the plight of his people in every need. Only a very few men left a mark in our history as true leaders.

Korah was one of those who sought to become leader but failed to pass the tests. According to Rashi, he came to Moses with ridiculous questions: “If a house is full of books (Torah) do you still need a MEZUZAH on the door?” (The MEZUZAH contains a portion of the Torah). And “If the TALIS is made entirely of blue wool do you still need TZITZIS?” (in the TZITZIS there is a thread of blue wool) — Korah failed to understand that all the books and all the studies, if they do not become a part of our everyday living, will give us nothing. — The TZITZIS were given to us as a reminder to perform the other commandments “LEMAN TIZKRU V’ASISEM ET KOL MITZVOTAI” — Take away the TZITZIS from the TALIT, and you have nothing left as a reminder. Take away the MEZUZAH from the door, and though you may have a house full of books, you will have nothing to remind you how to live outside of your home. In our own time, we witnessed a people that was highly educated, with houses full of books, that did very little to teach their own how to live as human beings.

Korah failed in the test of heaven, namely that of proving that he had no other motives but the welfare of his people. His jealousy of Moses and Aaron was too vividly displayed. He also misunderstood that a leader has to be and live like a normal human being in order to be able to comprehend his people. He complained, “KI KOL HAEDAH KULAM KEDOSHIM U’BETOCHAM HASHEM UMADUA TITNASEU AL K’HAL HASHEM,” “The whole congregation is holy, wherefore did you rise above the congregation of the Lord?” He thought that a leader rises above his people. He failed to understand that it is the people who raise their leader above them. Moses and Aaron did not seek the leadership, they were chosen. Korah tried to purchase the leadership with his wealth and here again he failed, for nature itself revolted against his dishonest approach to that position. He also failed in the last test, that of productivity. He amassed great wealth, but the Rabbis tell us that the keys to his treasures filled up many wagons. That is to say, he did not allow anyone to enjoy his wealth. He kept all of it under lock and key.

May this parshah be a lesson for those who seek the leadership!
