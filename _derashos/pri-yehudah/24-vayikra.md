“And (the Lord) called unto Moses” — All oral communications of the Lord to Moses whether they are introduced by “DABER,” “speak,” or by “EMOR,” “say,” or by “TZAV,” “command,” were preceded by a call (to prepare him for the forthcoming address) (Siphra). It is a way of expressing affection, the mode used by the ministering angels when addressing each other . . . To the prophets of the nations of the world, however, God revealed Himself in a manner which scripture describes by an expression ordinarily used for denoting events of a casual character . . . as it said (Numbers XXIII.4) “And God happened to meet (VAYIKER) Balaam” (the term “VAYIKER” from the root “KARAH” is connected with “MIKRAH” which denotes “chance,” “occurrence” . . . (Rashi).

This parshah, as it continues, teaches us an extremely important lesson on a perplexing subject, namely: “KORBONOT,” “sacrifices.” The extent of the importance of this lesson is to be seen in the very few words at the beginning of our text. In the word “VAYIKRA,” “and He (God) called,” we find the letter “ALEF” smaller than the other letters, making the word to appear “VAYIKER,” meaning that God happened to meet Moses, who, being the most humble man, as it says: “The man Moses was very meek, above all the men who were upon the face of the earth” (Numbers XII:3), tried to hide the fact that God called directly on him on such an important law. — All through the generations, this parshah was used as the text for little children when they began to study the Torah.

The text at the beginning of the laws pertaining to “KORBONOT” reads as follows: “ADAM KI YAKRIV MIKEM KORBON LAHASHEM,” “If a man of you offer an offering unto the Eternal, etc.” The words “MIKEM” and “LAHASHEM” (Lit.), “out of you” and “for God” (shall be the sacrifice), are of the utmost importance to be able to grasp fully the meaning of “KORBONOT” called for by the Torah. After all, one would hardly think that our Torah, the code of the highest morality, would uphold an institution that could find its place only among uncivilized people.

There is a great difference between the “KORBONOT” brought by other people and those called for in the Torah. Most of the “KORBONOT” were to please a god so that he might grant a wish. But Raban Gamaliel the son of R. Judah the Patriarch said: “Do His will as if it was thy will that He may do thy will as if it was His will. Make thy will of none effect before His will that He may make the will of others of non effect before thy will” (Avot 2:4). In other words, the Torah and MITZVOT, including “KORBONOT” were given to us for the purpose of changing us into better people. When one brings an offering without its affecting, without its making an impression upon the one who offers it, such a “KORBON” is not desirous to the Lord. — Selfishness, the interest that one has in his own wishes, is the greatest factor towards the committing of evil. A man who tries to purchase favors of God, even through offerings, has not changed or improved his selfish character. It only shows how much of a price he is willing to pay in order to satisfy his wants. But our Torah calls for us to make our will “of none effect before God’s will,” and to do away with our selfishness, with our self-centered way of living. Every precept in the Torah is in a way a “KORBON” on our part. The observances curb our desires. When one commits a sin, he must bring a “KORBON” as a reminder that he fell prey to his own desires which he did not curb. The Torah points out that the “KORBON” has to be “MIKEM” and “LAHASHEM,” “out of you” and “for God”; it must make thy will of none effect before His will. The “KORBON” must be able to change you into an unselfish human being. Only then will it be desirous before the Lord.

After every “KORBON” scripture adds the words “a sweet savour unto the Lord.” Rashi comments: “When God beholds the ready performance of His commandments, He is pleased.”

No one ever lightly esteemed any kind of sacrifice, particularly the highest type, life itself, which has been offered for an ideal or for the sake of one’s country. But the Torah teaches us a way of life whereby we sacrifice some fleeting pleasures as life requires it, which may save us from bringing the highest sacrifice. In AVOT 4:1 we are told: “Who is wise? He that learns from all men. Who is mighty? He that subdues his (evil) nature. Who is honored? He that honors mankind. Who is rich? He that rejoices in his portion.” With all of these, one may sacrifice somewhat of his selfishness which in turn will lead him to a happier life, a richer life, a life worth living.

Creatures also know of sacrifices. A domesticated animal will risk its life for its master. But that is not in the category of “LAHASHEM,” it does not add to the ennoblement of life, to the ability to live and enjoy life without fear, without envy, without uncontrolled ambition that leads to destruction. — Yes! There are many people who do wonderful deeds without knowledge of the principles of the Torah. They do their noble work from the sheer goodness of their hearts. But when we search into their lives and the society in which they lived, we may find that their characters also were formed, directly or indirectly, by the teachings of the Bible.

In today’s HAPHTORAH we read (Isaiah XLIII:23) “Thou hast not brought Me the small cattle of thy burnt-offerings, neither hast thou honored Me with thy sacrifices. I have not burdened thee with a meal-offering, nor wearied thee with frankincense.” Rashi comments: “But for idolatry you did bring offerings. I did not command thee to offer sacrifices; it was voluntary on your part.” — Nowhere in the Ten Commandments is there any reference to sacrifices, and even when sacrifices are first mentioned in our parshah, the expression used is “when any man of you bringeth an offering,” the first word “KI,” literally “if,” implying that it was a voluntary act. Sacrifices were perhaps, as Maimonides suggested, a concession to Israel so that they would not imitate the idolatrous practice of the heathens around them and build altars to idols (David Kimchi). — All this will prove that sacrifices according to the teaching of the prophets, are not the end results but rather the means to a result. By bringing any of the sacrifices on the altar before God, man confesses and atones for his sin which was that he did not sacrifice some of his mundane pleasures for the will of God — and that alone is the end result of “KORBONOT.”

The “MESHECH CHACHMAH” brings a GEMERA in “MENACHOT.” “BEN AZAY” said that “in scripture when it speaks of the law of ‘KORBONOT,’ God’s name does not appear as ‘EL’ or ‘ELOKIM’ but only ‘HASHEM.’ Because the names ‘EL’ and ‘ELOKIM’ are also found to be ‘Angel’ or ‘A Judge,’ but ‘HASHEM’ refers to God alone.” That is to say that “KORBONOT” must be from “MIKEM” “out of you,” “LAHASHEM” “to God,” and not for any other perception even when it means fundamentally God, for we find sacrifices brought to the golden calf, though it was based on religious sentiments (see Drashah on PARSHAT TISA).

The “RAMBAM” (in BET-HABCHIRAH:2) says: “It is traditionally known that Adam was created out of the earth of the same place where David and Solomon erected the altar. That means: man was created from the place where he finds forgiveness for his sins.” — On the place from which earth Adam was created, stood the altar on to which “KORBONOT” were brought and on which the further development of men is dependent.
