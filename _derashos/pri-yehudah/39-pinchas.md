The character of Moses as loyal, honest and unselfish, is widely displayed at the end of the last parshah and throughout today’s. In the last parshah, we are told of an incident. One of the heads of the tribe of Simeon displayed a disgraceful act before Moses and before the children of Israel. He brought a princess of Midian into the encampment for adulterous reasons and asked Moses if it would be permitted. When Moses told him, “No, she is not permitted to you!” he rudely asked, “Then who permitted you to take Jethro’s daughter as a wife, she too was a Midianite?” — Moses, as the head and chief judge of the children of Israel, should have punished the man at once, for he had brought shame, and as the Torah tells us, a plague on the children of Israel. But Moses controlled himself (and was criticized by the sages), because he felt that since he himself was involved, it would be much better were he not the judge in this instance. His leadership might suffer. People might mistrust him and accuse him of acting from selfish motives, rather than for the welfare of the Israelites. Pinchas saw what happened and killed the man of Israel and the princess of Midian. Although by so doing, he committed a sin of being a “MORAH HALACHAH BIFNE RABO,” “making a decision in front of his chief,” still “BEMOKOM SHEYESH CHILUL HASHEM EN CHOLKIN KAVOD L’RAV,” “In a place where the honor of the Eternal will suffer, one is not bound to give honor to his chief” (Sages). — It seems to me that when Pinchas saw and heard what had occurred, he realized that by deferring to Moses’ opinion of his contemplated action, he would only cause difficulties. Since Moses himself was involved, Pinchas feared that instead of honoring his chief, deference would have the contrary effect, because Moses could neither give consent nor reject the act.

In today’s parshah we are told that the four daughters of Zelophchad stood before Moses, saying: “Our father died in the wilderness and he was not among the company of them that gathered themselves together against the Lord in the company of Korah . . . Why should the name of our father be done away from among his family because he had no sons? Give unto us a possession among the brethren of our father.” And Moses brought their cause before the Lord (Numbers XXVII:1-11). The sages comment: “SHENITALMAH MIMENU HALACHAH,” “He has forgotten the law, therefore he brought their cause before the Lord.” — Here again Moses felt that he should not be the one to decide in this case for what they told him sounded like a bribe to him. They said: “Our father was not among the company of them that gathered together against the Lord in the company of Korah.” As we well remember, Korah was the one who led the revolt against Moses and Aaron. Therefore Moses did not wish to make any decision in this case.

Further we are told: “And the Lord said unto Moses, get thee up into this mountain of Abarim, and behold the land which I have given unto the children of Israel. And when thou hast seen it, thou shalt also be gathered unto thy people, as Aaron thy brother was gathered. And Moses spoke unto the Lord saying: Let the Lord the God of the spirits of all flesh, set a man over the congregation, who may go out before them and who may come in before them, and who may lead them out and who may bring them in, that the congregation of the Lord be not as sheep, “ASHER EN LAHEM ROEH,” which have no shepherd” (Numbers XXVII:12-23). — It is noticeable that Moses did not say “B’LI ROEH,” “without a shepherd” but “EN LAHAM ROEH,” literally, “the shepherd is not for the people.” He may be tending himself, he may be doing it for his own honor and dignity, but for the people he is not a leader. God told him to ordain Joshua as his successor and leader of the people. Yes, Joshua, being the pupil of Moses, inherited all the aforementioned qualities from Moses. Here again was a display of great courage, honesty and complete unselfishness on the part of Moses and this serves as a lesson for all men who would lead.

The test of good leadership was given by Moses in a very few sentences: “Set a man over the congregation, who may go out before them and who may come in before them.” A good leader must be a true representative of his people, he should be able to bring honor to them. Selfishness and dishonesty displayed by him will reflect badly on his people. But this alone is not enough. To be used only as a display piece does not make one a commander among men. He must also be active, productive, as Moses said: “And who may lead them out and who may bring them in.” That means he must actually lead them in their relationship with the outside world, as well as in their conduct among themselves.

The parshah closes with the following: “And the Lord spoke unto Moses saying: Command the children of Israel, and say unto them: My food which is presented unto Me for offerings made by fire, of a sweet savour unto Me, shall ye observe to offer unto Me in its due season” (Numbers XXVIII:1-3). The Torah goes on enumerating the various KORBONOT for the different times. Reference is hereby made to the lessons of good leadership. A pilot of his people must be willing and able to bring offerings for their benefit. Acting helpless in making a decision of law, when he is bribed in any shape or form, is considered an offering. To be told that his leadership is ended, as was Moses, and to accept it in good faith, coldly, undisturbed, and to be able to give such a splendid suggestion as to the choice of the man who would be responsible for the future welfare of his people, takes great courage. The Torah points out: “My food which is presented unto Me for offerings made by fire of a sweet savour unto Me.” Fire has two functions, to destroy and to build. One must be extraordinarily careful with any kind of an offering. It may sometimes become the opposite of a sweet savour unto the Lord. It depends mainly on the “KAVONOTS,” the thoughts behind the “KORBON.” Also, the time and place have a great bearing on the kind of KORBON to bring. “AL TADIN ET CHAVERCHA AD SHETAGIA LIMKOMO” (Sages), “Do not judge your friend till you come to his place.” Yes, place has much influence on man’s behavior, and so do time and season affect man’s character. Therefore, the Torah, at the close of this parshah, enumerates the various KORBONOT, offerings, to suit the different times. A good leader must know all of these and act accordingly.
