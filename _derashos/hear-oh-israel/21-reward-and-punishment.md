REWARD AND PUNISHMENT

The concluding portion of the book of Leviticus is concerned with one of the fundamental teachings not only of Judaism but of many other faiths as well, with a doctrine that underlies its entire ethical system, namely that of reward and punishment. The Torah presents to its followers a long list of commandments and prohibitions, of duties to be fulfilled and acts, earmarked as reprehensible, to refrain from. For him who has implicit faith in his Maker, whose heart is upright with the Lord, his God, no explanations are needed, no reasons have to be given for the acceptance of the commandments and compliance with the prohibitions. It is sufficient for him that these orders emanated from his Father in Heaven to make him heed them. When the patriarch Abraham received the divine command to offer up his beloved son Isaac as a sacrifice, he asked no questions, but got up early in the morning, saddled his donkey and was off on his way to do his Master’s bidding. However not all human beings are constituted like the father of our race. The average person, when a request is made of him that is the least bit burdensome or contrary to his natural inclinations, wants to know why. And it was to forestall this reaction of the common man, of the overwhelming majority that cannot be classified as either perfect saints or incorrigible sinners, to the line of conduct prescribed by Israel’s Torah that the declaration was necessary that there is reward for virtue and punishment for vice. It was on this account that it had to be made clear that, for example, in spite of certain temporary setbacks, that it might entail, honesty was in the long run the best policy, while, on the other hand, whatever immediate advantages crime might appear to bring, it did not, when reviewed from the standpoint of its ultimate effects, really pay.[^1] Hence it is the part of prudence to do good and turn away from evil, to be zealous in the performance of duty and avoid transgression.

So important did the compilers of the liturgy of the synagogue consider the doctrine of reward and punishment to be that they incorporated it in the daily devotions. The Shema’, which is the Jew’s confession of faith that is recited every morning and evening, is followed by the paragraph beginning with the statement “And it shall come to pass if ye will hearken diligently to My commandments”[^2] dealing with this very subject and making Judaism’s position in regard to it clear beyond the shadow of a doubt. Every act of man’s is recorded as in a book, declared Rabbi Judah the Prince,[^3] while his predecessor Rabbi Tarphon assures us that, notwithstanding the brevity of human life and the lack of opportunity for the completion of tasks begun, there is nevertheless compensation commensurate with the extent of the accomplishments, “and faithful is thy employer to pay thee the wage of thy labor, and know that the (full) reward of the righteous will be in the time to come.”[^4]

But why make such a do to about this matter of reward and punishment? If it is true that virtue is rewarded and vice is punished, who would be so foolish as to choose what would result in nought but harm to himself while neglecting what would conduce to his benefit? It is not surprising to find an inexperienced child play with fire and get his fingers burnt. But that adults, who ought to know better, should be so reckless, that is almost inconceivable. Wherefore, then, should it be necessary for the Torah to try to impress upon the minds of its readers so simple a fact as the wisdom of good behavior, of virtuous conduct and of the folly of wrongdoing, lawbreaking and crime?

One answer that might be given to this question is that unfortunately the operation of the law of retribution in the realm of human behavior is not so obvious to most men as are the effects of fire upon the child that plays with it. The great interval of time that often elapses between the commission of crimes and the accounting for them creates the illusion that there is no accounting, that “there is neither justice nor judge.” As often happens in the case of offenses against the laws of the state, the offenders console themselves with the hope that they might escape detection and thus avoid paying the penalty for their trespasses. But this is pure self-deception. Everything in this world has a price-tag. “Everything is given on pledge, and a net is spread for all the living. The shop is open, and the dealer lends on credit, and the ledger lies open, and the hand writes, and whoever wishes to borrow may come and borrow. But the collectors make their rounds regularly every day and exact payment from man with or without his consent, and they have whereon to base their demand, and the judgment is true judgment, and everything is prepared for the banquet.”[^5]

This view has, to be sure, been challenged by the agnostics and sceptics in the past and will continue to be questioned by those of the present and the future. “How can you be so sure,” they ask, “that there exists such a thing as divine justice, when there are on the one hand many fine, decent people that suffer and, in contradistinction to them, scoundrels upon whom fortune seems to be perennially smiling? Where was God when Hitler carried out his atrocities and outrages against humanity? Why did He stand by in silence when six million of our brethren died a death of violence at the hands of his henchmen? Is it possible that all of those that perished deserved their fate? Granted even that many of them merited what happened to them, must there not have been among them a large number that were innocent?”

These arguments against the validity of the belief in reward and punishment seem almost irrefutable. To accept them, however, would be tantamount to agreeing that there is no hope of reward for virtue nor any need to fear the consequences of vice. What such an admission would lead to is frightening to contemplate. It would mean the collapse of the entire system of ethics set up by Judaism and its daughter religions. The result would be unrestrained license, uncurbed violence and bloodshed, such as characterized, according to the account of Scripture, antediluvian society, that came to an end in the great deluge of the time of Noah.

Rather than allow such a catastrophe to overtake mankind again the standard-bearers of Judaism preferred to attribute man’s misfortunes to his own imperfections rather than the apathy or impotence of deity. Since it is beyond doubt that “the evils as well as the good come to pass by the decree of the Most High,” said the poet who wrote the book of Lamentations,[^6] “I myself must be responsible for my present plight” As for God, “righteous is the Lord, for I have rebelled against His command.”[^7] A similar explanation of their lot was given by the Jewish martyrs of the time of Trajan. When that Roman emperor challenged the Jewish heroes, captured by him, as he was about to put them to death, to call upon their God to deliver them as He had saved Chananiah, Mishael and Azariah, the friends of Daniel from Nebuchadnezzar, the latter replied “Chananiah, Mishael and Azariah were perfect saints deserving of having miracles wrought on their behalf, and Nebuchadnezzar a real king worthy of such miracles. You, however, are only a commoner. As for us, we have incurred the penalty of extermination at the hands of the Infinite, and if you do not slay us, He has many other means for executing His will. You are only one of His agents. Be assured, however, that He will one day in the future exact payment from your hand for our blood.”[^8]

Jewish thought has also suggested other ways out of the dilemma. One is that “the reward for virtue is not to be expected in this world”[^9] This assumption must not be dismissed as an alternative of despair. For suppose man was rewarded for his good deeds during the time of his sojourn on earth, of what consequence would that be, seeing that life is so short? If the compensation is to match the effort, is it not better that it be made in a realm where it would be of much longer duration? When Rabbi Tarphon postponed the reward of the righteous to the future he was really not asserting anything new. He was merely repeating in other words what the psalmist had said long before him, namely “He that giveth lavishly unto the needy, his righteousness standeth forever.”[^10]

Finally there is the contention of Antigonos of Socho that true virtue, like genuine love, looks for no renumeration. “Be not like servants that minister to their master for the sake of receiving reward.”[^11] Since we are commanded: “And thou shalt love the Lord thy God”,[^12] what sort of love would ours be if we were to demand that every expression of affection on our part be requited with a handout. The fact is that virtue is in final analysis its own greatest reward. “The recompense of a good deed,” said Ben Azai, “is the good deed (itself)”.[^13] No amount of wealth or other material emoluments can quite equal the satisfaction that comes from the knowledge of a deed well done, of benefits conferred and services rendered. Nor is the totality of the transient pleasures of the world worth as much as the contentment of being at peace with oneself. It is better to die — and no man lives forever — with a clear conscience and a good name, than to be surrounded, at one’s departure from earth, by a multitude of ill-gotten gains, which have to be left behind anyhow, and the more one is compelled to leave, the greater the pain. That was probably the thought underlying the remark of Ecclesiastes “A good reputation is preferable to fragrant ointment and the day of man’s death to that of his birth.”[^14]

Let no one, then, take lightly or mock Judaism’s belief in reward and punishment. “Taste ye and see that the Lord is good. Happy is the man that taketh refuge in Him.”[^15]
