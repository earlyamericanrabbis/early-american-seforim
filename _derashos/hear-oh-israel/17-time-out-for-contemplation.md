TIME OUT FOR CONTEMPLATION

Philosophical speculation was not a forte of early Jewry in either the biblical or the rabbinic eras. It was in ancient Greece, during the sixth century B.C.E. that the first systematic thinkers made their appearance. Jews did not begin to occupy themselves seriously with philosophy until the Middle Ages, just about one thousand years ago, after three centuries of contact with the Arabs, who had become the heirs of the intellectual legacy of the Greeks, that had been transmitted to them by the Syrian monks after having been banished from the Byzantine empire by that empire’s obscurantist rulers. Yet, although the Jews were not particularly distinguished in antiquity for their philosophical bent, the impression they made upon the first of the philosophizing Greeks, who came to know them personally, was that of being “a race of philosophers.” They are described as such in the writings of Theophrastus, a pupil of Aristotle, who lived towards the end of the fourth century before the common era and the beginning of the third, and is the first Greek author to mention the Jews by their name of Ioudaioi. The reason why the Jews were looked upon as philosophers by the representatives of the people, who had given to the world the most outstanding men of thought, was because, as Philo of Alexandria put it, on every seventh day of the week, that is to say on their Sabbath when they rested from their physical labors, or, to use the phraseology of the Bible, from “servile work,’[^1] they delivered themselves up almost entirely to philosophizing and contemplation.[^2] The expression “philosophizing” used here is to be sure, as Professor Wolfson points out in his work on Philo,[^3] a misnomer. What the erudite religious leader of Alexandrian Jewry during the first century of the Christian era had in mind, when he employed that term, was the study and interpretation of the laws of the Torah, which are divided by him into man’s duties to God and his obligations to fellowman.[^4] The fact remains, however, that without the leisure afforded him by virtue of his desisting on the Sabbath from his regular menial activities the Jew would not have been in a position to devote himself to the spiritual exercises that were conducive to his intellectual growth and moral development.

In the eyes of the practical-minded, hard-headed, materialistic Romans, with their insatiable appetite and lust for power, the enforced idleness observed by Jews on every seventh day of the week was but a proof of laziness on their part.[^5] The more understanding and sympathetic Greeks saw in the periodic break by the Jews of their ordinary routine, for the purpose of dedicating themselves to an entirely different type of discipline, a sign of their belonging to that group of human beings whom they (the Greeks) regarded as the elite of their society, namely the men of intellect, the brains of the community, who were alone fitted, according to Plato, to be entrusted with the management of the affairs of state. In the “republic” of Plato the philosophers constituted, as has been noted previously, the ruling class, the only citizens of the state eligible for public office, whether it be of an executive, a judicial or legislative nature.

Western society in our day resembles more that of the Romans of two-thousand years ago than that of the latter’s Greek predecessors. Its economy is one that is geared to production, to output, to getting things done, so speed-ups and movement. Industry is its greatest virtue and efficiency the criterion, the measuring rod of all values. Among the traits most admired in Americans is that they are a hardworking people, a nation of hustlers Whatever may be the situation elsewhere in the world, in America there is no room for a lazy man. The celebrated dictum of Captain John Smith, the head of the first English-speaking colony on American soil, still holds good today. “He who does not work, shall not eat.”

This apotheosis of work, which is, one might say, the cornerstone of the American philosophy of life, has been adopted by the builders of the youngest and newest of the world’s democracies, Israel. The largest and politically most influential party in that little country has set down as the foundation of its Weltanschauung what they call “the religion of labor.” Work has become its ideal, the purpose of existence, the end to which life is dedicated. For a country that is still in the making, for a people that wishes to get ahead such an orientation is vital. In order to survive Israel must continue to grow and it cannot grow without effort. It is the seemingly boundless energy of this newcomer to the Middle East that has aroused the fears of its Oriental neighbors. The Arabs do have some ground to be afraid of Israel’s phenomenal progress, because they feel themselves congenitally incapable of maintaining Israel’s pace. This high regard for labor, though it has been somewhat exaggerated by the modern builders of Israel, is however really not completely new in Jewish ideology. It goes back to the time of the psalmist who praises him that supports himself by the toil of his hands. “If thou will eat the labor of thy hands thou shalt be happy and it will be well with thee.”[^6] It was commended by Ecclesiastes, who said: “Sweet is the sleep of the toiler,”[^7] while sloth was censured by the proverbist in the remark: “Go to the ant, oh sluggard. Regard her ways and be wise.”[^8] The teachers of the Mishnah and the Talmud, too, were of the same opinion. “Love work and hate lordship,”[^9] advised the sage Shemayah. “An excellent thing is the study of the Torah combined with some worldly occupation”[^10] declared Rabban Gamaliel the son of Rabbi Judah the Prince. “It is better to engage in the most menial kind of work rather than become an object of charity,”[^11] said another, while Rabbi Judah, the editor of the Mishnah, in a comment on the fourth of the Ten Commandments, remarked that just as it is was mandatory to rest on the seventh day of the week, so was it a duty to work on the six days preceding it.[^12]

But precisely because of the exalted place assigned to labor in the human economy, on account of its beneficial results, is it necessary to guard against attaching to it greater importance than is its due. Work is only a means of an end, not the end itself. There is nothing intrinsically virtuous in the drudgery of toil. The sweat of hard work is at best a necessary evil, an unavoidable step that must be taken to reach the goal. It is only primitive cultures that make use of crude instruments that wear out the bodies of the toilers. The mark of a highly developed civilization is the possession of labor-saving devices that make work a joy rather than a hardship; and such inventions are for the most part the result of the inspiration and intuition that come to man during his hours of leisure that afford him an opportunity to think and reflect. Creative work cannot be hurried. It must be given a chance to mature. But once it has matured, it is not only gratifying but also materially rewarding.

Illustrative of this point is the story of the electrical engineer who was engaged to locate the defect in the wiring of a house. He tapped in a few places, told the proprietor what was to be done and presented a bill of \$100. The owner was completely taken aback. “One hundred dollars for a few taps? Why, that is highway robbery!” “Yes,” answered the expert. “I am charging you \$100 — \$1 for tapping and \$99 for knowing where to tap.” The value of trained minds, of persons with know-how is receiving ever wider recognition not only in industry but in every walk of life. And this brings us back to the institution of the Sabbath and the uses of the leisure which it provides.

That periodic rest from the daily grind, relaxation from tension and regular vacations are an absolute necessity for man’s physical wellbeing is a piece of advice that no physician nowadays fails to offer to our hardworking, overambitious professionals and businessmen. But what do we do with our leisure time once we have it? How do many of us spend our vacations? Do we take time out for study or contemplation, for developing our minds, for increasing our stock of knowledge or bethinking ourselves about our way of life? There is little evidence of this in our famous and well-frequented winter or summer resorts When they are not on the golf-course or at the beaches, most of our vacationers while away the best hours of the day and the night playing canasta or bridge or gin rummy or pinochle or some other game of chance, that may give them momentary excitement but does not advance them intellectually and certainly does not make of them better men and women, let alone better Jews and Jewesses,

This is not the way of Judaism. It is not what our tradition has taught us The Sabbath, which has been most faithfully observed in the past by the complete cessation of all regular activities, all ordinary exertions for a livelihood, was never with us a day of purely physical rest. Though it has been asserted that “sleep on the Sabbath is a delight”[^13] the holy day was not intended to be frittered away completely in mental idleness. The freedom it afforded from worldly cares and mundane occupations was to be utilized for the Jew’s spiritual regeneration. “If Jews would keep only two Sabbaths properly, they would be redeemed,”[^14] remarked one of our preachers of old. If Jews are to survive as the bearers of the great civilization called Judaism they must again become what they were believed to be by Theophrastus, the pupil of Aristotle, “a race of philosophers.”
