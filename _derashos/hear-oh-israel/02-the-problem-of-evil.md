THE PROBLEM OF EVIL

The concluding chapters of the Sidra of Bereshith describe the progressive moral deterioration of the descendants of Adam and Eve, the first human couple to inhabit the earth, to whom God had given dominion over all the rest of His creatures. The first experiment in world-building had turned out to be a fiasco. When the Eternal, who had previously pronounced all that He had wrought to be “very good”,[^1] noted the great wickedness of man on the earth,[^2] He regretted that He had made him,[^3] and in the sadness of His heart He decided to “erase” the human race from “the face of the earth”.[^4] The consequence of this divine displeasure was the great deluge that swept over the globe and destroyed whatever was alive on it — all except Noah and his family and the animals that took refuge in his ark. With Noah the history of man begins all over again. And that era, too, would, as our sages observe, have ended in failure were it not for the appearance of Abraham, who made up for all the shortcomings of the generations preceding his.[^5]

This sinister development in the character of the human species, that is recounted by Scripture, has caused infinite embarrassment to theologians. If, as the psalmist expressed it, “God is good to all”;[^6] if, as it is emphasized in the liturgy of the Day of Atonement, it is in furthering the well-being of His creatures and their benefit that the Creator is interested, not their destruction and death — “for Thou de-lightest not in the death of him that dieth but that he turn back from his way and live”[^7] — how is one to account for the presence of evil in the world created by Him? And if by some inadvertence wickedness managed to obtrude itself, to muscle in as it were, why did not He, for whom nought is impossible,[^8] prevent it from lifting up its head? Why did He give the race of man a chance to become corrupt before he cracked the whip of discipline upon it? Why did He allow the process of putrefaction to set in and only then clean out the rot, when by issuing a restraining command at the beginning the course of decadence and decay would have been arrested before it had an opportunity to reach dangerous proportions? Above all, what explanation can there be for the indifference and silence of a God, who is characterized as “a king that loveth righteousness and justice”, while atrocities are being committed that cry to high heaven? “Wherefore, oh Lord, stands Thou afar off? Why dost Thou hide Thyself in times of trouble?”[^9]

The sordid realities, with which life on earth is often filled, has led those who thought about these matters to several different conclusions. One was that there really is no God, and if there is, He is not concerned with the fate of His creatures. He doesn’t care what happens to them. Nothing is handed to anybody in this world. Whoever wants something badly had better get it himself, for “af I am not for myself who will be for me?”[^10] Enlightened self-interest may caution us against proceeding too brutally in the pursuit of our objectives, because our fellow-travellers on earth might oppose us in the realization of our ambitions. Basically, however, it is believed by those who hold this theory that self-fulfillment is the motivating force behind all human effort and striving.

Others deduce from the fact of the presence of evil, in a world supposedly created by a just and righteous God, the existence of two diametrically opposite and opposing principles competing with one another. They see in the universe a perpetual tug of war between good and evil, light and darkness, between constructive and destructive elements. This was the doctrine taught by Zoroaster in ancient Persia. The world was, according to him, divided between Ahura-Mazda or Ormuzd, the personification of life and hight and goodness, and Ahriman, the principle of darkness and evil. It was up to man to align himself with the former and strive for his victory.

Neither of these two explanations of the presence of evil in the world were acceptable to Judaism. The first, which denied the existence of a divine Providence, watching over the affairs of men, would leave mankind completely at the mercy of chance and circumstance. If there be no God, whose seal is truth and whose throne is founded on justice, what assurance would there be that even the deeds man performs out of enlightened self-interest would be successful? It is the feeling a person has that he is not alone, when he does what is right and decent and proper, that even “when he walks through the valley of the shadow of death he need not be afraid because God is with him”,[^11] that fills him with confidence in the ultimate attainment by him of the goal of his idealistic strivings. Take away this certainty, this anchor of hope, and man becomes like a rudderless bark, exposed to the fury of the raging sea of life without any prospect of ever reaching a port.

As for the other hypothesis, the theory advanced by the Zoroastrians, although it has some merit —the Bible itself admits the operation within man of two contrary tendencies, the inclination towards the good and the leaning towards evil — that, too, is not satisfactory. For if the rule of the cosmos were divided between two such contradictory and contrasting authorities as light and darkness, good and evil, and life and death, there would be, instead of a universe, that is a unified world following one eternal law, a permanent rift. If there is any fact that science has tried to impress upon us — nay this is the fundamental assumption underlying the body of human knowledge that goes by the name of science — it is the certainty that the same rules of nature apply everywhere, that there are no exceptions, and if there be, there has to be a reason for these exceptions in the laws of nature itself. Since it is considered axiomatic that the world is one, the power behind it, the force or being that makes it click, too, must of necessity be one. The same God who formed the light also created darkness. He that “maketh peace” produced also the contrary of peace[^12] “From the mouth of the Most High,” said the author of Lamentations,[^13] “proceed the evils as well as the good.”

But is not this a logical contradiction? How can both good and evil originate from one and the same source, and what could have been God’s purpose, granted that He was the author thereof, in creating evil? There is much that has been and can be said on this subject. First of all it is well to remember that good and evil are not absolute but, like other adjectives, relative terms. There is the classic story of the Chassid, who, feeling very much cramped in his one-room cottage, applied to his rabbi for a solution of his problem. Instead of alleviating his congestion, the man of God ordered his devotee to board in his house his goat and his donkey. When, later on, the Chassid’s complaints became, and understandably so, more vociferous than they had been previously, his spiritual adviser commanded him to add to the residents in his home his chickens and whatever other livestock he possessed. In vain did the Chassid protest against this further encroachment on his living space. Orders of the rabbi were orders and they had to be obeyed. Finally, when the crowding in the already congested quarters reached the point of unbearability, the holy man permitted the desperate master of the household, that had been turned into a bedlam, to take the animals out again. Thereupon the Chassid’s sense of relief in being able to rid himself of these undesirable tenants was such that he could not thank the rabbi sufficiently for his wonderful advice.

God and evil are, then, relative terms. We never appreciate how well off we are until we compare our situation with that of others less fortunate than we. And so it is with everything else. In lands of almost perpetual sunshine like Mexico, for example, clear, bright weather is no particular cause for rejoicing. How can one get excited over cloudless skies when one blooming sunny day follows another? It is necessary to dwell in climes, where fog and must usually obscure the light of the sun, to value the brilliance of this luminary. Only by contrast with the evil can the good be recognized,

Secondly it should be borne in mind, as Maimonides points out in his Guide of the Perplexed, that many of the evils from which man suffers are self-imposed. Individuals who ignore the warnings of their physicians, who defy the rules of diet, who indulge in activities that are injurious to their health, have only themselves to blame for the consequences. God can certainly not be held responsible for the hatred of their fellows that many men heap upon themselves by their indiscretions, their arrogance, vanity, selfishness or greed and their failure to check their impulses.

Then again many of the things, that at first appear to be evil in men’s eyes, turn out to be really blessings in disguise. Rabbi Alaba recounts[^14] how he was forced, in the course of his travels, to spend a night in the open field because the townspeople had refused to put him up. The wind came and blew out his light. A cat ate the rooster he carried with him to wake him from his sleep and a lion devoured the donkey that served as his mount. That same night a band of marauders attacked the town and took all its inhabitants captive. If he escaped their fate, said Rabbi Akiba, it was due solely to the series of mishaps that had befallen him. Whence he concluded that “whatever God does is for the best.”

The Jewish physicians of Germany, who were the first among the professionals to be driven out of that country when Hitler came to power, would agree with Rabbi Akiba. They were certainly the luckiest among those whom antisemitism had compelled to leave the fatherland, for at the time, when they departed, they were at least able to take most of their possessions along with them — a privilege which those that followed later on were not granted.

Who can, therefore, foresee what is best for him?

> “J asked for wealth that I might be rich.
> God made me poor that I might be humble.
> I asked for health that I might be strong.
> God made me weak that I might be wise.
> I prayed for all things that I wanted.
> God answered by giving me what was most useful.”

The fact is that no excellence can be achieved without a certain amount of strain and at least temporary discomfort. Israel, observe the sages of the Talmud, has been compared to the olive because, like the latter, it produces the best that is in it only under pressure. The high civilization attained by the Athenians is attributed by an outstanding historian of our time to the ruggedness of their terrain, which compelled them to use their wits in order to eke out a livelihood. Privation and austerity are a part of the necessary toughening up process whereby soldiers are conditioned to bear up under fatigue of war and combat.
