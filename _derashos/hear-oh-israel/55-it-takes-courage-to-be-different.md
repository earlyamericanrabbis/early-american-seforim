IT TAKES COURAGE TO BE DIFFERENT

One of the basic teachings of Judaism, that runs through Hebrew Scripture like a silver thread, is the doctrine of Israel’s election, the idea that the Jews are God’s “chosen people”. That this honorific title, which the Bible conferred on the descendants of the Patriarchs did not put them in the position of a Herrenvolk, a master-race possessing the right to dominate and exploit the rest of humanity, such as the German Nazis arrogated unto themselves not so very long ago, is made abundantly clear by more than 3,000 years of Jewish history. Even during the few centuries in which it enjoyed complete political independence and national autonomy Israel was far from being outstanding in numerical strength, material resources or military power. In fact, according to the testimony of the Torah itself, the Israelites were “the least of all peoples”,[^1] often directly dependent on God’s bounty and in most periods of their existence poorly equipped with weapons of defense, let alone offense.

The proof of Israel’s chosenness was not its superiority in those qualities and assets on which men usually pride themselves, but rather its spiritual eminence. Israel became God’s “peculiar treasure” when it accepted at Sinai the role of serving as “a kingdom of priests and a holy nation.”[^2] This assignment imposed upon the erstwhile slaves of the Pharaohs, that had been liberated from their bondage by the divine Redeemer, the obligation of rising ethically above the common run of men, by maintaining moral standards higher than those that prevailed among the Canaanites, the Egyptians and the other nations with whom they came in contact. In short they had to behave differently from all the other families of the earth if they were to fulfill their mission, as it was was said “And I separated you from the peoples that you might be Mine.”[^3]

Of this distinctiveness and of the necessity of safeguarding and preserving it the best minds in Jewry were aware right from the very start. Moses already called attention to the lofty plane of Israel’s laws of social justice. “What great nation is there,” he asked “that has righteous statutes and ordinances like this entire Torah?”[^4] That this was not just the biased opinion of a patriot blinded by national chauvinism has recently been borne out by archeology. Less than a century ago an expedition of scientists digging in Iraq unearthed the “code of Hammurabi”, a contemporary of father Abraham. This set of laws, found inscribed in cuneiform writing on clay cylinders, represents the highest achievement of the jurisprudence of ancient Babylonia. The code exhibits striking resemblances with the ordinances contained in the portion “Mishpatim” of the Torah of Moses. This circumstance led many modern Bible students to believe that the laws of the Torah, which even according to the most conservative view were not promulgated until centuries later, were copied from the earlier Babylonian document. However the inference is completely mistaken. For it is only superficially, in subject matter, style and language, that the two codes resemble each other. When one looks beneath the surface at the philosophy underlying them, one cannot help noting the yawning gulf that divides them.

Israel’s Torah, for example, considers all men equal before the law. “There shall be one form of legal procedure for you. The stranger shall be as the native,”[^5] is the fundamental principle laid down in it, which has become the basis of modern democracy. The Babylonian code, on the other hand, makes a distinction between a nobleman and a commoner.[^6] While the former stipulates that whenever an injury has been caused to a human being exact retribution shall be carried out — “an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth”,[^7] the latter says: “If a man knock out the tooth of a man of his own rank, they shall knock out his tooth. If he knock out the tooth of a common man, he shall pay one third of a mina of silver.”[^8]

It is true that Mosaic law did not completely abolish the institution of human chattel slavery. Though the Israelites were forbidden to impress into service their fellow-Hebrews, they were permitted to acquire and make use of Canaanite slaves.[^9] But when they did, they were compelled to treat these bondsmen of theirs humanely. Though they were considered as their property, their murder was punishable by death,[^10] and if they caused them to lose an organ of the body — even if it be only a tooth — they were forced to give them their freedom.[^11] Canaanite slaves who had escaped from their masters were not permitted to be returned to them.[^12] This was a much better deal than the negroes received under the constitution of the United States of America prior to the Civil War, when the “fugitive slave law” was enacted, rendering compulsory the extradition by the Northern states and the return to their owners of negro slaves from the South, who had sought refuge in the North. What a tremendous step forward this biblical precept must have been in its own time!

Yes, Moses was right when he pointed to the superiority of the code he had brought to Israel over any previously established set of civil laws. That was why he was fully justified in cautioning his followers against copying the practices of the Egyptians, in whose midst they had lived as well as those of the Canaanites, whom they were to supplant, nor to “walk in their statutes.”[^13] Both these highly civilized nations of antiquity were guilty of abominations that were the direct cause of their disappearance. Israel was to beware of such perversions, so “that the land might not spew” it forth as “it was to vomit out the nation” that was before it.[^14]

This separatism, which was preached in the Torah, and which was alleged as the motivation behind the Mosaic dietary laws,[^15] the injunctions concerning the holidays,[^16] the purity ritual’[^17] and the regulations of sex morality,[^18] was further elaborated during the post-biblical period. Towards the end of the days of the Second Temple Jewish religious leaders passed a number of enactments the purpose of which was to set up a barrier between Jews and the rest of the world.[^19] And there was good ground for such precautionary measures. True, the ancient Greeks and Romans were in certain respects very much advanced above the Egyptians, Babylonians and Canaanites. One cannot help admiring, when one sees them, their literary masterpieces, their works of art, their organization and their scientific discoveries. But when one reads about their moral depravity, their dissoluteness and license, their cruelty and savagery, their utter lack of feeling, which is evidenced by allowing human beings to be torn to pieces by ferocious beasts for their amusement, one cannot blame Israel’s teachers for erecting walls of separation between their people and the heathen.

According to rabbinic law a Cohen (priest) was prohibited from marrying a convert to Judaism.[^20] The reason for this prohibition was that the descendants of Aaron, who served at the altar when the Temple was in existence, were not permitted by biblical law to take as their partners women whose reputation was in doubt.[^21] Now it was reliably reported by a man of the calibre of the Roman philosopher Seneca that the morals that prevailed in the society, with which he mingled, were so loose that he could not vouch for the virtue of any woman in the entire Roman empire.

I believe we have now devoted enough time to antiquity. What happened then may be very interesting, but after all what we are chiefly concerned with is our own age.

> “Not heaven itself upon the past has power;
> But what has been has been and I have had my
> hour.”

And in this age, too, Jews continue to foster and practice separatism in one form or another. They may be very broadminded and tolerant. They may have the highest regard for their non-Jewish neighbors and their beliefs. So long as they persist in cultivating a particularistic Jewish way of life and in working for the perpetuation of Judaism of whatever brand it may be, be it Orthodox, Conservative or Reform, they thereby as much as say that this way is superior to any other.

Why is Judaism worth holding on to? Because st teaches that life is better than death; because st believes that there is nothing evil about the body and sts appetites, because it asserts that whatever God has created was meant to be enjoyed; because st 48 opposed to extremes; because st insists that actions speak louder than words; that practice is more important than belief; because it feels that evil must not be accepted but resisted and overcome. Jews may have their faults. Who hasn’t? But they also have their virtues. They are by nature philanthropic. They are not given to violent outbursts, preferring peace to war at all times. Jews take their family responsibilities seriously. They have very high standards of sex morality; and though they are not teetotallers, they manage to remain sober. For all these reasons the Jewish group owes it to itself to keep itself intact; and the only means of maintaining its integrity is to practice separatism, to be different.

This aspiration and desire is constantly challenged by the diametrically opposite tendency toward assimilation, imitation, taking on the coloring of the environment and adjusting to it. The process can be traced throughout Jewish history. Moses had hardly turned his back on the children of Israel, after the revelation at Mount Sinai, to receive further instructions from the divine Lawgiver, than the rebellious people asked for and made itself a golden calf.[^22] The prophets engaged in unremittant war against the trend toward the idolatry of the Canaanites during all the days of the First Temple[^23] And what was it that compelled the Maccabees to rise in defense of the faith of their fathers, but the fact that so many of their contemporaries, led astray by the glitter of Greek civilization, had abandoned their hereditary beliefs and practices?[^24]

In our day, too, notwithstanding the increase in the building of synagogues and schools in our country, there is evident a steady decline in Jewish religious observance. There may not take place that complete formal defection from the Jewish fold that was so marked a phenomenon among the Jews of Central Europe, at the beginning of the 19th century. Nevertheless with the rise in the rate of intermarriage, from which no family seems to be immune, the hold of Judaism on the Jewish masses is certainly weakening, and, however optimistically inclined one may be, he cannot view the future without some apprehension if not actual alarm.

But why should this be happening if it is recognized that Judaism is so supremely worth-while? Why should the very people who plead most eloquently the cause of Jewish survival, who tell us that the Jewish religion must not be permitted to die out, that not only Jewry but mankind would be the loser if the Jewish way of life were to disappear, why do they sanction the abolition of those differences that were chiefly responsible for the preservation of the Jewish tradition? Are we not talking out of two corners of the mouth when, on the one hand, we praise the beauty of the Jewish home and, on the other, we scrap the Jewish laws of marriage that safeguard the sanctity of that home by declaring that these laws are no longer operative today? Is st not contradictory to speak of the importance of practice over against belief and then do away with most of the practical requirements of the Torah because of the personal burdens they impose and the sacrifices they demand from those who would observe them?

What is the reason for this sort of behaviour? The answer is not difficult to find. It is that it is much easier to follow the crowd than to be different. When, in Rome, you conduct yourself as the Romans do, no one singles you out as a target of abuse or ridicule. You are lost in the mass and that renders you safe and protected. Besides when you do what the majority does, you do not have to justify yourself. “Forty-million Frenchmen can’t be wrong.” What is good enough for so many, so you say to yourself, should be good enough for me. It is related that an Irishman once entered a synagogue on the afternoon of the Day of Atonement just at the time when that most highly prized of all honors, that of reciting Maftir Yonah,[^25] was being auctioned off. One member of the congregation offered \$40 for the privilege. A second put in a bid of \$50. Then the Irishmen decided that it was time for him to throw his hat into the ring, and he called out “Sixty dollars!” When a Jewish acquaintance of his, who happened to recognize him, tried to put him off by saying: “Pat, do you know what you are bidding for? This isn’t for gentiles. It’s for Jews only,” the Irishmen replied: “I don’t care. If it’s good enough for the Jews, it is good enough for me.”

On the other hand he who persists in going his own way is immediately called upon to defend himself, to give a reason for his position and point of view. The non-Jewish world has often been resentful of the Jew’s particularism. Those who remember the story of Esther, will recall that the most effective argument used by the Jew-hater Haman in his effort to turn the gentile population of the Persian empire against the Jewish minority in its midst was to tell them that the “laws of these Jews were different from those of any other people.”[^26] The alien, the stranger is usually viewed with distrust, whence springs dislike. That was why Judaism had to command its devotees to “love the stranger.”[^27] Only persons endowed with courage, a courage which stems from a profound conviction of the rightness of the cause to which they are dedicated, are able to withstand such criticism. People must have the blood of martyrs flowing in their veins to be willing to give up a life of ease for the sake of an ideal. Not all men are so constituted. Most are like that celebrated English man of letters and notorious roué, Oscar Wilde, who remarked on one occasion: “I can resist everything except temptation.”” When the young Maimonides heard in Morocco, where he was living incognito, about certain Jews, who, in order to escape persecution, had outwardly embraced Islam by pronouncing the sentence: “There is no God but Allah and Mohammed is His prophet,” he did not condemn these weaklings, who, to save their lives, had yielded to pressure. He chose rather to commend those hardy souls that preferred to endure every hardship rather than break — even formally — with the religion of their fathers, when by just saying a word they could have made life easy for themselves; and he advised all to seek refuge in countries where they could practice their creed openly and without fear.[^28]

Being a Jew means being different, and to be different is never a simple assignment. It takes courage, strength of will, determination, character. It must not be inferred, however, that there is something virtuous about being different in and by itself. To be different for the sake of being different is sheer contrariness. It is the behavior of a Moishe Kapoir. You have probably heard of this contrary individual, whose very nature was to do everything in reverse. Because everybody else stood up for the Shemoneh ‘Esreh (silent devotion), he recited it sitting down, and, whereas other people sat Shiv’ah in observance of mourning, he would stand. Judaism does not ask us to reject everything our non-Jewish neighbors happen to possess, because much of what they have and stand for may be very good. Rabbi Akiba, for example, greatly admired the modesty of the Medes[^29] and Rabban Gamaliel had nought but praise for the etiquette of the Persians.[^30] What we are asked to do is to be discriminating in our choice. When we see something wholesome, we should by all means accept it. When we note what is reprehensible, on the other hand, we must avoid it. The Talmud points to a seeming contradiction in the words of the prophet Ezekiel. One time he reprimands Israel for acting like the nations around it.[^31] Another time he charges it with failing to act in accordance with the practices of its neighbors.[^32] What he meant — and this clears up the difficulty — was that they imitated the corrupt among their neighbors but failed to follow the example of the virtuous among them.[^33]

The point to be remembered is that it is impossible to differ, when we feel that we are in the right, without being prepared to make some sacrifice, and that it is, because there are those who have the courage of their convictions and are willing to fight for them, that the world is able to progress morally. Once this fact is recognized, the criticism levelled against those, who choose to be different, on account of their differences will be borne with stoic fortitude.

> “It is easy enough to be prudent,
> When nothing tempts you to stray,
> When without or within no voice of sin
> Is luring your soul away;
> But it’s only a negative virtue
> Until it is tried with fire,
> And the life that is worth the honor on earth,
> Is the one that resists desire.
> “By the cynic, the sad, the fallen,
> Who had no strength for the strife,
> The world’s highway is cumbered today,
> They make up the sum of life.
> But the virtue that conquers passion,
> And the sorrow that hides in a smile,
> Is is these that are worth the homage on earth
> For we find them but once in a while.”

It takes courage to be different.
