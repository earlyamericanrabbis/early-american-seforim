WHEN ISRAEL REJOICES

The “days of awe” are followed by a period of wholehearted rejoicing. The seven days of Sukkot together with the two days of Sh’mini ‘Atzeret and Simchat Torah are designated in contradistinction to other festivals as “the time of our gladness”. Why of all Jewish festivals the holiday season of Sukkot should alone have been so characterized has been variously explained by the ancient preachers of the synagogue. One reason given for the boundless joy that the Jew feels on Sukkot is that he has successfully withstood his trial that lasted from Rosh Hashanah unt Yom Kippur, that he has come out from the judgment spiritually purged and purified and can begin the new year with a clean slate.[^1] Another cause alleged for his jubilation on Sukkot, more than on Pessach and Shabu‘ot, is that his harvest is now completely gathered in and he does not have to worry any longer lest drought or a hurricane or a flood destroy his crop. His fruits and grain and vegetables are safely tucked away in the barns and cellars. A further circumstance that is said contribute to the joyous character of this holiday is that its celebration is unmarred by any untoward incident, as is that of Passover, which commemorates the destruction of the Egyptians. Pesach is called “the time of our freedom”, because on it were our ancestors freed from the slavery of Egypt. Yet, although freedom is highly prized by us, because without it our spiritual development would have been impossible, we cannot help recalling that it was achieved only at the expense of the death of those who had us in bondage. Therefore our rejoicing on Pessach is not as complete as it is on Sukkot[^2] And there is a fourth factor that enhances the glamorousness of this holiday and that is the many symbolic expressions of happiness that it provides, such as the dwelling in the fragrant Sukkah, the procession in the synagogue with the four species of the festival bouquet, the palm-branch, the citron, the myrtle leaves and the willow branches, and the sweetmeats and other delicacies consumed at the table. That is why the rabbis applied to this festival the verse “a plentitude of blessings in Thy presence”, construing the word soda‘ as the numeral “seven” and taking soda‘ s’machot to refer to the seven religious symbols employed in the celebration of the Feast of Tabernacles.[^3]

This analysis by our sages of the causes making for the cheerful nature of the Sukkot festival affords one a fine insight into Judaism’s philosophy of life and especially its conception of happiness. It proves first of all that the Jewish view of the world is far from being a gloomy one. Despite the many misfortunes that have befallen him — and what people can point to a longer record of suffering and woe than the Jewish? — the Jew is far from being a pessimist. The Jewish calendar may have its weeks of mourning, but it also has its periods of rejoicing. The weekly Sabbath is a day of joy on which grieving of any kind is forbidden. “And thou shalt call the Sabbath a (day of) pleasure”[^4] says the prophet. Even a solemn festival like Yom Kippur is a day of brightness, of brilliant lights and festive attire. The disposition of the Jew, notwithstanding the perpetual wail that issues from the Jewish press, is at bottom a happy one. Aye, if he were not such an inveterate optimist, it is doubtful whether he would have been able to pull through until now.

The Jew’s happiness is, however, not of the hilarious, reckless sort, but a sober, rational type of joyousness. He does not find it necessary, in order to be in good spirits, to forget himself, to throw discretion to the wind, to dismiss from his mind duties and obligations. The sweetness of life does not consist for him of stolen waters and enjoyment is not equivalent with him to sin. On the contrary, when the Jew rejoices he wants to have reason for being glad, and in order that he may be justified in rejoicing his conscience must first be clear, his account with God and man must be settled. Then and only then does he feel free to celebrate.

A second qualification that determines the specific character of the Jew’s rejoicing is his realization that this rejoicing can be complete only when the entire produce of the field is gathered in. He cannot, like the revellers of Brussels in Lord Byron’s poem, deliver himself up to unrestrained pleasure at a time of crisis and say. “On with the dance, let joy be unconfined.”” He remembers that in this world there is no joy without sorrow.[^5] Therefore at the very moment when his cup of earthly bliss is full, under the wedding canopy,[^6] he breaks a costly object in order to inject a note of sadness into his merriment. And on Sukkot, the feast of joy, his happiness is restrained. He is ach sameach,' i. e., as our sages explain, joyful with a reservation, tempering his ecstasy over a rich harvest by removing in the midst of wealth and plenty to the frail, windblown Sukkah.[^7]

A third characteristic that distinguishes the Jew’s feeling of elation is that it is not a selfish, sadistic emotion. He cannot rejoice all by himself. He cannot be jovial when others are sad. His self-preservation has at times necessitated the extermination of his enemies. Yet it was not the death of the evildoers that he celebrated but the cessation of their evil. This attitude is strikingly illustrated in an anecdote related in the Talmud about Beruria,[^8] the learned wife of Rabbi Meir. This celebrated sage had been very much annoyed for some time by certain rowdies, who lived in his vicinity, so much so that he wished they might die. Whereupon his virtuous and gentle wife called it to his attention that the wording of the psalmist, in praying for the eradication of evil from the world, is not yitamu chot’im min ha’arez, “Let sinners cease from the earth”, but yitamu chata’im min ha’arez[^9] which can be construed as “Let sins cease from the earth”, and that it was the latter that he ought to ask of the Almighty.

And there is a fourth distinguishing feature that marks out Jewish joyousness, namely that the highest form of human happiness is conceived not as being the result of the realization of personal ambition, but the fulfillment of duty. His supreme joy is the simchah shel mizwah,[^10] the satisfaction he derives from carrying out God’s commandments. Since Sukkot is the holiday that affords him more opportunities for the performance of such obligations, therefore, is it, in a wider sense than any other festival, “z’man simchatenu[^11] an occasion for rejoicing.

This is how our teachers interpreted the meaning of this holiday. It is a message of incalculable benefit for a reckless, pleasure-mad, selfish world. If men would but listen to its wisdom, they would not seek solace for their troubles in drink, excitement, and other such diversions, but find it in setting their houses in order and making the best of life. Realizing how fleeting and shortlived even the greatest pleasures of this world are, they would be modest in their demands and content with little. And furthermore they would not build their happiness on the misfortune of others, but rather take pride in the good they have been privileged to do to fellowman, in the services they have been able to render, for such accomplishments are the truest sources of human satisfaction. When one can rejoice before God,[^12] when a human being can say: “My conscience is clear because I have done my full duty,” then he experiences genuine happiness. “And thou shalt be only happy.”[^13]
