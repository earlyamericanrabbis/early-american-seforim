PHILOSOPHY OF HISTORY

The event to which the glamorous Jewish festival of Chanukah calls attention is one of those great moments in the annals of the Jewish people, that constituted a turning point not only in its own history but in the history of mankind, of the entire human race. The victory of the Maccabees over the armies of Antiochus of Syria assured the survival of the Jewish religion; and Judaism’s ability to withstand the onslaught of Hellenism made possible, in turn, the emergence of its two offsprings, Christianity and Islam, which in the course of several centuries brought into their orbit one half of the society of man. Whether the roots, struck in the hearts of those who profess to be followers of these three monotheistic faiths by their ethical teachings, have sunk deep into their being or not, whether their influence upon their conduct and character has been profound or only superficial may be questioned. What is certain beyond the shadow of a doubt, however, is that but for what happened on the battlefields of Judea some 21 centuries ago the history of Western civilization would have taken an entirely different course than it did and that the human world would not have had the look it has today.

What was it that enabled the comparatively small band of virtually unarmed Judean insurgents under the command of Judas Maccabeus to triumph over enemy forces vastly superior to them in numbers, training and equipment? Whence did the weak few derive the power and means to defeat the mighty and many? How is the remarkable outcome of the uneven contest to be explained? Secularist historians, in their attempt to account for the unexpected windup of the Maccabean uprising, tell us that the reason for its success was that its first leader Judas was a born general. His native resourcefulness as a strategist more than offset the advantages of the professional military commanders of the armies thrown against his irregulars by Antiochus Epiphanes and the latter’s successors with their orthodox tactics. Also the Jews, despite their inferiority in numerical strength and arms, had their heart in the struggle, which the Greeks did not. The former were fighting, as Judas is reported to have expressed it by the First Book of Maccabees[^1] “for their lives and their laws”, “for their people and the Holy Place,”[^2] whereas the mercenaries hired by the Syrian rulers were lured solely by the prospect of booty and other such material rewards. Finally, they say, it must be remembered that the Hellenism that was current in the part of Asia Minor, that had been conquered by Alexander the Great, was a debased form of the once glorious civilization produced by Greece. It was the outer veneer of that great culture that the Orientals, who had become a part of the Greek-speaking world, had assimilated without the inner spirit. For such decadence the virility of the Jews was more than a match. That is why, according to these historians, the handful of Jews won out against the overwhelming odds that confronted them.

The Jewish authors of the apocryphal books of the Maccabees as well as the liturgists, who composed the prayers “For the miracles” and “In the days of Mattathias”, that are intercalated in the silent devotion as well as the grace after meals on Chanukah, had a different explanation of the matter. As they saw it, it was not Judas Maccabeus’ military genius, nor the desperation of his followers, nor the moral degeneracy of their opponents, important though these factors may have figured to have been in the overall results, that were ultimately responsible for the extraordinary feat of the Maccabees. It was God who wrought salvation for Israel by crowning with victory the valor of the first warriors, in the history of mankind, for religious liberty. It was He who endowed them with heroism, and because He stood behind them, did the battles they fought end in triumph for them. That is why it is to Him and Him alone that thanks are due “for the miracles and the deliverance and the heroic exploits and the acts of salvation and the wars, that He had waged for our forefathers in those days at this time.”[^3]

This view accords with the philosophy of history that underlies the historiography of the whole of the Hebrew Scriptures, beginning with the account of the creation of the world in the book of Genesis and up to and including such records of the events of the days of the second commonwealth as are contained in the books of Ezra, Nehemiah and Chronicles. In all of them the chief actor, the most important of the dramatis personae, about whom everything else revolves, is no one but God. He is “the man of war, who cast the chariots of Pharaoh, the horse and their riders into the sea.”[^4] Like the conductor of a mammoth marionette show He stands behind the scene and manipulates the nations of the world as though they are puppets in his hand. “Behold the nations,” says Isaiah, “are as the drop of a bucket. They are counted as the small dust of the balance.”[^5] “They are as nothing before Him.”[^6] The individuals or nations that temporarily wield power are merely the instruments of His will, the means whereby the divine master plan is executed. Thus Assyria, which put an end to the idolatrous kingdom of North Israel, is described by the prophet as “the rod of God’s anger”,[^7] sent against the ungodly nation, while Nebuchadnezzar, the king of Babylon, who destroyed the first temple of Jerusalem, is referred to by Jeremiah as “God’s servant.”[^8] However the one as well as the other were destined to pay heavily for their arrogance when the heavenly judge would have finished making use of them, when their mission as castigators of His people had been completed.[^9]

Is this nothing more than religious mysticism, an invention of the poetic fancy, of the same order as the naive mythology brought forth during the infancy of the human race, only on a more grandiose scale? Then why cannot the game of war be played with the precision of a dress tournament? Why should there be in every contest of arms a number of imponderables that render the issue uncertain? “How is it possible,” asks Israel’s lawgiver in his swansong to his people, “that one person should pursue a thousand and two put to flight a myriad” — as it has been seen to happen recently in modern Israel’s Sina: campaign — “unless their Rock had given them over and the Eternal had delivered them up?”[^10]

Such denouments in the dramas of history cannot be pure chance. There never was a more efficient war machine than that which the Germans had forged in preparation for the last world war. Nor could it be said that the Nazis were lacking in material resources, for they had at their disposal for a while all the wealth of Europe. As for the morale of the Wehrmacht, it was, at the beginning, at least, as high as that of any fighting force could be. For the overwhelming majority of the German people there was nothing more worth living and dying for than the Vaterland. Nevertheless they lost the war. Why? If you ask the German military leaders, they will tell you that it was due to a series of unfortunate accidents. It just happened that he weather conditions on D Day favored the Allied invaders. If only Hitler’s luck had held out a little longer, the forces of the Allies might never have broken through and the war would have ended quite differently from the way it actually did. The truth of the matter is, however, that the difficulties that the seemingly invincible army of Hitler came up against were not unique in human history. It was exactly the same type of situation that confronted the kings of Canaan with their horses and chariots, that were pitted against the virtually unarmed Israelites at Megiddo in the days of Deborah. A heavy downpour of rain, so Scripture relates[^11] disabled the 900 horse-drawn vehicles of Sisera,[^12] so that they and the host of 10,000 footsoldiers[^13] became an easy prey for the Israelite defenders.

These are what are called “imponderables.” But it is precisely these imponderables, that no military expert can foresee and that set at nought the most perfect and scientifically worked out plans, that he who believes in Providence, in the rule of supreme justice, attributes to God. “The Lord bringeth the counsel of nations to nought. He maketh the thoughts of peoples to be of no effect.”[^14] That was how the prophetess Deborah explained the victory of the Israelites of her time. “From heaven they fought. The stars in their courses fought against Sisera.”[^15] And she concludes her poem with the firm conviction: “Thus will all Thine enemies perish, oh Eternal, but they that love Him will be as the sun when he cometh forth in his might.”[^16]

It is this sort of faith that heartens the fighters for the right who find themselves temporarily in dire straits, this belief that the affairs of men are not abandoned to blind chance but that there operates in the world a divine law, which eventually causes justice to triumph, that sustains them in their struggle and gives them strength to carry on until victory is theirs, even as it did in the case of the Maccabees. Let then the modern Maccabees not despair. “Might is not forever — not even that of Russia — nor will the crown — of her underlings — endure unto all generations.”[^17] We have been assured that “the Lord will not cast off His people, neither will He forsake His inheritance.”[^18]
