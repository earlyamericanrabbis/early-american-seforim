“SILENCE THE ACCUSER!”

The purpose of the Day of Atonement is, as its name indicates, the at-one-ment or reconciliation of man with his Maker. The response to the former’s plea of “Forgive, I pray Thee!” is God’s answer “I have forgiven in accordance with thy request.”[^1] How this is accomplished is portrayed in the Agada of the Talmud as follows. Two attorneys stand before the heavenly tribunal. One, the Kategor or prosecutor, presents the case against man. The other, the Sanegor or defending attorney, speaks in his defense. The defendant’s virtues and vices are placed on two separate scales of a balance. If the former outweigh the latter, the scales of justice are tipped in his favor and the verdict is acquittal. If they incline in the opposite direction, he stands convicted.[^2]

The assumption underlying the parable is that no human being can claim to be perfect. “There is no man on earth so righteous as to do only good and never sin.”[^3] Each has his faults. The question is only: Does the good in him predominate over the evil or vice versa?

Not only individuals, peoples too are judged by the ratio between their merits and their demerits. The sages of the Talmud[^4] speak of a day in the millennial future, when all the nations of the world will be brought before the bar of divine justice and each will be called upon to give an accounting of its deeds, of its constructive accomplishments or the lack thereof during the time of its sojourn on earth. Numerical size, the extent of the territory occupied or of the power wielded by those being judged will be of no moment whatever in the appraisal. All that will be taken into consideration will be the spiritual contributions, the positive impact made on the human conscience, the refinement of character, the ideals bequeathed to posterity. “Have you produced anything like Israel’s Torah,” the heavenly judge will ask. “Have you fulfilled its commandments?” Measured by these standards, Israel, though it is the least of all nations, will emerge from the trial ace-high, leading all the rest.

This is, of course, pure poetic fancy, and a biased one at that. What else could one expect to find in a book like the Talmud, composed by Jews, for Jews, than a picture favorable to Jews? And yet this parable has a profound bearing on our time, on the era of momentous happenings in which we are living, an era of world-shaking events and radical geographic changes following each other in rapid succession, an age in which states are being made and remade, in which empires are dying and others spring up to take their place.

One of the most remarkable occurrences in this revolutionary period in the annals of the human race was the birth of the modern state of Israel. It was remarkable because it did not come about in the usual manner in which states are founded — by the violent overthrow of government or the illegal usurpation of power. True, once it had come into being, the newly born commonwealth was compelled to fight desperately for its right to exist. However the first impetus to its emergence on the arena of history was the voluntary consent and the free and unbridled vote of two thirds of the United Nations, representing the considered will of the overwhelming majority of the human race in body assembled. It was as though the peoples of the world had, in a moment of genuine remorse, decided to make belated amends for an historic injustice of nearly 2000 years’ standing. Mankind had for one brief instant of time risen to the moral heights of following the dictates of its conscience, which demanded that the homeless wanderer among the families of the earth be permitted to return to his ancestral estate.

This happened on the 29th of November of the year 1947. Since then the leaders among those, whose support and influence were chiefly responsible for the consumation of Israel’s statehood, have apparently had a change of heart. The clear and unmistakable policy during the past several years of not only Soviet Russia, which has — except for a very short interval before and after the proclamation of Israel’s independence — consistently been an enemy of Zion, but the socalled “friendly” powers of the West, has been to abandon Israel to its fate. They did this by not granting Israel permission to acquire, even by outright purchase, the means of defense against hostile neighbors, that had been receiving as free gifts all the arms they asked for, arms which they had made it known would be used for but one purpose — the extermination of Israel.

This reversal of attitude on the part of Israel’s former sponsors and friends is not due to anything the infant state had done or failed to do. It is not on account of any actual and flagrant violations of the charter of the United Nations that it is being discriminated against, or for some daring unprovoked act of aggression that it is being punished. Whatever crime of such a character might be charged against Israel was more than matched by its neighbors. The sin that is being visited upon the only true democracy in the Middle East is of an entirely different character. Its nature becomes apparent upon a glance at the map. It is its smallness, its territorial unimportance, its geographic insignificance, its lack of resources of economic value, the absence of any firm ties binding it to any other state or combination of states representing powerful interests or military might, which has to be reckoned with, that constitutes Israel’s chief offense. “How can we” — that is the stock argument of all ministries of foreign affairs, whether they be those of Britain, France or our own Department of State — “afford to antagonize the owners of the world’s richest oil supplies, whose domains stretch over a vast terrain covering a sizable proportion of the earth’s surface, just in order to give consideration to a little two-by-four state that has nothing to offer us except trouble? How can we, even on purely moral grounds, ignore the wishes of 45,000,000 Arabs and their many times over that number of Moslem sympathizers, so that we might accede to the demands, however justified they might be, of a million and a half Jews? Isn’t majority rule a fundamental principle of democracy? Isn’t the contribution to the happiness of the greatest number the highest goal of social justice?”

It is difficult to answer such an indictment, to refute charges of so formidable a character. Israel may be as innocent as a lamb. But is it fair to sacrifice the welfare of the entire world merely in order to uphold the integrity of a country with a total population of less than two million souls? Or should American or Englishmen or Frenchmen be asked to die in order to preserve Israeli independence?

By the ordinary rules of arithmetic Israel would on all counts, whether guilty or not guilty, be doomed. But history does not operate by these rules, nor does God follow them. “The race,” said Ecclesiastes, “is not to the swift nor is the battle unto the brave.”[^5] “There is nought to prevent the Lord,” remarked a Jewish hero of old, “to save by many or by few.”[^6] The truth has nothing to do with numbers. An entire world was wrong about the question as to whether the sun revolved around the earth or vice versa, until one lone man came along and set it right. Though, therefore, the rights of the majority unquestionably take precedence over those of any minority, they must be rights, or the entire moral structure upon which society is built falls to the ground. Size, territorial extent, military power — these do not count on judgment day. All that matters is right. Israel is, by virtue of its very diminutiveness, the test of the strength of the ethical principles to which the great powers pretend to adhere. It is, as Judah Halevi put it, “the heart of the world, that registers the soundness of the entire organism.”[^7] A chain, it has been said, is as strong as its weakest link. Acquiescence in the infraction of any equitable international agreement, however small and unimportant the segment of humanity affected thereby may be, is an invitation to bolder and more widespread and further-reaching violations.

Mankind should have learned this lesson from the first world war which was brought upon it by the attitude that “a treaty was only a scrap of paper.” It should have recognized this truth after the second world war, which could have been avoided if its instigator had been stopped, when he began by picking on his defenseless Jewish subjects, whose persecution was dismissed as an internal affair, and then proceeded to gobble up piecemeal, one after another, his country’s smaller and weaker neighbors. It ought to be apparent now that the dictator of Egypt, who started by preventing Israel-bound vessels from passing through the Suez Canal, has ended by seizing that important international artery of communication altogether. By failing to stand behind Israel when it first protested against the breach of international agreements involved in the attack on Israeli shipping, the West has not only not succeeded in winning the friendship of the Arabs. It is even in danger of losing access to the vital deposits of oil located in their countries because it has exposed to the Arab blackmailers its moral weakness. It has shown them that it is subject to threats of force, that it stands ready, whenever it might seem profitable, to sacrifice principle for expediency. So they hold out for an ever higher ransom, and in the end, the kidnapped child may be slain anyhow.

But it is not only because of what it would do to undermine faith in the integrity of the “free” world that the annihilation by default of tiny Israel would be a major human catastrophe, but also on account of what the little state symbolizes as the hope of the downtrodden and the oppressed and what it has accomplished during the ten short years of its existence for the realization of that hope. Whoever has not witnessed the misery of the Jewish inmates of the foul pestholes of such Mellahs as that of Casablanca in Morocco, and afterwards seen how the living corpses, that used to walk about in what resembled mass graveyards, come to life again by the magic touch of the freedom and the stimulating, bracing atmosphere of the land of the fathers, has never beheld a miracle. Let us be truthful with ourselves. Israel is far from perfect. It has its bureaucracy, its vested interests, its politicians, and, because there are people in it that dabble in politics, its measure of corruption. The Yishub can no longer claim, as it did in the days of the mandate, that its jails are empty of Jewish criminals. Nor is every act of retaliation carried out by the guardians of its frontiers morally justifiable. Last but not least, there is much to be desired in the observance of Judaism’s religious traditions in the land which is the cradle of the Jewish faith. In many respects Israel resembles other states of comparable size and status.

But it is also different. What other community, for example, has given refuge to so many homeless strangers, who were, besides their penury, physical and nervous wrecks, and made of them healthy, wholesome human beings? Where in the world is so much loving care devoted to children? Where is so much done with so little to improve the lot of the common man, of the masses? Where is such intensive intellectual activity, so much social and economic progress, such strides forward in the development of agriculture, commerce and industry on record as in Israel? Israel boasts today one of the lowest infant mortality rates, the proportionately highest school-population and a literary output per capita that is without equal. It represents in its totality the noblest experiment in human planning and integration. It shows what can be accomplished with the raw material of humanity provided the will is there and the means are made available. The world would be the loser if Israel, that first gave it its moral law, would be no more. It must not happen.

But what is there to be done? By what means shall we silence Israel’s accusers? How can we convince the world that Israel must lve? How can we assure ourselves that Israel will survive? Look at what Israel has against it — the Arab states, the Moslem world, Russia and, to top it all, the most powerful interests in the United States of America. Verily Israel’s position is like that of David when he exclaimed. “Lord, how many are mine enemies, how many those that rise up against me, that say concerning my soul: There is no salvation unto him in God.”[^8] “All nations have surrounded me...surrounded me Ike bees.”[^9] Even that tough little prime minister, Israel’s most popular leader, plucky, fearless Ben Gurion is no longer so sure of himself as he once was. That is why he is opposed to preventive war. Oh, he has no doubt that Israel would give a good accounting of itself if it had to face, even now, the entire Arab world alone. But what if the Arabs’ recently won Communist friends send volunteer troops into the Arab countries, as they have already provided them with modern arms? Would Israel be in a position to match such forces?

In answer to this last question it might be well to cite a homily contained in the Pesikta of Rabbi Kahana.[^10] It is an interpretation of that extraordinary dream that the patriarch Jacob dreamt on his way to Aram Naharaim, the dream of the ladder reaching unto heaven with the angels running up and down on it[^11] He was in very low spirits at that time, a penniless exile, fleeing from the wrath of his brother Esau, uncertain of the morrow, not knowing even where his next meal would come from and what dangers he might encounter on the road. In the dream he dreamt that night, his whole fate and future, that is to say the history of his descendants who were named after him, was, according to the Midrash,[^12] revealed to him. He saw the angels, who were the genii of the different peoples with whom they were destined to come in contact in the course of their long career in the world, go up the ladder and come down again. The one of Babylonia, he noted, climbed up seventy rungs before his descent, the genius of Media fifty-two rungs, the one of Greece 180 rungs — the number of rungs corresponding in each instance to the number of years of Israel’s subservience to the respective peoples. Then he beheld the genius of Rome ascending to the dizziest heights without stopping, as it were, and he became frightened. “What will happen to my poor children,” he asked himself, “when the Romans will take over the government of the world? Will they ever be redeemed from the hands of these tyrants?” But God reassured him. “Be not dismayed, oh Israel.” “Even if thou, (Esau,) liftest thyself up as the eagle and placest thy nest among the stars, from there will I bring thee down, saith the Lord.”[^13] Roman rule, too, though it will have lasted longer than other, will eventually come to an end. Then the Almighty ordered Jacob to go up the ladder. But Jacob was afraid. “If I go up, is it not possible that I may have to come down again as they did?”[^14] Again God tried to comfort him by telling him that his case would be different. Once he would rise to eminence, he would never descend anymore. But Jacob was lacking in faith. He refused to make the attempt. In punishment therefor God informed him that his descendants would be subjected to the nations of the world for many, many years.

The moral of the homily is obvious. The coalition against Israel, as history has proven, is not perpetual, everlasting. Even the mighty Roman empire, which endured for five whole centuries, finally fell apart, and the descendants of the Jewish inhabitants of vanquished Judea, of “Judea devicta,” witnessed its downfall. “Fear not my servant Jacob, saith the Lord, and be not dismayed oh Israel. For I will save thee from afar, and thy offspring from the land of their captivity.”[^15] Who knows whether it was not on account of craven fear that Jewry did not attain sovereignty for 2,000 years, whether that was not the reason why the Jew remained so long a slave of slaves?

The situation has now changed. The Israeli of our time is made of a different mold from that of his predecessors in the Diaspora. He is fearless, unperturbed, daring, bold. This fact impresses itself on anyone who visits modern Israel and notes the spirit of its people. It is related that a non-Jewish correspondent of a large American daily happened to be near the Gaza strip at the time when the mutilated body of a twenty-one-year-old settler of the border colony of Kissufim, who had been dragged into Egyptian territory and killed, was returned to the village. It was a ghastly sight, and the correspondent, in order to complete his account, decided to interview the mother of the slain youth, who was the widow of one of the fighters in Israel’s war of liberation, in Tel Aviv. As she told him, without a quiver in her voice, the story of her life and her struggles, what a wonderful son the dead hero had been and how he had vowed, after his father’s death, to guard Israel’s frontiers, the younger brother of the deceased, a boy of ten, who was standing by and listening, looked up to his mother and asked: “Mother, when will I be big enough to take Uru’s place?” And the correspondent, moved to tears, said to himself: “Such a people will never be destroyed.”

However it be, faith, self-confidence is an essential ingredient for Jewish survival. There have always been some Jews who possessed such faith, who believed literally in the promises of the Almighty, to such an extent that they dared to hold the divine Master of Fate to His promises. One of them was Rabbi Lev: Isaac of Berdichev, a Chassidic saint of the nineteenth century. On a Yom Kippur eve like tonight, this champion and pleader of the cause of Israel decided to have a debate with God, a showdown, a “Dm Torah mut Gott.” No, it was not sacrilege. Did not the prophet Isaiah, too, picture the Eternal as saying to Israel: “Come let us reason together. Though your sins be as scarlet, they shall become white as snow. Though they be red like crimson, they shall become as wool”?[^16] So he, too, that is Rabbi Levi Isaac, asked God: “What do you want from your people Israel? Why are you perpetually harassing Israel? Has not Israel carried out your commandments as faithfully as any other nation of the world? Has it not obeyed your laws and acknowledged your sovereignty as much as the English and the Italians have paid homage to their rulers and glorified their kings? Since this is so, let there be an end to Israel’s sufferings. “Forgive thy people according to the abundance of thy kindness!”[^17] Until you will, I will not leave my place. I will not stir from my position. Yitgadal veyitkadash sh’meh rabba.[^18] Magnified and sanctified be His great name.”

In the same vein Israel pleads with us, its American brethren. “Don’t believe my accusers. Be not led astray by their arguments. Whatever they may say against me on this night of nights, when man’s sins are confessed, my merits outweigh my faults. And I will not budge from here until you have answered my call. “I know for certain that my redeemer liveth.”[^19] And if I put my fate into the hands of God and am ready to risk all, why should you be afraid to venture just a little bit?
