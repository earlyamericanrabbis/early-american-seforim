THE JEWS AND MEDICINE

Parshiot Tazria’--Metzora’ of the book of Leviticus touch on subjects which belong, properly speaking, to the domain of medicine. Primarily, of course, it is with ritual cleanliness and uncleanliness that the Torah is concerned, not with personal hygiene or public health. However, the ascertainment of the ritual status of women immediately after childbirth and under other conditions, as well as that of individuals afflicted with leprosy and the other physical disorders listed in our text, presupposed a considerable knowledge of gynecology and pathology, which must have been in antiquity, as they are today, highly professionalized sciences requiring special training. The diagnosis of the different symptoms of the skin disease referred to by Scripture as the plague of Tzera‘at, which is ordinarily translated “leprosy,” was, as the book of Deuteronomy[^1] also testifies, one of the functions of the priests. It was the descendants of Aaron, who were the first official Jewish clergy, that were vested with the task of deciding, by examining persons exhibiting these symptoms, whether they really suffered from leprosy or not, and whether it was necessary to quarantine them in order to prevent their contaminating those with whom they might come in contact. These facts, in and by themselves, may not yet warrant the conclusion that medicine was widely practiced among the Israelites who went forth from Egypt, although, on the other hand, such a possibility is not completly ruled out, since the country from which they came had at the time admittedly attained a high level of civilization and the medical profession was well established in it. The Bible itself speaks of the midwives and wet-nurses, that assisted the Hebrew women in the land of the Pharaohs, as they gave birth to their children and during the period that they had to suckle them. Nor is it safe to infer from the isolation prescribed for the leper, by the laws of Moses, and the requirement, mentioned in Deuteronomy,[^2] to cover up excrement, that the modern rules of sanitation were already fully known to Israel’s lawgiver and that we have in the Mosaic legislation a forerunner of the sanitary code first adopted in our day and age. On the contrary, it would seem from the statement in Exodus[^3] that “If Israel would heed God’s commandments and do what was right in his eyes,” it would be immune from the maladies visited upon the Egyptians “because I, the Lord, am He that healeth thee,” that the prevention and cure of disease were regarded as the exclusive functions of God. He alone wounds and heals, even as life and death are in His hand, as it has been said: “I kill and I make alive, I have wounded and I heal.”[^4] At all events it did King Asa little good, in the matter of the foot ailment, to which he succumbed in the thirty-ninth year of his reign, to consult the physicians instead of seeking the help of God. He died, as the second book of Chronicles[^5] informs us, two years after he had been stricken, and all the skill of the professional healers did not avail to save him.

Perhaps the only safe assertion about Judaism’s attitude in these early days toward the physician and the science of medicine is that it had not yet been crystallized. But if prior to that time its position is doubtful, all uncertainties and doubts fall to the ground when we reach the age of the sage and proverbist Ben Sira, who lived and wrote just prior to the era of the Maccabees. In his book of maxims, from which excerpts are quoted in the Talmud, the art of healing figures as a highly respected profession and the medical practitioner as a person deserving of all the honors and emoluments that might be heaped upon him for his competence and knowledge. “Honor the physician before thou hast need of him,” so he exhorts the reader at the beginning of the 38th chapter of his work. “The knowledge of the physician shall lift his head, he shall stand before the nobles, and from the king he shall receive gifts.” To be sure, cautions Ben Sira, the physician cannot perform the miraculous cures, he may effect, all by himself. He needs the aid of Him as whose instrument he serves and who is the source of all knowledge. “From God a physician getteth wisdom.” But to seek the advice of a physician in the event of illness is not only permissible from the standpoint of Jewish law. It is mandatory, a duty that a human being owes himself. The authorization of physicians to practice their art is implied, according to the rabbinic commentators of the Bible, in the claim of an individual, who has been injured through the fault of another, to medical expenses. “Only he shall pay for the loss of his time, and shall cause him to be thoroughly healed.”[^6] However the sages of the Talmud went far beyond mere toleration of the medical profession. In fact, they declared it forbidden for a scholar to take up residence in a community that did not have at least one physician.[^7] And if it be asked: “Since God is the ultimate author of sickness and health, what right has the physician to interfere with His sovereign decrees?” the answer given would be that which was attributed by the Midrash[^8] to Rabbis Akiba and Ishmael. These two teachers of the Mishnah, who flourished at the beginning of the second century of the common era, met, as they walked through the streets of Jerusalem, a man who was sick. “Masters,” he said to them, “tell me how I can be cured.” ‘Do thus and thus,” was their reply, “and you will be healed.” A bystander, who overheard their remark, thereupon asked the rabbis: “Who inflicted this illness upon the man?” “Who but the Holy One Blessed Be He?” answered the interrogated. “In that case,” said the questioner, “you acted contrary to God’s will. He punished and you wished to heal.”

“What is your occupation?” asked the rabbis.

“T am a farmer. Don’t you see the vine-cutter in my hand?”

“But who created the vineyard?”

“The Holy One, Blessed Be His Name.”

“Tf that is so, then you interfered with the work of God. He created the vineyard and you cut away its fruits.”

“But if I would not weed the vineyard,” interposed the farmer, “and gather its fruit, it would not produce anything.”

“Then, fool that you are,” said the sages, “why don’t you apply the knowledge you have acquired by your experience as a tiller of the soil to the life of man? ‘Man’s days,” says the psalmist,[^9] ‘are as grass.’ Just as the vine will not grow and bring forth without care and cultivation, but is certain to die, so is it with the human body To regain its health it requires herbs and medicines, and its tiller is the physician.”

In other words although God is recognized as the supreme healer, as the court of last resort for decreeing a person’s recovery from illness or his death in consequence thereof, the physician is the instrument whereby his recovery, if he is destined to recover, is to be effected. To neglect the employment of such an instrument would, therefore, be tantamount to showing lack of appreciation, aye contempt, of what a merciful Providence has prepared for man’s benefit. “God bringeth out medicines from the earth, and let a prudent man not refuse them.”[^10]

It was this attitude toward the medical science, which has been maintained to this very day, that was responsible for the inclination that Jews have shown throughout the ages for the study and practice of medicine and the high esteem in which they held the physician, so much so that many of the outstanding Jewish religious leaders and legal authorities combined the office of rabbi with that of the medical practitioner. Maimonides was hailed by one of his Moslem contemporaries as a healer not only of the body but also of the soul. The same could have been said of countless other stars on the horizon of Jewish thought, beginning with Mar Samuel, one of the founders of the Talmudical academies in Babylonia at the beginning of the third century of the common era, to Isaac Lampronti (1679-1756), the author of the Talmudical encyclopedia, Pachad Yitzchak, and Graziado, who was a member of the Sanhedrin convoked by Napoleon Bonaparte in Paris 150 years ago.

The close union of the medical profession with the calling that symbolized and demanded the highest idealism contributed greatly toward raising the standards of the Jewish physician. Students of medical history often cite the oath prescribed by Hippocrates, a contemporary of Socrates, to graduates of medical schools about to begin the practice of their art, as proof of the exalted moral calibre of the men dedicated to the healing of the human body. That in order to fulfill their mission physicians must be idealists of a sort is, of course, plain common sense. Yet the level of ethics that Hippocrates expected his disciples to uphold does not begin to compare with the standards set up by his Jewish counterparts. For while the Greek “father of medicine” asked of those whom he had taught his art only that they exercise it “for the benefit and not the hurt of their patients, that they give no deadly drug to anyone, even though it be requested, that they would not aid in abortions nor commit an act of seduction, and that they keep confidential any knowledge they might have of a patients’s condition,” the Jewish physicians went much further. Thus for example one of them — it is believed that this was none other than Moses Maimonides ——- would pray every day: “Almighty God, Thou who hast created the human body with infinite wisdom... inspire me with love for my art and for Thy creatures. Do not allow thirst for profit, ambition for renown and admiration, to interfere with my profession... In the sufferer let me see only the human being.”[^11] Another Jewish member of the medical fraternity, Isaac Israeli, who practiced in North Africa at the end of the ninth century, cautioned physicians[^12] to be neither overhasty nor dilatory. In acting too precipitately they might make mistakes, while by waiting too long they might be contributing to the patient’s death. “Acute cases call for speedy thought and action.” Furthermore, says this Jewish medical authority of the early Middle Ages, the physician must not think that he brings about a cure. He merely prepares and smoothes the way of nature, which is the actual healer; and nature acts as it does because God fixed its laws.

Finally there is the injunction of a still earlier Jewish physician Asaph, who is said to have lived in Mesopotamia in the seventh century of the common era, which warned his pupils against permitting the spirit of pride, that is personal vanity, so to overcome them as to harm their patients instead of helping them. Selfish considerations must be completely suppressed by them in favor of the all-over-arching goal to which they are dedicated, namely that of healing.[^13]

The old Jewish tradition of esteem for the medical art and its practitioners stands in bold contrast to the antagonism often displayed by the medieval church and in our day by the adherents of the Christian Science movement. The cause of such antagonism is either superstition and ignorance on the part of the socalled standardbearers of religion on the one hand, or the contempt shown for religious values by the protagonists of science on the other. In Judaism the clash, when occasionally it did arise, was never too keen, and the reason was that religion and such sciences as that of medicine looked upon each other not as rivals but as collaborators, partners, complementing each other — the one devoted to healing the body, the other to rehabilitating the soul. Only in our day are these complementary functions of medical science, each indispensable for the recovery of the sick, being recognized. That is why most hospitals are now provided not only with competent physicians to provide the physical therapy of the patients and surgeons to perform the unavoidable surgical operations, but also with chaplains, who have the role of cheering the sick, of setting their minds at ease, thus filling them with the desire to fight and thereby assure and speed their recovery “By encouraging the patient to look forward to being cured,” said Isaac Israeli more than a thousand years ago[^14] “even if the physician is himself not convinced of it, he will greatly strengthen him,” and thus help bring about the unexpected and seemingly impossible.
