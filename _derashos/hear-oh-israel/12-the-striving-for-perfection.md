THE STRIVING FOR PERFECTION

The first half of the book of Exodus is concerned with the story of the enslavement of the children of Israel in Egypt and of their subsequent deliverance from this their house of bondage. According to Scripture the miraculous and divine played a most important part in the liberation of the sorely oppressed Israelite slaves from the strangle-hold of their Egyptian taskmasters. Not once but over and over again is the thought repeated in the biblical text that God was going to redeem His people “with an outstretched arm and with great judgments.”[^1] Nevertheless there is nothing fantastic, contrary to reason or insulting the intelligence of an adult mind in the Scriptural account of the actual means whereby the children of Israel, who had been doing forced labor for the Pharaohs, won their freedom from the chains of the tyrants. The mechanics of the efforts that culminated in their attainment of their independence followed strictly the universal rules of psychology, those spiritual laws that operate wherever the human soul rebels against tyranny and oppression and that eventually lend victory to the arms of the oppressed over their oppressors. It was entirely natural that the Hebrew midwives as well as the mothers of the children themselves should have defied the inhuman decree of the king ordering all Hebrew males to be thrown at birth into the Nile, and that one of these Hebrew infants, eluding the vigilance of the Egyptian equivalent of the Nazi Gestapo, should have managed to grow to man’s estate. Didn’t a million and a half of the seven and one half million Jews, caught in Hitler’s trap, survive the fiend’s water-tight plan for their extermination? Nor was there anything um-probable about the fact that this Hebrew child, that was destined later on to engineer and carry out the Israelite break for freedom, should have been raised in the tyrant’s own palace as the adopted son of his daughter. This sort of trick is often played by fate upon autocrats who wield the axe of tyranny against their fellowmen. In their own household does the master of the universe cause to spring up the nemesis that ultimately puts an end to their despotic rule. Anyhow, where could the future savior of Israel have been more secure and sheltered than in the home of the oppressor himself?

Finally it was imperative that Moses be reared in such surroundings, as Scripture informs us that he was, in order that he might be fitted for the role that had been assigned to him. “No prisoner has ever escaped from his dungeon without outside help.”[^2] If Moses had grown up as a slave, worn out by drudgery, he would never have had the mind to think about or appreciate the value of freedom, nor would he have known how to proceed in order to achieve it. Most of the leaders of successful uprisings against despotism of one type or another were outsiders who espoused the cause of underdogs. Bolivar, who helped the now independent republics of South America throw off the hated yoke of Spain, was a Spanish aristocrat. George Washington, the commander-in-chief of the army of the Revolution and the first president of the United States of America, the foundation of which was made possible by the success of that revolution, was a wealthy Virginia planter. Mirabeau, the genius and guiding spirit of the French Revolution that put an end to the Bourbon dynasty, was a member of the French nobility, a count, the son of a marquis. And while Lenin and Trotzky and Stalin, the stars of the Bolshevik heaven, did not emanate from families of wealth and power, they were certainly not members of the Russian proletariat, that is ignorant peasants or laborers toiling with their hands, but intellectuals who worked chiefly with their brains. In short, in order to be able to teach his Hebrew kinsmen, languishing in Egyptian servitude, the meaning and love of liberty and condition them to take advantage of the offer of release from bondage, when it would be made, Moses had to be himself a freeman. As a matter of fact every trait of Moses’ character, as it is portrayed in Scripture, the interest he displayed, in spite of his having grown up in a milieu of wealth and luxury, once he had reached man’s estate, in the lot of his hapless brethren, the tenderness he evinced even to mute beasts, as indicated by his behavior toward the lamb of his father-in-law’s flock that had wandered off into the wilderness — all these things contributed, as the Midrash observes,[^3] to his preparation for the herculean task, that was to be his, the task of leading the children of Israel out of Egypt and welding the cantankerous, quarrelsome, ignorant, unruly mass of slaves into a well-disciplined, united people, dedicated to the worship of the one God, who had created the world, and to obedience of His Law.

In one respect, however, it seems that the man whom God had selected to be Israel’s spokesman did not at all qualify for his role. He was lacking that attribute, which in him, whose business it is to plead the cause of those he represents, would ordinarily be regarded as essential — namely eloquence, the gift of speech. And what was even worse, he was painfully aware of his deficiency, so much so that when the Almighty called upon him to accept the mission that was to change the entire course of Israel’s history, his immediate reaction was one of emphatic refusal. ‘Send whomever thou wouldst send”[^4] — anyone except me. “I am not a man of words... for I am halting of speech and slow of tongue.”[^5]

The excuses Moses presented did not, of course, carry any weight with God. Since, as the Midrash remarks,[^6] it was the intention of the divine Judge to perform miracles through Moses in order to bring Pharaoh and his people to their senses in regard to their treatment of the Israelites, Moses’ inadequacy as an orator posed no obstacle. “God can help with little as well as with much,” said Jonathan the son of Saul to his braves.[^7] In fact the more defective the instruments employed in executing it, the more impressive the feat. “Man,” say Israel’s sages, “may feel ashamed to be seen using imperfect implements. God’s instruments, however, are all of them broken vessels.”[^8] How could it be otherwise? Since the virtuous and saintly are the agents of His will in the world and in comparison with Him, who is perfection itself —- as it was said: “the Rock His work is perfect”[^9] — even the most righteous does not quite measure up — “Behold the heavens are not pure enough in His sight”[^10] — God must perforce have only imperfect vessels at His disposal.

What a marvelous lesson not only for a Moses, but even more so for the majority of mankind, for the average human being, who cannot claim to have reached anything like the spiritual heights attained by the father of prophets! How comforting it is for the humblest individual to know that, notwithstanding his imperfections and defects, he is not precluded from bettering himself, from advancing and making progress. This is not just a matter of belief. The history of civilization reveals that it was with very crude, primitive utensils that mankind started out on its road of scientific and technological progress. It was by gradually refining and slowly improving his mechanical aids that man attained what he did in our day. Similarly it was with his imperfect, faulty, undeveloped brain that he had to endeavor to comprehend the nature of God and understand His purposes in creating the world. The people that was to become later on “a kingdom of priests and holy nation” began its career as a horde of slaves, so stultified by back-breaking toil, that when the glad tidings of redemption were brought to them, “they listened not to Moses because of shortness of breath and hard labor.”[^11] God always goes out to meet those who earnestly seek Him, as Judah Halevi expressed it:

> “Longing I sought Thy presence,
> Lord, with my whole heart did I pray,
> And going out toward Thee,
> I found Thee coming to me on the way.”

Let, then, no man be deterred by his recognition of his indubitable and inescapable shortcomings, from aspiring for the ideal. To reach it completely may be impossible. “But they who aim at it and persevere will come much nearer to it than those whose laziness and despondency make them give it up as unattainable.”
