WHEN GOD SPOKE WITH MAN

The festival of Shabuot commemorates an event in human experience the uniqueness of which can hardly be denied. The appearance to mortals of beings described as divine is a phenomenon oft remarked in the literatures of antiquity. Nothing is more common in the epics of the ancient Babylonians, in the poems of Homer, the Greek, and Vergil, the Roman, or in the dramas of Sophocles and Euripides than conversations between gods and men. But that not a god but the God of the universe, the almighty Creator and supreme Ruler of all that is, that He should have communicated directly, not with just one of His creatures, selected from among the rest because of his superior intellectual and spiritual qualifications, which fitted him for this distinction, but with an entire people, consisting of grown men and women as well as young children, in all the diversity of their mental and moral equipment — that is unparalleled in the annals of humankind. “Has ever such a thing come to pass or has anything like it been heard,” so Moses is reported to have asked with reference to the revelation at Sinai, “that a people should hear the voice of God speak out of the midst of the fire, as thou hast done, and remain alive?”[^1] Never before in history was such an occurrence noted nor has any repetition thereof been recorded since then. This is what tradition has to say concerning what transpired in the wilderness between Egypt and what was to become the Land of Israel on the sixth day of Sivan some 3300 years ago.

The sophisticated mind of modern man is, to be sure, not ready to accept these claims of Hebrew Scripture. Science, declare the wise men of our day, has taught us that this world, in which we live, operates according to certain fixed laws of universal application, that are not subject to change or alteration. What holds true for one place at a given time must be presumed to apply everywhere and for all time. “The earth,” to quote a Talmudic maxim, “is one block.”[^2] Everywhere on the terrestrial planet fire burns and water seeks its lowest level, and that is how these elements can always be depended on to react. If, you want us to believe that God actually spoke to the Israelites in the desert of Sinai in the third month following their departure from Egypt, then, pray, show us an analogue in our own present-day experience. Else how can we be certain that the allegations of the Bible are fact and not fiction? Surely, if our primitive ancestors, to whom the knowledge accumulated by man since the age in which they lived was inaccessible, were capable of recognizing the voice of deity, why should such intercourse with the divine be impossible for us, the generation that discovered the secret of the atom?

There is but one answer that those, who have implicat faith in the authenticity of the affirmations of our Sacred Writ, can offer to this challenge, and that is that proof from analogy is not available in this instance, because nothing comparable to the Ten Commandments has ever been divulged to man. The Torah was given only once, and has never been replaced or superseded by a better and more perfect model. Christianity does indeed claim that the new covenant made with a Jew, whom it acknowledged as the Messiah and son of God, is an improvement on the old testament given to Moses.[^3] Similarly Mohammed of Mecca insisted that his Koran was the correct and original version of the Word of God, which the Jews had distorted.[^4] However by their very repudiation of those teachings of Judaism, which were to them unacceptable, the two daughter faiths, through whom the Hebrew Scriptures became the property of almost one half of the human race, paid tribute, albeit indirectly, to the originality of the religious genius of the Jewish people. For that it was the children of Israel and they alone that as a nation had an intimation of the presence of the living God — that is an historic fact that neither Christianity nor Islam would question or deny.

Exactly what this experience consisted of, what methods or mechanics were involved in what is referred to as “revelation” may never be known. “There is no way,” observes Dr. Abraham Heschel in his book “God in Search of Man,”[^5] “of explaining how thoughts of the infinite God move along the narrow path of a human mind.” But why worry about the means when it is the results that really concern us’ In a work of genius it is not the intermediate stages, that have to be traversed in its creation, but rather the finished product that is of the greatest moment. So, too, the revelation at Sinai is to be judged by the value of its contents rather than the circumstances amidst which it took place. Viewed from this vantage point, the outcome of Israel’s encounter with its God at the mountain in the desert strikes one as truly stupendous. Mankind has unquestionably made tremendous strides forward since Sinai in the exploration, not only of the nature of the physical universe, but of the character of reality as a whole. Nevertheless the ethical principles and the religious axioms enunciated in the solitude of that wilderness have never been excelled. If one were to scour all that has been written by the thinkers of humanity during the past three-thousand years on human behavior, one would find no pronouncement that sums up better the whole duty of man than the injunction of “Thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself.”[^6] Nor are the historians of human civilization able to point to a group of reformers that were more consistent in their denunciation of injustice in every shape and form, more tireless in espousing the cause of the underprivileged and oppressed, and more forgetful of self in their eagerness to help others than Israel’s prophets.

Sometimes one wonders whether the rabbinic apologetes of Jewry were not indulging in national self-adulation when they harped on the moral superiority of Israel’s seers over their pagan counterparts.[^7] Yet when one contrasts the lofty, spiritual idealism of a Moses with the degeneracy and the lowly, physical lusts of a Balaam,[^8] one is forced to agree that the former must have heard God’s call. With regard to him it is said: “And He called unto Moses”,[^9] wayikra ’el Mosheh, whereas in the case of the later the expression employed in the Sacred Writ is wayikor,[^10] “And the Lord met him by chance,” the implication being that it was only accidentally, without advance notice, as would be appropriate in matters of importance, that the divine will was made known to him.

A classic illustration of the reasoning of credulity is furnished by the argument whereby a naive Chassid supported his claim that his rabbi conversed with angels. When a doubting Mitnagged (opponent) challenged his allegation by asking’ “How do you know he does?”, the rabbi’s devotee replied, as though it were something self-evident: “He told me so himself.” “But what proof have you that he spoke the truth?” the doubter continued his attack. This thrust the believer countered by the question: “Do you think a person who converses with angels would tell a he?” In the case of the prophets of Israel the same method of deduction might be pursued but in reverse. When an ordinary commoner evinces the courage displayed by an Isaiah to stand up before the rulers of his country and fling their crimes into their faces, showing them up as “companions of thieves and lovers of bribery,”[^11] when he can come into the very temple courts and, denouncing the hypocrisy of the worshippers, tell them that God “would not hear their prayers because their hands were filled with blood,”[^12] we may accept without hesitancy his assertion “My eyes have seen the King, the Lord Hosts.”[^13] Nor need we doubt that the statement “the heaven is My throne and the earth My footstool... yet is it such a one that I regard — the poor and contrite of spirit, who trembleth at My word”[^14] was correctly introduced by the remark “Thus saith the Lord.”.

We have no means of accounting for the sublime grandeur of the message of our Scripture, and it is as futile to try to substantiate the veracity of the prophets’ pretensions that God had spoken to them as to demonstrate the beauty of music to a person who is both deaf and insensitive to sound. Yet of the supreme worth of their bequest there cannot be the slightest doubt. What if some copyists’? mistakes have crept into the written text as it has been transmitted? Does the detection of a few spots in the sun detract from the brilliance of its light or diminish by one iota its value as the source of energy and warmth, that causes life to spring up and furthers development and growth?

There may be those who think that Jeremiah was suffering from hallucinations when he reported that “the word of God was burning in him like a consuming fire, which he was unable to hold back,”[^15] or that the Israelites must have been under the spell of an optical illusion when they listened without protest to the statement “You have seen that I spoke with you from heaven.”[^16] That does not invalidate the prophecy of the former nor the Ten Commandments purported to have been heard by the latter. It has been related that when the celebrated psychiatrist Max Nordau, who became the collaborator of Theodore Herzl, the founder of political Zionism and his second in command, was asked whether it was necessary to be crazy in order to be a Zionist, he answered: “It is not absolutely essential, but it helps.” Similarly prophesying may have been accompanied at times by certain psychic phenomena that served as its outward manifestations.[^17] But its real credentials were the eternal truths that make up its contents, not the gestures which it shared in common with all forms of ecstasy, inspired or otherwise.

It is widely agreed that there is something overpowering about the impact that the Hebrew Scriptures have had on the minds of men for the past 2,000 years. “The omnipotence of God,” to quote Dr. Heschel gain,[^18] “may not always be perceptible, but the omnipotence of the Bible is the great miracle of history.” It “is the perpetual motion of the spirit, an ocean of meaning, the waves of which beat against man’s abrupt and steep shortcomings and the echo of which reaches into the alleys of his wrestling with despair”. “Thou shalt not kill!’ “Thou shalt not commit adultery!”? “Thou shalt not steal!” “Thou shalt not covet!”[^19] “Oppress not the stranger!”[^20] “Lie not to one another nor deceive each other!”[^21] “Put no stumbling block before the blind!”[^22] “Justice, justice shalt thou pursue!”[^23] — these words ning out perpetually from the eternal Book of Books, bringing back the sheep who have wandered from the fold. Their ultimate authorship is attributed by Jewish tradition to none other than God. Israel’s Torah is the word of the living God. “Grass withers and flowers fade,” declared Isaiah,[^24] “but the word of God standeth forever.” “If ye believe it not, verily ye shall not endure.”[^25]
