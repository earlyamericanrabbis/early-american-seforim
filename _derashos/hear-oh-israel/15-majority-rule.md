MAJORITY RULE

The purpose of the observance of the group of ordinances designated by Scripture as mishpatim is, as the ground meaning of the word indicates, the realization of the ideal of social justice. In many instances the law is quite clear and explicit. There is, for example, nothing vague or equivocal about the rules laid down in the Torah for the treatment by his master of the Hebrew slave, nor about the night to compensation for damage done to a person’s property, nor about the penalties for various types of bodily injury, theft or murder. What is often in doubt is the guilt or the extent of accountability of the individual brought to court on one charge or another. If the men, sitting in the seats of justice and called upon to pronounce judgment upon the actions otf those whose cases are submitted to them, were possessed of divine omniscience, there would, of course, be no difficulty in determining the guilt or innocence of the accused. God, who knows everything, cannot be deceived, diverted from the truth or influenced in one direction or another. The human jurist, however, no matter how able, learned and incorruptible he may be, has only the evidence presented to him to base his decisions on.[^1] Nor is his reasoning always infallible. Where a court of law is constituted of more than one judge — and rabbinic Judaism looked with disfavor upon one-man-courts. “Only God has the right to judge single-handed.”[^2] — there is always the possibility of differences of opinion and perfectly honest differences at that. This being the case, practical considerations have prompted the adoption of the rule that, in all instances in which unanimity cannot be reached, the view of the majority is to prevail. “After the many must one incline.’[^3]

Such a rule sounds fair enough. It is democracy in action. To be sure the truth does not depend upon numbers. “One on God’s side,” it has been asserted and rightly so, “4s a majority.” But how is it to be determined who is on God’s side? What unerring method is there for establishing this fact? According to the theory of probability the likelihood of its being found among the many is greater than its being in the possession of the few. It is a simple matter of arithmetic. “Two (heads) are better than one.”[^4] That is why of all the courses that might be followed in achieving justice that of the majority is the most likely to produce the desired results, to come closest to fulfilling the aim of giving to each litigant his just due.

So far so good. But let us not be overhasty. The feeling that the collective opinion of the majority has a better chance of approximating the truth than the view of the minority is based on the assumption that the individuals making up the jury or court are persons of equivalent qualifications, that they possess the same ability to think logically and that they are all fully acquainted with the law. It could not apply where all the brains are vested in the few while the many are, compared to them, devoid of intelligence. Furthermore when a matter demanding specialized training or knowledge has to be decided on, as for instance whether a bridge is strong enough to hold up under a certain amount of traffic or an illness from which a certain individual has died is ordinarily fatal, the testimony of a million laymen is not worth as much as the evidence of a single expert.

The usefulness of the majority rule, therefore, has its limitations. It is valid only where that majority is well-informed and has the capacity for independent and intelligent thinking, where it does not allow itself to be carried away by its emotions and is not prejudiced by malicious and false propaganda. These conditions are, alas, not always fulfilled. It is, of course, well known that dictators are never at a loss in putting on a show to prove that the masses are behind them. All they have to do to secure endorsement of their measures is to threaten with the direst consequences failure to come out and vote in their favor. A tight censorship of the news assures full support of their policies, however mistaken they might be. But even in a democracy demagogues, who know something about mob psychology, can so influence the voters as to win an election. The Torah warns against such dangers. “Do not take up a false report,”[^5] it cautions. “Be not an accomplice of the wicked to become an unrighteous witness”. “Follow not the multitude — that is the majority — to do evil.”[^6] The fact that they are the majority and that the vote of of the majority is decisive does not exonerate you, if what is sanctioned by that majority is unjust and unfair. Remember that majority rule is only a device, a means, and often a very clumsy and imperfect means for arriving at the truth, certainly no substitute for the truth. “The voice of the majority,” observed Schiller, “is no proof of justice.”

The realization that human beings are not born either “free or equal,” because experience shows the contrary to be true — “As they differ in their features so do they vary in their opinions,” observes the Talmud[^7] — impelled Plato, one of the profoundest thinkers of all time, to draw up as the blue-print of an ideal state, in which justice would be properly carried out, one in which every citizen was assigned his particular task, according to his capacity. Only those on the top echelon, the philosophers, whom Plato regarded as the elite of human society, were in this state given the right to rule. Judaism was opposed to such an arrangement. While its sages agreed that only those competent could be entrusted with the administration of justice and legislation in general — “Whoever is unacquainted with the legal forms used in documents of divorce or marriage should not occupy himself therewith,” said the Amora Samuel[^8] — it made provision for every citizen to qualify.

The reason why the Torah was given to Israel in the wilderness rather than in one of the thickly inhabited centers of population was to indicate that it was free and available to anyone who cared to acquire it. Democracy, it has been pointed out, is built on education. Without an intelligent well-informed, alert citizenry it cannot function, but must fall into decay. The same holds true for Judaism Its future is directly dependent on knowledge. Jewish congregations in America have the right, like organized units of other denominations, to decide by majority vote what sort of policies they wish to pursue, which ritual is to be followed by them, whether they are to be labelled as Orthodox, Conservative or Reform or by any other title, and no one is allowed to interfere with them in availing themselves of the prerogative of freedom of worship. If their members are men of intelligence and learning, their decisions will assure the future of Judaism in this country. If they are not, they are likely by their ineptness to dig the grave of the Jewish religion in this land of the free, which imposes no limitations on any of its citizens in regard to the faith he is to follow. The greatest peril facing the American Jewish community is ignorance. If that could be eradicated, the prospects of its survival would be immeasurably improved. “When all thy children will be learned of the Lord, then will the wellbeing of thy children be (correspondingly) great.”[^9]
