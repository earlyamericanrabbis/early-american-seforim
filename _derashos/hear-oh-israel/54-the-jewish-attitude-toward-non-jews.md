THE JEWISH ATTITUDE TOWARD NON-JEWS

One of the frequently used words of the English language that betray the deep influence on the speech of the Anglo-saxon peoples of the ideology of the Old Testament is the term gentle. Patterned upon the Hebrew expression employed in referring to members of nations other than the Jewish this word implies the awareness on the part of Israel’s ancestors of the existence of a distinction between them and the rest of the world. That this consciousness of being different from their neighbors served as an incentive to Jews in the past for the maintenance of the highest moral standards is already indicated in the Bible. “Now therefore,” reads the message that was transmitted to the Israelites encamped about Mount Sina: in the name of God, “Gf ye will hearken unto My voice indeed, and keep my covenant, then ye shall be Mine own treasure from among all peoples, for all the earth is Mine; and ye shall be unto Me a kingdom of priests, and a holy nation.”[^1] The Jew always felt uplifted and sanctified by the fact that he had through his forbears been made the recipient of God’s commandments. He was proud — and would anyone blame him for entertaining such feelings, seeing how justified they were? — of his spiritual heritage. It caused him to strive for nobility of character, for religious and ethical perfection. Noblesse oblige. Self-identification with the moral elite, the mere desire to emulate the example of men and women who have contributed to the progress of mankind, to the improvement of the human lot, ennobles. No wonder the prophet Isaiah exhorts his Judean contemporaries, those that were following after righteousness and were eagerly seeking the Lord, to look unto the rock whence they were hewn, and the hole of the quarry whence they were digged, to model their conduct upon that of their father Abraham and of the matriarch Sarah who had given birth to them.[^2]

However just as true nobility is always coupled with regard for the rights of others, so the high rating that Jews placed on their own mission and destiny did not produce any diminution in their esteem for their non-Jewish fellowmen. The same Bible, which announces Israel’s election as the standard-bearer and custodian of God’s eternal Law, proclaims the truth that all human beings living on earth, whatever their color or creed might be, are derived from a common ancestor and that every member of the human species is created in God’s image. And lest there be any misunderstanding about how these blood-relations are to demean themselves toward one another, lest any follower of the Torah of Moses make the mistake of believing that the injunction of “And thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself”[^3] applies only to his coreligionists, he is reminded of the fact that his Bible is “the book of the generations of man”,[^4] that the ethical doctrines preached by it are to be practiced on and by all of God’s children, that they were never intended to be the monopoly nor to remain the exclusive possession of any particular group.

This broad humanitarian attitude toward the non-Jewish world, which is borne out by such statements of the psalmist as “God is good to all and His mercies extend to all His works”,[^5] seems to be negated by a number of ordinances of an apparently discriminatory nature laid down in the Pentateuch, the basis of all Jewish religious law. Jews are enjoined there against intermarriage with those who are not of their race[^6] They are told that, while they may not accept interest for loans extended to their fellow-Jews, they may exact it from non-Jews.[^7] They are also permitted to sell to their non-Jewish neighbors meats forbidden to be consumed by them, because they were not derived from freshly slaughtered cattle.[^8] Do not such measures prove that the biblical ruling concerning the equality of all men in the eyes of the law, the maxim of “Ye shall have one manner of law, as well for the stranger as for the homeborn” was not meant sincerely?[^9]

It may be said unqualifiedly that such conclusions are entirely unwarranted, because they disregard completely the motives behind the aforementioned regulations, and in law the motive, the purpose, the end to be achieved is all-important. If Jews were forbidden to intermarry with non-Jews, it was not for racial reasons, but in order that their attachment to the faith of their fathers might not be weakened.[^10] Even if st were possible for a man and a woman, bound together by ties of matrimony, to be sincere devotees of two distinct religions, the relationship would not be the happiest, for theirs would be a house divided against itself. That it was such considerations and not the difference of race that divided the Israelites from their non-Jewish neighbors is demonstrated by the cordial welcome given to the Moabitess, Ruth, the ancestress of King David, when she came to join the Jewish fold,[^11] by the warmth with which gentile converts to Judaism have always been received into the ranks of Judaism, and by the fact that the daily prayers of the synagogue contain a special blessing dedicated to the righteous proselytes,

The relaxation of the prohibition against the charging of interest in fiscal transactions with non-Jews again was due simply to the fact that trade is a two-sided, reciprocal operation; and since gentiles, who were not bound by the provisions of Jewish Law, could not be compelled to refrain from exacting interest for loans they might extend to members of the Jewish faith, the latter had to be given the same privilege in the reverse case. That this arrangement was not prompted by any intent of discrimination is evident from the fact that the life and property of a non-Jew is as inviolable and sacred as that of a Jew. Israel’s Bible does not say “he that sheddeth the blood of a Jew” but “He that sheddeth the blood of any man, by man shall his blood be shed”.[^12] Also commandments like “Thou shalt not steal”[^13] are stated without modifiers or qualifications in favor of any tribe or clan or family. Jewish law has always shown the greatest solitude for the stranger in the gate. He was to be given the same opportunity to make a living as the native-born Jew.[^14] If he was in need, he shared with the Levite and the Jewish orphan and widow the benefits of special provisions made for the maintenance of the poor.[^15] The rabbis of the Talmud preached good-will between Jew and gentile and ordered their followers to show their respects to their gentile neighbors, in the event of death, by attending their funerals.[^16] If Jewish law permitted the sale to non-Jews of victuals excluded from the Jew’s diet, it was not because of the poor quality of the products, but because of the recognition that the ritual restrictions of Judaism were not binding on non-Jews. And Judaism has always been tolerant of the beliefs of others, as the prophet Micah put it: “For let all the peoples walk each one in the name of its God, but we will walk in the name of the Lord our God for ever and ever.”[^17] It is interesting to note that while Judaism makes no attempt to foist the distinctive religious doctrines of Jewry and the rather heavy burden of Jewish law on the non-Jewish world, it does not on that account exclude those, who are not adherents of the Jewish faith, from salvation. Any gentile, who abides by the general rules of public morality, has an honored place in the world to come[^18] just like Israel’s saints.

Merit, so the Talmud teaches, is to be given recognition wherever it is found. Every people has certain excellencies, so one of its sages expressed himself, which are worthy of emulation[^19] If there was anything that the Jew has been taught to avoid, it is the nursing of a grudge or the bearing of rancor on account of past and long forgotten wrongs. Despise not the Edomite, says the Bible, although some of his forefathers had behaved toward their Israelite kinsmen as Esau had toward Jacob.[^20] Contemn not the Egyptian, though thou wast once a stranger in his land.[^21] The sins of the fathers, in other words, so Judaism holds, must not be visited on their innocent children.[^22] The Jew has always been ready and willing to forget and forgive. He does not blame the Englishmen of today for the cruel edicts of expulsion of all Jews from the British Isles in the thirteenth century, nor is the existing government of Russia held responsible for the sins committed against Jewry by the czars who ruled over its vast domains until four decades ago.

It is a misrepresentation of the truth, therefore, to read a derogatory meaning into the word gentile as it figures in the ideology of the Jew. This ideology may recognize differences of function between Jew and non-Jew. Just like individual human beings so nations, too, have, as a result of certain historical developments, their distinctive roles to fulfill. The ancient Greeks gave the world philosophy, and science and art. The Romans enriched civilization by means of their legal institutions and their ability as organizers. Israel’s contribution lay in the realm of religion. It was chosen by the Creator to bring to mankind the belief in the one and only God of the universe and to teach it its moral law. That is the significance of the idea of the priest-people,[^23] the title bestowed upon Jewry in return for living up to its lofty mission of being a light unto the nations[^24] an example of justice and righteousness to the world. God grant that the opportunity be afforded the Jew again speedily and in our time to carry out this historic destiny not only as an individual but also a respected member of the family of the nations of the world.
