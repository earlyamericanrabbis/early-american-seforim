“ALL OF THEM RIGHTEOUS”

In addition to dwelling in the Sukkah for an entire week the special ritual obligations of the Jew on the feast of Tabernacles include the use, in connection with the holiday prayers, of the “four species” of plants listed in the Torah,[^1] namely the palm-branch, the citron, the myrtle-stalks and willow-twigs. It has been thought that these particular forms of vegetation were selected because they were typical representatives of the fruit harvest of the land of Israel which coincides with the Sukkot festival.[^2] However, outside of the Lulab, not a single one of the four varieties comprised in the festival bouquet belongs to the seven kinds of growths of the soil for which the Holy Land was distinguished.[^3] The fruit harvest alone could, therefore, not be cue to the explanation of the rite of N’tilat Lulab, “the taking” or “shaking” of the palmbranch and its ancillaries. It had to have another meaning and the endeavor to discover what that meaning might be challenged the ingenuity of Jewish religious thinkers since the earliest days of Jewish teaching and preaching.

All sorts of interpretations are cited in the compilation of homilies called the P’sikta on the pentateuchal lesson for the first day of Sukkot.[^4] One sees in the distinctive qualities of the four varieties of plants the individual attributes of God. Another recognizes in them an allusion to the patriarchs or fathers of the Jewish race. A third makes them refer to the matriarchs or mothers of Israel. In the opinion of a fourth they symbolize the Sanhedrin, Israel’s Supreme Court and Legislative Council, while according to a fifth they point to the Jewish people as a whole, in all the complexity and heterogeneity of its composition. The common denominator of these different attempts to explain the significance of the "Arba‘ah Minum is that they all emphasize the great diversity of endowments that exists among human beings. In God, of course, all excellencies are combined in a single personality. He is at one and the same time majestic and just and exalted and meek. Among men, however, these qualities are apportioned separately, divided, some persons being distinguished. for certain traits while others shine in other respects. No one individual, however, can possess all virtues simultaneously. Only of God can it be said that “He acts like an elder on the day of judgment and reveals Himself as a youthful warrior at the time of battle.”[^5] For mortal man this is impossible. The man of action is rarely capable of patient study, and the scholar, who lives in his ivory tower, could hardly be expected suddenly to come forth from his retirement in order to translate into deeds the results of his thinking. It is only by the pooling of their individual resources that men can make progress, only by supplying each other’s wants, by trading services and goods that they attain a high measure of civilization and culture. The deal state, observes Plato in his “Republic,” is one that is so organized that every citizen knows his place and performs the function for which he was destined by the Creator by virtue of his endowment. When every instrument in an orchestra strikes the right note, a note that blends with the rest, the result is harmony. The same holds true for the complex political organism called the state. In order that peace may prevail there must be coordination, and coordination is possible only when every individual recognizes his particular mission and fulfills it to the best of his ability.

The truth to which the “four species” call attention is abundantly illustrated by experience. The evolution, for instance, of a people like Israel was due to a combination of factors, to wit the dignity of Abraham, the self-sacrifice of Isaac, the fecundity of Jacob and the brilliance, albeit shortlived, of Joseph. If Israel has maintained itself until this day, it is to be attributed to the authority that was conferred upon its elders and the competition among its scholars to excel each other in learning, as well as to the replenishment of the ranks of the latter by a constant supply of new recruits willing to assume responsibility and the faithful recording and preservation of tradition. The totality of Israel embraces men of deeds as well as persons of intellect, of those possessing both these virtues and of many not distinguished in either. Individually the best has but little effectiveness. The most brilliant general is impotent without an army to carry out his commands. Collectively, however, they constitute a mighty force, in which even the least talented contributes to the success of the entirety.

How’ Why? Of what use can individuals be that resemble the willow leaves that have neither fragrance nor savor? One might answer as one of our moderns did:

> “I have no voice for singing.
> I cannot make a speech.
> I have no gift for music.
> I know I cannot teach.
> “I am no good at leading.
> I cannot ‘organize’,
> And anything I write
> Would never win a prize.
> “It seems my only talent
> Is neither big nor rare,
> Just to listen and encourage
> And fill a vacant chair.
> “But all the gifted people
> Could not so brightly shine,
> Were it not for those who use
> A talent such as mine.”

We sometimes wonder exactly what the prophet had in mind when he declared: “Thy people are all of them righteous.”[^6] How could all Israelites be described as perfect saints, even if they were appraised with the liberality of their rabbinic apologetes, who stated that “the least worthy among them had to their credit meritorious deeds just as a pomegranate is full of kernels”?[^7] The solution of the difficulty hes in knowing where to put the accent, which point to emphasize. Taken individually none could measure up to the ideal, but when viewed as a whole, when their accomplishments as a group are considered, then “thy people may indeed be accounted righteous and worthy of inheriting the earth.”[^8] This, too, is what the author of the Midrash meant when he stated that no son of Israel, even he who was, like the willow leaves, bare of all redeeming virtues, was expendable.[^9] The reason is that when taken together the individual members of the community make up for each other’s deficiencies and become thereby deserving and worth preserving.
