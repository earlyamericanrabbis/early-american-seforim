THE POWER OF FAITH

Fundamentalists have acclaimed the cleaving of the Red Sea as the foremost intervention of the divine in the normal workings of nature, as the miracle of miracles. Modernists, on the other hand, if they do at all accept the historicity of the event, have set it down as one of nature’s freaks that occur every now and then for no one knows why. For some unaccountable reason, they say, call it Providence or what you will, the wind, conniving with the tide, drove the water aside at a shallow part of the Red Sea, laying bare its foundations, so that the Israelites were able to cross dryshod to the other side, after which the waves receded drowning the Egyptian pursuers in the watery grave.[^1] From the standpoint of Judaism’s official interpreters of Sacred Writ, the rabbinic commentators whose views are collected in the Midrash, it would appear as though both sides had missed the point. What was important about the cleaving of the Red Sea was not so much, according to these expositors of the Bible, the miracle wrought by God as the faith displayed by men. This is evident from the manner in which the ancient Jewish homilists pictured the cleaving of the Red Sea to have taken place.[^2] It didn’t come about in their opinion at the mere stretching forth of Moses’ staff. Israel’s lawgiver first had a long argument with the Almighty, because he believed such a feat to be a contradiction of the immutable laws, that had been fixed by the Creator when He had brought the world into being, and he, Moses, did not wish to be instrumental in breaking these laws. When God had satisfied him on this score, the Jewish leader, in obedience to God’s command, turned to the sea with the request that it do God’s bidding and be parted asunder. But the sea paid no attention to his pleas. It stubbornly refused to divide. While the contention was still going on and the end of the deadlock was not yet in sight, and while the various factions among the Israelites standing at the shore of the sea were as yet uncertain as to what to do, whether to return to their masters or frighten the Egyptians or fight them or march forward into the main, two of the tribes of Israel, those of Judah and Benjamin, plucked up courage and hurled themselves into the sea, whereupon the sea divided. It was then, observes the Midrash, that God said to Moses. “Why criest thou unto Me?”[^3] This is no time to indulge in prayer. “Speak unto the children of Israel that they march.”[^4]

This interesting homily gives one an insight into the rabbinic conception of the relationship between miracles and faith. The rabbis did, of course, believe in miracles. Nought was impossible for God. Whether these miracles could be explained scientifically or not was, so far as they were concerned, beside the point, It didn’t matter. What was important was not the means of their accomplishment but their author, the force that stood behind them. This much, however, is clear from the rabbinic account of the cleaving of the Red Sea, namely that it isn’t because miracles happen that men have faith, as seems to be indicated by the literal sense of the statement of the Bible: “And when the Israelites saw the great work which the Lord did upon the Egyptians, then the people feared the Lord and they believed in the Lord and in Moses His servant.”[^5] Rather because men have faith, miracles become possible. For those who always wait for some unusually favorable opportunity to turn up before they will budge, miracles, 1.e. the unbelievable and, therefore, seemingly impossible, never transpire, for they are never sure that the right moment has come and that by taking advantage of it they would meet with success. Those, however, who possess faith and courage and daring, who are ever willing to try and unafraid to take risks, often do, by dint of these qualities, make what seems impossible become possible. Yes, they need but put their feet into the sea, and the sea opens up for them and permits the brave venturers to walk through it to dry land.

Have not such miracles been wrought in our own day? Have we not seen mighty empires crumble and disintegrate for lack of confidence, whilst small nations with but little more than faith to buoy them up displayed marvels of endurance? Has it not happened before our very eyes that a barren, neglected, poverty-stricken, resourceless little land was turned into a thriving emporium, a garden-spot of the world, and that persons condemned to hopelessness and despair were transformed into upstanding, useful, productive members of society? All this came to pass as result of devotion to and belief in an ideal, whereas at the same time socalled practical men of the world, who preferred what they thought was security to what they termed romantic experiments, lost all that they possessed. The collapse of France with its magnificent army and its Maginot line and the stubborn defiance, on the other hand, of the British under the most savage bombardment; the miserable failure of Italy, after years of preparation and training, over against the gallant resistance, before the coming of German troops, of the ill-equipped and numerically vastly inferior Greeks; the liquidation in Europe of Galut Judaism with its merchant princes and its moneyed aristocracy and the simultaneous rise of the reborn Jewish community of Palestine whose chief strength lies in its Chalutzim, many of whom have no property of their own — all these phenomena bear eloquent testimony to the power of faith and the dismal consequences of its absence. It isn’t, then, the occurrence of miracles that causes men to have faith, but the possession of faith that makes miracles come about. Their confidence in their ideal, the certainty they have that they can succeed if only they apply themselves in earnest to the realization of their goal — these are the factors of success, the things that render possible the impossible. “God,” it has been said, “does not give us ready money. He issues promissory notes, and then pays them when faith presents them at the throne. Each one of us has a check book.”

What, now is this thing called faith? Is it nothing more than superstition, than credulity of the category implied in the maxim of the proverbist that “a fool believeth everything?”[^6] Is it merely a covering for ignorance’ Not at all! Faith is the heroism of the intellect. It is that faculty in man which picks up the thread in his quest for certainty where it is dropped by reason. Even science, that foremost expression of man’s intellect dedicated to the discovery of the truth, could make no progress in achieving its aim without the support of faith. It would be lacking the incentive, the stimulus that would goad it on to ever greater adventures into the world of the unknown. Reason needs faith to mde upon in order to reach its goal, just as faith needs reason to purify and refine it and rid it of the dross of superstition and credulity, that may encrust it, by directing it toward those regions to which the senses have no access or for which there is no time to wait until they have been explored by reason’s scouts. I mean such matters as the belief that there operates in the universe a directing genius that guides its destinies and is Himself of a moral nature, the conviction that virtue is rewarded and vice punished, that wickedness conduces to its own undoing and that justice must triumph in the end. All these postulates of religion reason must relinquish to faith.

It is, therefore, wrong to look upon faith as a sort of superstition. On the contrary, like his bread, it is man’s staff of life, without which he would be unable to carry on from one moment to the next. “If ye believe not,” said the prophet, “then indeed ye shall not be established.”[^7] There are in the world today millions of human beings who, were they to consider their momentary status only, would have no reason for prolonging their existence on earth. Yet, somehow or other, these persons, who have apparently lost everything, their homes, their jobs, their means of earning a livelihood, go on living. Why? How? “By their faith” is the answer.

One sometime wonders how the rank and file of the Jews of the East European pale, most of whom lived on less than from hand to mouth, Luftmenschen, whose economy was built on quicksands and whose sustenance was derived from the air, one wonders how they managed to pull through. The secret of this miracle is to be found in their firm trust in the goodness and mercy of God. Take, for example, a character like J. L. Peretz’s Yochonon Melamed, a poor teacher, who never earned enough to provide even for his own family. His brother dies. His orphaned niece, deserted by her husband, is about to give birth to a child, throwing added burdens on his weak shoulders. Does he despair? No, not he. “He who gives life,” he exclaims when he hears the news that the baby was born, “will give the wherewithals of living,”[^8] and life goes on.

It is such dauntless faith that mankind requires in the present crisis through which it is passing. True, the immediate prospects are dark. The danger that threatens is great. Wickedness is ensconced more securely on its throne than ever and the forces of evil are carrying the day. Only a miracle can now save civilization. But will it, can it happen? “Yes, it can,’ is the answer, “if only men have faith, for “the righteous lives by his faith.”

> “O, yet we trust that somehow good
> Will be the final goal of ill,
> To pangs of nature, sins of will,
> Defects of doubt and taints of blood,
> That nothing walks with aimless feet,
> That not one life shall be destroy’d,
> Or cast as rubbish to the void,
> When God hath made the pile complete.”
