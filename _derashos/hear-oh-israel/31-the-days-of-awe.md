“THE DAYS OF AWE”

“So let us declare the mighty holiness of this day, for it is one of reverence and awe.”[^1]

The collective name used in Hebrew to designate the solemn festivals with which the Jewish calendar year begins is Yamim Noraim, “days of awe.” It is during this pivotal period that the divine Judge is pictured as holding His annual assize and decreeing the destinies of nations and men. Not only individuals but “even countries are sentenced thereon unto war or peace, famine or plenty.”[^2] The title “days of awe” is, therefore, most aptly chosen. For who can be composed and tranquil when his future is hanging in the balance, when the uncertainties of life in general, the insecurity of man’s entire existence dawns upon his mind? How is it possible for him to escape a feeling of oppression when he recalls the uneven fluctuations of fortune, when he remembers how speedily the hourglass of his brief sojourn in his terrestrial home may run out. As the Chronicler notes[^3] man is but a transient tenant, a guest for a while in his nether abode. “Our days on earth are like a shadow.” “And ah if they would only be as fixed as the shadow of a wall or as enduring as the reflection of a tree,” commented our sages. “But alas they are as fleeting as the shadow cast by a bird in flight?[^4] “Man,” declared the psalmist, “is like unto vanity. His days are as a shadow that passeth away.”[^5] And so fear and trepidation used to fill the heart of the Jew on the days of awe in generations past.

But there was nothing unusual about such an attitude, for until comparatively recent times fear was regarded as the normal mental equipment of man. It was looked upon as the foundation of religion. “Primus in orbe does fecit timor” said the Latin proverb.[^6] “Fear first created gods in the world.” According to the psalmist “the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom.”[^7] Men were afraid of many things besides God. They feared the destructive angels that peopled the world. They were in dread of the “terrors of the night and the arrows flying by day, of the pestilence walking in darkness and of the fevers ravaging at nontime.”[^8] “Man,” they believed with Job, “was born unto trouble.”[^9] Sickness, famine and war were unavoidable and regular phenomena of human existence. A person might pray that the epidemics, that periodically swept over the world, decimating its population, might somehow or other pass him by. Psalm 91 used to be recited as an antidote against illness. The giving of charity was prescribed as a form of insurance against penury. “Give the tithe that thou mayest become rich,” ‘asser bish’vil shetit‘asher’, was the implication, according to the rabbis,[^10] of the first two words of Deuteronomy 14.22. But, of course, there was no absolute certainty that the prayer for health would prove effective or that the expectation of wealth would actually be realized.

In the last century or so an almost complete reversal, so far as this matter is concerned, has taken place in human thinking. With the progress made by medical science in combating disease and thereby extending the human life-span on the one hand, and the reduction, on the other, through social legislation, of the extremes of poverty with all their dread consequences, the two greatest worries of men, those concerning their physical wellbeing and the eking out of a livelihood, have been very much curtailed. Freedom from fear is no longer looked upon as an ideal impossible of realization, as a goal that can never be attained. Given time the human race, so it is believed, may yet, by means of wise manipulation succeed in reaching the happy state that its earliest ancestor never achieved merely because he was prevented, after tasting the fruit of the “tree of knowledge” in the Garden of Eden, from partaking also of the “tree of life” that grew there.

But in order to attain this felicity, say our modern doctors of the soul, man must first of all exorcise from his consciousness the emotion of fear itself, Nothing, such is their firm conviction, is as damaging to the human personality as fear. Fear makes craven cowards out of otherwise brave men. It turns would-be heroes into traitors. It brings about defeat before the adversary has been met. Fear is the chief cause of insanity and the foremost factor in all suicides. The imaginary devils that torment the human mind can be far more devastating than the real ones. Montaigne, the herald of the modern age of reason in France, observed, as far back as four centuries ago, that individuals who are merely threatened with the forfeiture of their estates or their freedom often live in greater anguish, with consequent loss of appetite and repose, than such as are actually poor or exiled. It is characteristic and also significant of the new attitude, that has begun to prevail in the civilized world of our day, that the two non-fiction books that have won greatest popularity among the reading public of our age, “Fear” and “Peace of Mind”, are both dedicated to the overcoming of the phobias that haunt human beings and upset their psychic equilibrium.

Yes, the conquest and subjugation, if not the actual elimination, of fear appeared to be one of modern humanity’s greatest triumphs, when it was suddenly dealt a setback that sent it reeling to the lowest rungs of mental depression. If the Jew of former times was tense on Rosh Hashanah on account of the uncertainty of whether during the twelve months to come, instead of being destined for life and health and prosperity, he might be sentenced, by circumstances beyond his control, to sickness, hardship or even death, how much more grim and ominous a meaning must the questions of “Who shall live and shall die?”[^11] have for us who dwell under the shadow of the atom bomb? With the memories of the world war still fresh in our minds, those of us who have sons of draft age, subject to call at any moment, are particularly concerned, for we have more than an inkling of what faces our children on the field of battle, and the mere prospect is terrifying. However even those who are in no immediate danger of being parted from their dearest ones cannot help being frightened when they try to visualize the consequences of the release of a single one of these deadliest of all missiles ever devised by the human genius — in comparison with which all the fiends and monsters of the past, real or fancied put together, shrivel into insignificance — over one of our large and populous industrial centers in the course of a third global conflict. The anticipation of such an eventuality is enough to throw him who thinks about it into a panic and drive him to distraction.

How shall we live with these fears? What can we do to bear up under the strain of worry occasioned by our apprehensions? Whither shall we turn for reassurance in order to quell at least a portion of our anxieties? Our scientific experts and our military strategists offer us but little comfort in our plight. The former have already informed us in no uncertain terms that there exists no real defense against the atom bomb once it had been discharged, while the latter are forced to admit that war is at best a gamble, and that, as it is waged today, warfare is total war that leaves no element of the population of the country attacked unaffected. For both these reasons our statesmen and diplomats did all they could to localize the Korean war and to prevent it from spreading to other parts of the globe. The possibility of the outbreak at any time of a third global conflagration has, however, thereby only been deferred, not eliminated. The question that the citizens of ours or other countries ask themselves in these days of awe is still, to use the phraseology of the psalmist: “Where can we find refuge from this terror? How will we be able to survive the impending crisis? What measures must we take to live through the ordeal that is confronting humanity?”

In the absence of alternatives we are again compelled to resort to the only real arsenal of hope and source of strength, that has so often lifted man out of the depths of despondency and the gloom of despair and filled him with faith and courage — our religion. At the conclusion of the poem Unetaneh Tokef we read that “penitence, prayer and righteousness avert the evil decree.” This remark requires an explanation, for it is by no means clear how the practice of these three virtues could alter our situation, what magical properties they might possess to soften the fury of the atom bomb. It becomes necessary, therefore, to analyze the statement, examining each term separately, to arrive at a thorough comprehension of its implications.

The first concept mentioned is that of Teshubah, repentance, the turning over of a new leaf, evidenced not only by the correction of faults but by the fulfillment of omissions as well. What now is the period of grace allowed for repentance? When should a man begin to put himself into a fit state for the hearing in the court of heaven? Rabbi Eliezer’s answer is “a day before his death.”[^12] But since no one knows when his summons may arrive, he must always hold himself in readiness, as the Preacher[^13] expressed it: “At all times let thy garments be white and let oil not be lacking on thine head.”[^14] In other words one essential for parrying the blow is preparedness, providing against any eventuality. “Happy is the man who is always afraid”,[^15] who takes every possible precaution in coping with the emergencies he has to face. Such normal fear, we learn from psychiatry, far from being injurious, as its exaggerated neurotic form undoubtedly is, serves a very useful purpose. Like pain it was created to serve as a danger signal, putting man on the alert against the peril that lies ahead, spurring him on to action when action is imperative. When fear sounds the alarm, adrenalin pours into the bloodstream making the pulse beat faster and quickening the sensibilities. “Thus fear will sometimes add wings to the heels just as on other occasions it nails them to the ground and fetters them so that they cannot move.” When man is prepared for the worst, resigned to anything that might happen, the worst often does not come to pass.

The classic illustration of this truth is the ‘Akedah, the binding of Isaac, that is so often referred to in the High Holiday liturgy. What was so remarkable, so outstanding about this act, that involved our ancestor Abraham and his son Isaac, to cause it to be cited so frequently in our prayers as the “merit of the fathers”? Why is it constantly held up as a symbol of Jewish martyrdom, even though the actual sacrifice was in this instance, at least, not carried out? The answer is given by the Torah itself. “And (the angel) said (to Abraham): Lay not thine hand upon the lad, neither do anything to him; for now I know that thou fearest God, seeing that thou hast not withheld thy son, thine only one, from Me.”[^16] The meaning of all this is quite obvious. God was not interested in the sacrifice itself. The offering up by Abraham of his son Isaac would have added nothing to the glory of the Master of the Universe. God’s purpose in His command to the first of the patriarchs was merely to test the latter’s loyalty, his readiness, if needs be, to make the supreme sacrifice. Once Abraham had passed that test, once he had shown by his movements that nothing would stand in his way in carrying out the behest of his divine master, the deed itself was no longer of any consequence. Yes, Abraham, and, as the Midrash notes, Isaac also,[^17] were ready for everything. They were steeled for the worst. That is why the worst did not come to pass. By the same token, as our sages observe, Sarah, who was weaker than they and had been kept in the dark about his intentions by her husband Abraham out of his anxiety to spare her, died from fright when the news reached her — the Midrash says Satan brought the information — that her child was about to be immolated on the altar.[^18] The death of the matriarch, it should be noted, is related by Scripture immediately after the account of the binding of Isaac.

“The best way to maintain peace,” George Washington is reported to have said, “is to be prepared for war.” This gospel preached by the father of our country has been endorsed by our elder statesman Bernard Baruch. It applies equally in the strategy to be followed in the war against fear. One reason is that man has no right to leave the disposition of his affairs entirely to the Almighty, to expect miracles to be wrought for his sake. God, according to the well-known adage, helps those that help themselves. “In whatever way a person wishes to go, there is he led.”[^19] Only when a human being has taken every precaution, when he has done whatever is in his power, is he justified in throwing himself on God’s mercy and hoping for His salvation. Also when the inevitability of some loss or sacrifice is recognized, whatever might be salvaged from the wreckage becomes a gain.

The second item in the three-point program outlined in our liturgy is “prayer”. The person who prays is of necessity an optimist, for if he were not convinced of the efficacy of prayer, he would not resort to this means of appealing to the Supreme Judge who controls his destiny. Like the patriarch Jacob he may, on account of his lack of merit, feel that he is unworthy of God’s clemency[^20] Yet since the Lord is his “light and salvation”,[^21] he is, like the psalmist, without fear, regardless of what fate may have in store for him. Paradoxical, isn’t it? But what alternative is there for man? As Solomon ibn Gabirol pointed out in his poem “The Royal Crown” that is still recited by our Sephardic brethren on the evening of the Day of Atonement:

> “And though Thou seekest out my sin,
> From Thee to Thee I fly to win
> A place of refuge, and within
> Thy shadow from Thy anger hide
> Until Thy wrath be turned aside.”

The optimistic view of life has greater logic in its favor than the pessimistic. As the ghetto Jew in “The World of Sholom Aleichem” put it, “there are always two possibilities.” If war were to break out tomorrow, one of two things will happen. Either you are sent to the front to fight or you are not. If you do not go to the front, all is well of course. If you do, there are still two possibilities, Either you will fall in battle or you will come through unscathed. If you survive — and the majority usually do — you should consider yourself fortunate and ‘feel grateful. If, on the other hand, you do not, why then your worries are over.

Is this an oversimplification of the facts? Perhaps, And yet since every human undertaking, every venture or adventure, whether it be in the field of business, professional life and even marriage, is a gamble, the only means at man’s disposal for making headway is to take the gamble and hope for the best. The psalmist confesses that he would most assuredly have fallen a prey to his adversaries, when “false witnesses rose Up against” him “breathing forth violence, were it not for” his “faith in the goodness of the Lord in the land of the living.”[^22] So too would all moral progress in the world have been stymied without the confidence

> “That somehow good
> Will be the final goal of il,
> To pangs of nature, sins of will,
> Defects of doubt and taints of blood,
> “That nothing walks with aimless feet,
> That not one life shall be destroyed,
> Or cast as rubbish to the void,
> When God hath made the pile complete.”

Finally there is listed as a counteragent against the evil decree Zedakah, which is not to be translated, as it often is, charity”,[^23] but according to its ground meaning of “righteousness” or “justice”. One of the greatest sources of strength in times of trouble and crisis, when man has to fight for his very existence, is a clear conscience, the conviction that the cause he is defending is just. This certainty makes him brave as a lion. It girds him with superhuman power and thereby assures his victory. It was chiefly because truth and justice were on their side that the armies of the Allies in the last world war prevailed against the minions of Hitler. It was for the same reason that the slender forces of the Yishub, the handful of virtually unarmed Jews in Palestine, won out against the numerically superior legions of their Arab attackers.

If the nations that stand for freedom and democracy are to be victorious in eventual showdown with the Communist aggressors, they must first put their own house in order. They must make certain that their conscience is clear, that they have not been guilty of injustice to the downtrodden and the oppressed by lending a hand to tyranny and despotism, that they have not taken advantage of the weak nor violated the rights of the underdog. No weapon is as powerful as the truth. God’s truth, said the psalmist, “is a shield and a buckler.”’[^24] “Justice,” according to the rabbinic maxim, “pierces mountains.”[^25] On the other hand there is nothing more enervating than the guilt complex of an uneasy conscience.

> “Though thy slumber may be deep;
> Yet thy spirit will not sleep.
> There are shades that will not vanish.
> There are thoughts thou canst not banish.”

“Penitence, prayer and righteousness avert the evil decree.” Preparedness, optimism and a clear conscience — these are the three props upon which we must lean in order to bear up under the anxieties and strains of these days of awe in which we live. If we are not to be crushed and pulverized by the fears engendered by the atom bomb and other monsters of man’s own making, that threaten the annihilation of our world, we must wrap ourselves in this threefold armor of the spirit. We will then at least be free from fear and proof against being overwhelmed by our own imaginings and worries. “Be thou not afraid of sudden terror, neither of the destruction of the wicked when it cometh.”[^26] “Ye may take counsel, but it will be annulled. Ye may speak the word, but it shall not stand, so long as God is with us.”
