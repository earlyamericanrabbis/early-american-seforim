ON BEING GOOD

Chapter 24 of the book of Genesis describes the courtship and marriage of the second patriarchal couple, Isaac and Rebecca. It was a strange kind of courtship, that whereby the daughter of Bethuel and sister of Laban the Aramean became the wife of the son of Abraham and Sarah, a courtship quite different from the usual and commonplace in our day. The wooing was done not by the man directly concerned in the marital union, in which it was intended to eventuate. It was carried out by a person whose only interest in the matter was the fulfillment of a mission but who, on account of his detachment, was able to pursue his goal objectively and go about his task rationally with his eyes wide open unaffected by mere emotion. In selecting a life-partner for his master’s son the servant Eliezer adopted as the criterion of fitness for that role not external qualities, that usually figure so high among the young in the choice of a mate; but those traits that are indicative of inner excellence, of character, of a beautiful soul. “Behold,” so he prayed, when he reached his destination, “I am standing at the fountain of water. Let it then be that the maiden to whom I will say ‘Let down, I pray thee, thy pitcher that I may drink, and she will say ‘Drink and also thy camels will I give drink, her hast thou appointed for thy servant for Isaac and thereby shall I know that thou hast shown kindness unto my master.”[^1] In short in the opinion of the major domo of our ancestor Abraham the prime requisite that the future mistress of the patriarchal household had to meet was benevolence, kindness, goodness of heart.

In the value scale of human ethics doing good occupies the position of first importance. The advice of the psalmist to the “man that desireth life, that loveth days that he may see good therein”[^2] is to “refrain from evil and do good”,[^3] and in the eyes of the sage of the Mishnah, Rabban Johanan son of Zakkai, the good heart was the source and fountainhead of all other virtues.[^4] That it is meritorious and praiseworthy to do good seems to be almost universally recognized. The Persians, Greeks and Romans, as well as the thinkers of the Middle Ages, are agreed on the cultivation of the summum bonum, the highest good, as the goal of human striving. The question is only: What does the word “good” stand for? Is it that he be good to himself or good to others, or does goodness lie somewhere between these two alternatives?

There is a view that would equate goodness with selflessness. The truly virtuous or good person, according to this theory, the perfect saint, is he who suppresses whatever individual desires or private ambitions, he may harbor, for the sake of his ideal. This ideal may be the will of God, the interests of state, the well-being of humanity as a whole or the future of his own people. Since time immemorial men have been in the habit of holding up as examples of commendable virtue the martyr and the hero. It was by the promise of reward in the here or the hereafter that human beings were for ages induced to suffer privation and torment, aye to forfeit life itself. Whether the compensation in prospect consisted of immediate entrance into paradise or eternal bliss in the life after death or the liberation of the working class from dependence on the employers of labor, the yoke was borne with equal patience and denial of self.

Over against this definition of goodness there is the directly opposite view that has gained dominance and prominence since the appearance of democracy in modern times. According to it “good” is that which enlarges and enhances the self. Whatever makes for the liberation of the individual, for the promotion of the dignity of the human personality, for the realization of man’s personal hopes, for the flowering of his talents, is declared to be good. Whatever thwarts or hampers their development is considered evil A maximum of individual liberty and a minimum of interference on the part of the government is the goal to be striven for. The function of the state is to be the servant of its citizens, not their master, in whose hands they are but tools and instruments.

Which of these two interpretations is the correct one? On which side does the truth lie? A study of the Jewish sources leads to the conclusion that it is to be found neither on the one nor on the other side but somewhere between the two extremes. Judaism does not view with favor the complete suppression of the claims of the self. It does not regard mortification of the flesh as a virtue nor does it look upon poverty as an ennobling ideal. It is, of course, fully aware of the fact that wealth often has the effect of producing arrogance in its possessors. “When Jeshurun waxed fat,” Moses testified, “he kicked.”[^5] Also a certain amount of control over the appetites is not only wholesome but even essential to the individual’s well-being. That is one reason for Israel’s dietary laws and its marital regulations. But this must not be construed as warrant for going overboard in the opposite direction On the contrary a human being’s usefulness to others depends to a very large extent on his personal fitness. One cannot serve God with a sick body, observed Maimonides. It is, therefore, the duty of the individual to keep well. He, that neglects or injures his health needlessly, is not only a fool but a sinner, too, in the eyes of this medieval Jewish sage. Furthermore in order to be a philanthropist a person must possess some means of his own. He, who is himself dependent on charity, can hardly be counted on to improve the lot of others. Man’s first duty is, therefore, to himself. Before he can think of helping his neighbors, his personal needs, however modest they be, must be taken care of. Economic independence is basic to any career of service to humanity, and to achieve it man must turn to himself. He cannot expect others to do it for him. “If I am not for myself,” said Hillel, “who will be for me?”[^6] Nay, in order to be able to sympathize with his fellow-travellers on earth in their trials and tribulations and be moved to satisfy their wants, a person must himself have experienced the discomfort of privation; and one can’t experience discomfort unless one loves and knows the feeling of comfort. The injunction of “Thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself”[^7] assumes love of self in the first instance.

The cultivation of the qualities of self-reliance, the strengthening of the autonomy of the individual, the liberation and development of man’s private resources and the elevation of the dignity of the human personality are unquestionably of prime importance in the attainment of whatever excellence may be achieved by man. Human progress has been greatest wherever a premium was put on private initiative and the highest incentives were given to self-help.

With all these undoubted advantages of systems of political democracy and free enterprise, such as the one under which we have been living in America, it is necessary to beware of the dangers inherent in such systems, which their opponents have been only too quick and ready to point out. To leave the individual too much to his own devices, they say, is to encourage selfishness. Furthermore setting up private profit as the basis of a country’s economy, instead of the actual needs of the population as a whole, must lead in the course of time to the concentration of wealth in the hands of a few, who, by the power they wield, become the dictators of the fate and fortunes and, eventually, the oppressors of the masses. The inequalities of extreme riches on the one hand and of abject misery on the other arouse envy. Envy breeds discontent, and discontent is the cause of those upheavals that have plagued humanity since the beginning of the existence of organized society. The only way to obviate such developments is to adopt the principle of “Let thy brother live with thee”,[^8] that is by establishing such conditions as will reduce the tensions due to the material imbalances existing in the world. This was recognized long ago by Israel’s sages. “Said Rabbi Judah the son of Simon ‘This poor man sits there and complains: “Wherein am I different from so-and-so that he should be sleeping in his own house while I am here?’ When, then, thou arisest and givest unto him (the means of alleviating his lot), as sure as thou livest, I shall account it unto thee, (says God), as if thou hadst made peace between Me and him!”[^9] The more widespread the area of general human prosperity, the greater will be the prospects of stability and peace in the world.

Now it is not so easy to carry out such a program without some regimentation or restriction of private initiative. The difficulty lies in the fact that men are not created equal and superior ability demands recognition even in states professing the doctrine of complete economic equality like modern Russia. It is not persons of talent that one has to worry about. “Seven years the famine raged,” says a Talmudic proverb, “but it never passed the door of the skilled craftsman.”[^10] It is those who are not endowed with outstanding ability that require protection; and such protection would be provided only by a society reared on the principle of the love of fellowman. The spiritual climate that would produce it has been portrayed by one of America’s foremost moralists as follows. “What is rich? Are you rich enough to help anybody? to succor the unfashionable and the eccentric? rich enough to make the Canadian in his wagon, the itinerant with his consul’s paper which commends him to the charitable, the swarthy Italian with his broken words of English, the lame pauper hunted by overseers from town to town, even the poor insane or besotted wreck of man or woman feel the noble exception of your presence and your house, from the general bleakness and stoniness... is not this to be rich, this only to be rightly rich?”

“There are those,” remarked the German Jewish novelist Berthold Auerbach, “who look upon selfishness as the highest form of wisdom and feel that it is only out of stupidity that human beings practice benevolence. In reality it is just the reverse. Only the judicious are really good. Only the clever know what is good for others, while at the first appearance of disadvantage to himself the stupid man deserts” True wisdom is always coupled with kindness. The proverbist in depicting the ideal woman describes her as one “that openeth her mouth with wisdom and on whose tongue is the law of lovingkindness.”[^11] Rebecca, who won the heart of Eliezer by her hospitality, turned out to be besides, as Scripture records, a prudent wife and far-sighted mother.

Goodness is then, as our analysis has shown, neither a denial of the self, nor its magnification beyond its dimensions to the point where it would crowd out everything else, but rather the extension of its sympathies to include in their orbit all the sons of man. It may be summarized in the two commandments of the Torah that have been cited before namely “Thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself”[^12] and “Let thy brother live with thee.”[^13]
