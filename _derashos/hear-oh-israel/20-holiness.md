HOLINESS

The assignment which the opening sentence of chapter 19 of the book of Leviticus presents to the reader is one that appears to be from the very start impossible of execution. Of the adjectives used to describe the personality of God there is none that characterizes His nature more fully than that of his sanctity. “Holy, holy, holy is the Lord of hosts. The whole earth is full of His glory” sang the angels in the vision of Isaiah[^1] Holiness is the quality which marks off the Supreme Being, the author and source of all things, from His creatures. Over against Him, who has been described as “mighty in holiness,”[^2] all else must be construed as secular, worldly, mundane, profane. How, then, one may ask, can beings, which by virtue of their worldliness, are the very antithesis of godliness, be expected to emulate or resemble God in His attribute of holiness? What reason is there for assuming that those, whose mortality and physical nature render them congenitally unclean, might be able to measure up to the lofty spiritual stature of a Being, that is perfection itself, so that the command could be issued to them of “Holy shall ye be for I the Lord, your God, am holy!”? Yet that is precisely the goal that the Torah holds out to its followers. Israel was to become “a kingdom of priests and an holy nation.”[^3] Its life was to be sanctioned by the fulfillment of a set of rules and disciplines prescribed for its conduct, and the rationale, the motivation underlying these rules and regulations that were to guide its actions, was “For thou art an holy people unto the Lord thy God.”[^4] “Therefore shall thy camp be holy.”[^5]

It is obvious that in order to arrive at a correct understanding of these injunctions it would be necessary first to make a thorough analysis of the implications of the concept of holiness. What do we actually mean when we say “God is holy” or that a particular place is sacred or that a certain individual or thing are invested with the quality of sanctity?

The first word that comes to mind when the term “holiness” is mentioned is that of reverence. “Holy art thou,” so we say in the Amidah of the High Holidays, “and revered is Thy name ” The God, who is “mighty in holiness” is also “revered in praises.”[^6] When the patriarch Jacob awoke from his sleep, in which he had dreamed about God, he exclaimed. “Indeed, how reverent is this site!” When Moses heard the voice of deity calling to him from the burning bush, he was moved to remove his shoes in token of reverence.[^7] Such, too, was Joshua’s reaction when the divine emissary addressed him outside of the walls of Jericho.[^8]

The acknowledgment of the holiness of a being or object is, then, in the first place, equivalent to the recognition of their supreme value, which causes him who entertains this exalted opinion of their worth to treat them with circumspection and consideration When for example it is asserted that “‘life is sacred,” what is meant thereby is that it must not be tampered or dealt lightly with but protected at all costs. Similarly the expression “the sanctity of the plighted word” implies that once a promise has been made, there is no reneging on it, but every effort must be exerted to honor it. Holiness produces, therefore, in him who sees it, reverence, veneration, respect, and an attitude of worship and humble compliance.

But what is it that makes those who are supposed to be endowed with this quality holy? How is the trait of holiness acquired? How does it come to a person or thing? Jewish religious ideology singles out two distinct types of sanctity. There is Kedushat Hagguf, intrinsic holiness, a holiness due to the holy object itself, an inseparable part of its essence and nature, and Kedushat Damm, which is rendered “derived sanctity,” that is to say a quality which the thing regarded as holy possesses by virtue of the fact that such a quality has been conferred upon it by means of consecration. Thus, for instance, it is not the composition of the soil that makes a cemetery holy ground. The mineral content of its dirt may be no different from that of any other piece of earth. It is the fact that the remains of human beings, who were greatly beloved, are buried there that invests the land, in which they are interred, with sanctity. Similarly the parchment used in making Torah scrolls is nothing more than the skin of animals — something very secular and banal, As for the ink used in writing on it, it may be chemically the same as any other ink. It is what is written on these scrolls, their contents and the significance, that these contents have for those that hold the scrolls in esteem, that makes them holy.

If we start out with this as a premise, we must conclude that nothing outside of God Himself and the gifts He bestows on His creatures, such as life and its functions, is intrinsically holy. Everything else, buildings, festivals, sacrificial objects called holy, are holy merely by virtue of having been declared holy through dedication or consecration. There is hardly anything in this world, however unimportant and valueless it may appear to be in and by itself, that is not susceptible of sanctification, that is of attaining the quality of holiness by being made holy. The bread men eat, the work they do, the relations between a human being and his neighbor, the bonds between husband and wife, between parents and children, a person’s dealings with his employees, with customers, clients and associates — all these are capable of being uplifted to a point where their sacred character becomes a matter of public knowledge. What is needed is only an ideal purpose, an ennobling objective, a worthy aim. The menial tasks performed by a physician of the calibre of an Albert Schweitzer for the primitive inhabitants of an obscure corner in darkest Africa evoke the admiration of the entire human world. Ghandi is remembered as a saint not because he was made of different stuff than other human beings. He could certainly not have lived to the ripe age of 78, when an assassin’s bullet put an end to his life, without the necessary vitamins to sustain him. The poverty he affected and the long-protracted self-imposed fasts were more for show than anything else. “You don’t know,” confessed a Hindu who was very intimate with the famous Mahatma, “how much it cost us to keep Ghandi poor.” Special provisions had to be made, as he travelled around the country, for housing the goat that supplied him with milk and supplying him with the hut he used as a shelter and the loom on which he did his weaving. The extreme veneration in which the idol of the Hindu people was held by his followers was due chiefly to the fact that his life had been dedicated to the liberation of 450,000,000 of his fellow-Indians.

The ground meaning of the word for holy in Hebrew, namely Kadosh is like that of Harem in Arabic, something aloof, separated, withdrawn from common use. The formula used by Jewish husbands under the wedding canopy in betrothing their wives is: “Behold thou art consecrated unto me.” The meaning is. “From now on you belong to me exclusively. You are no longer in the market as the potential bride of anyone else.” In reality, however, true holiness must not be thought of as consisting of running away from the world so as to be able to practice austerity without disturbance. It is related that Rabbi Elijah of Wilna, popularly known as the “Wilner Gaon”, who was reputed to have led a very saintly life, once asked the celebrated preacher, Rabbi Jacob of Dubno, who was a contemporary of his, to give him a lesson in true saintliness. The Maggid replied: “Leave your home and go out into the wicked world. It is easy to be a saint in such surroundings as your household, where you are shielded against temptation. But you betake yourself to places in which your virtue will be challenged at every step by the sirens that lurk everywhere. If you will there be able to maintain your integrity and your innocence, then you will indeed have proven yourself to be a real saint ”

Genuine saintliness is to mingle with the society of man and endeavor to elevate it from its lowly position to a higher, loftier plane of existence. No one is holier than God Almighty, who is the very essence of holiness. Nevertheless it is said of this holy God that He “dwells among the children of Israel in their defilement”[^9] This is, then, the purport and meaning of the command “Holy shall ye be for I the Lord your God am holy!” — not to shut oneself up in one’s own ivory tower, to seek refuge behind the protecting walls of a cloister, but to interest oneself in whatever concerns one’s fellow-travellers in the world, to share their problems, to take part in their joys and sorrows, to live not only a life that is free from reproach but one that is rich in constructive accomplishments. “Hate not thy brother in thine heart, but correct thy friend (if he is in need of being set right.”[^10] “Be neither vengeful nor vindictive... but love thy neighbor as thyself.”[^11] “Commit ye no unrighteousness in judgment but always have just weights and measures.”[^12] If you will thus conduct yourselves, you will have fulfilled, as much as it is humanly possible, the injunction of “Holy shall ye be, for I the Lord your God am holy.”[^13]
