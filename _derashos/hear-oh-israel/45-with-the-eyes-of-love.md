WITH THE EYES OF LOVE

The Song of Songs, which is read in the synagogue on the Sabbath of the intervening days of Passover, is one of those books of the Bible concerning which there existed some doubt among the fixers of the Jewish canon as to whether it was to be admitted into the august company of the Holy Writings.[^1] The reason for their hesitancy is not difficult to see. In its literal meaning this little work would hardly pass for a religious book. God’s name isn’t even once mentioned in it unless we recognize in the ending yah of the word shalhevetyah[^2] an abbreviation of the tetragrammaton, the four letters constituting His Jewish proper name. Nor is there as much as a hint of man’s duties to his Maker or to his fellow-mortals. The seven chapters, into which the slender volume is divided, appear to consist almost entirely of ecstatic outbursts of sensuous, passionate human love, in which no scene is barred and no description is censored. Nevertheless this collection of presumably erotic lyrics was, after having been reinterpreted as an amorous dialogue between the Heavenly Bridegroom and His bride Israel, not only incorporated into Israel's sacred literature, but declared by so authoritative a voice as Rabbi Akiba’s as “holy of holies.”[^3] And properly so, because a religion that lacks the fervor and passion of love is no religion. It is as flat and uninspiring as a mathematical equation or a chemical formula. The first command in the Sh’ma‘, the Jewish confession of faith, following the affirmation that “God is one” is: “And thou shalt love the Lord thy God.”[^4] And how must a human being love God? By what means can he show that his attachment to his Maker is genuine, sincere? Not by praising God only when He is the dispenser of blessings and bounty, but by accepting Him regardless of what He may decree for His creature. “Whatever measure He may mete out to thee,” says the Mishnah in explanation of the phrase d’kol m’odeka,[^5] “thank Him exceedingly.” This was also the attitude of Job, as evidenced by his statement: “Even though He slay me, yet do I hope unto Him.”[^6] A love that will not take the evil together with the good is not worth wasting time on. Only love is broadminded enough to embrace both; and the reason for its generosity and largesse is that it seeks out and directs its attention exclusively to the good, and, being preoccupied therewith, overlooks and condones the evil, as the proverbist expressed it “Love covers up all transgressions.”[^7] In the love-filled eyes of the lover his beloved is “all beautiful and there is no blemish in her,”[^8] even though she herself may be quite conscious of her imperfections, as the shepherdess admits in the Song of Songs: “I am black.”[^9] But these shortcomings fade away so that they seem not to exist in the moment of ecstasy, when the lovers are wrapped up in one another, exclaiming: “I belong to my beloved and beloved belongs to me.”[^10]

Now all this may apply to equals. Two human beings in love with one another may to disregard each other’s faults on account of the virtues that appeal to them. “If you will not carp at me, I will not pick on you.” But how can this measuring rod be applied to such disparities as those which exist between God and man? Concerning her Heavenly Bridegroom, the mortal bride must perforce agree, regardless of how His chastisements may hurt that “The Rock, His work is perfect, for all His ways are justice.”[^11] On the other hand, however, could the Bridegroom in this instance truthfully say to the bride: “Thou art all beautiful, my beloved, and there is no blemish in thee?” Surely God, whose seal is truth and who looks into the innermost recesses of the human heart, is not to be deceived. He knows how sinful Israel has been. Isn’t, therefore, the allegory being carried too far in this case? Isn’t the figure of speech being stretched to a point where it becomes absurd?

The rabbinic homilists do not think so. There was a moment, they say, when Israel was free from blemish, a moment when those who were but human and fraught with all the weaknesses of the flesh rose to the spiritual stature of angels.[^12] That was the time when, upon hearing the offer of the Eternal at Sinai, the Israelites replied in one acclaim: “All that the Lord hath spoken we shall do.”[^13] Such moments may be rare, but they are not, therefore, completely ruled out. And it is necessary for those who are steeped in the quagmire of mundane life to know that they are capable of rising to such heights. It is necessary for bolstering their self-confidence, for otherwise they would never make the effort to hoist themselves up, but sink ever deeper and deeper into the mud. There is nothing unusual about human selfishness, indifference, forgetfulness, lack of gratitude. It is the natural mode of behavior of beings created as self-contained microcosms, little worlds unto themselves of only limited duration. Nothing is gained by calling attention to and thus, perhaps, magnifying a common failing. What is surprising is that man should be able to conquer his natural impulses, and, if he is to be helped in this endeavor, it is these triumphs that must be sought out for commendation.

This was the method employed by the saintly Rabbi Levi Isaac of Berdichev. When one of his followers came to him with a complaint against the seemingly sacrilegious conduct of a certain coachman, who was found greasing his wagon while wearing his phylacteries, the great apologete for Jewry exclaimed in ecstasy: “What a wonderful people Thy Jews are, oh Master of the Universe! Even when they grease their wagons their minds are filled with thoughts of Thee.”

The Song of Songs in its rabbinic interpretation became so popular with Jews because, by building up their ego, by picturing the community of Israel in a more ideal ight than was warranted perhaps by reality, it supplied Jewry with a goal to strive for. Thus for instance the words “I am asleep but my heart is awake”[^14] received the following meaning in their allegory.[^15] “Said the congregation of Israel before the Holy One Blessed Be He: ‘I am asleep so far as the fulfillment of the commandments is concerned. Yet my heart is awake to the performance of deeds of kindness. I may be asleep so far as charity is concerned. Yet is my heart awake to the practice thereof. I may be asleep in regard to the sacrifices. Yet is my heart awake to the recitation of the Sh’ma‘ and prayer. I may be asleep in regard to the Temple. Yet is my heart awake for synagogues and houses of study. I am asleep as regards the end. Yet is my heart awake unto the redemption.’ ”

Such was the view Jews obtained of their people when seen with the eyes of love. Whether truthful or not, it must have been of tremendous encouragement to them in their striving for self-improvement, which is a gift vouchsafed, of all God’s creatures, to man and man alone, as a poet of our age expressed it:

> “Progress, man’s distinctive mark alone,
> Not God’s, and not the beasts’, God is, they are;
> Man partly is, and wholly hopes to be.”
