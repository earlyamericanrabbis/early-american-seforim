WATER AND THE TORAH

The two concluding days of the Jewish fall festive season are referred to in the liturgy of the synagogue by one and the same name Sh’mini ‘Azeret, “the eighth (day constituting) the conclusion festival”. Yet they have distinct and separate functions that appear to be totally unrelated to each other. The distinguishing feature of the first of these two days is the prayer for rain, that is intercalated in the Mussaph service with a motif all its own, while the ritual characterizing the second are the Hekefot or processions around the synagogue of the Torah scrolls, which are responsible for the name by which it is popularly known of Simchat Torah, “the rejoicing over the Torah”.

The reason for the recitation by Jewry of the prayer for rain on the day after the return from the Sukkah is that that day marks the beginning of the rainy season in the Land of Israel; and water, which is next to the air he breathes the element most necessary to sustain man’s life, has since its earliest history been the number one problem of the country looked upon by Jews as the land of their fathers. Moses described it, to the Israelites wandering in the wilderness, as a country requiring God’s constant solicitude. “It is a land which the Lord, thy God careth for, the eyes of the Lord thy God are always upon it from the beginning of the year even unto the end of the year.”[^1] The patriarchs realized this fact whenever famine broke out on their pasture lands in Canaan and they were compelled to seek relief from the periodic drought by journeying with their flocks and their herds to better watered grazing fields in the neighboring countries.[^2] That is how the prayer for rain came to occupy so important and prominent position in the traditional Jewish liturgy, that has its roots in the Land of Israel. That is also why of all the festivals celebrated at the national sanctuary in Jerusalem during the days of the second Temple none was more joyous than that of “the drawing of water” during the week of the feast of Tabernacles. “He that has not witnessed the rejoicing of the house of (water)-drawing,” reports the Mishnah, “has never seen a joyous celebration.”[^3] The purpose of the procession of the Torah scrolls on Simchat Torah, again, is to celebrate the completion on that day of the reading of the book, that has been regarded as the Jew’s most treasured possession, his inseparable companion throughout the ages, given to him before his entrance into the Promised Land and remaining with him after his expulsion from his national home. Now neither the Mishnah, nor its commentary, the Talmud, make any mention of the festival of Simchat Torah. Evidently the special exercises of that day and the merriment associated with it did not originate until after the Talmudic era and outside of the boundaries of the Holy Land.

Sh’mini ‘Azeret and Simchat Torah seem, therefore, on the surface, at least, to be ideologically as far apart as they could possibly be. The one has a purely agricultural significance. It is bound up with things as apparent to the senses as climate, as physical as the growth of the soil, and as concrete as the weather. The other, on the other hand, is completely detached from the earth or aught that springs from it. It revolves about something as non-material and abstract as the contents of a book, and as intangible as the words of the Torah. Nevertheless, despite their great divergence in character and meaning, Sh’mini ‘Azeret and Simchat Torah are really very close to each other, because they are complementary as well as parallel, like two facets of one and the same coin.

They are parallel, because what water is to the human body that the Torah has been to the soul of the Jew. Just as the Jew could not survive physically without moisture, so would he perish spiritually if he did not steep himself in the teachings that molded the character and created the personality of his people. The Torah has often been compared to water by Israel’s prophets and teachers. “Ho, every one that thirsteth,” said Isaiah[^4] to his contemporaries, “come ye for water,””? while Jeremiah bemoaned the fact that Israel had forsaken “the fountain of living waters, to dig themselves broken wells that hold no water.”[^5] So long as Jews drank this water, Jewish life was vigorous, strong, and all attacks upon Jewry failed. Whenever they allowed the source to run dry, what we would call “Judaism” withered like a plant parched with thirst.

The rain for which Jews pray on Sh’min ‘Azeret and the love and longing they express for the Torah on Simchat Torah do, then, exhibit extraordinary similarities, correspondences and parallelisms. But the two festivals also complement each other by serving converse purposes. The first, because it is so directly bound up with the climatic conditions peculiar to the geography of the Land of Israel, has the mission of bringing the Jews that live outside the boundaries of the Holy Land closer to the material needs of the Jewish national home. The prayer for rain, which has no meaning at this time of the year in countries like the United States of America, with its heavy precipitation in summer as well as winter, reminds us of how vital an adequate water supply is to the very existence of the Jewish national home and ought to spur us American Jews on to do whatever lies in our power to assure its availability.

There was a time when all that could be done about this matter was to pray and wait for the Omnipotent, who is the one “that causeth the wind to blow and the rain to come down”,[^6] to do the rest. In those days saints like Chon, the Circle-drawer[^7] and Chanina the son of Dosa,[^8] who knew their way around in the household of the divine Rain-maker, were the heroes of the hour, and it was on the effectiveness of their supplications that Jews placed all their reliance. In our age it has been discovered that it is not necessary to place the entire burden on the Chonis and Channa son of Dosas. For it is possible, by artificial irrigation and the use of synthetic fertilizers to make the barren soil fruitful and cause the desert to bloom. If only the means for such irrigation and soil reclamation could be provided, the results would be virtually underwritten. Whoever supplies these means, whether by outright gift or in the form of a loan in the guise of an Israel Bond subscription, performs an act of benevolence and piety equivalent to those of Israel’s holy men of antiquity. Such tangible assistance should be the outcome of the Sh’mini ‘Azeret celebration of Jews living outside of Israel.

Simchat Torah has the diametrically opposite function of reminding our brethren in the Holy Land of what it is that unites them with world Jewry. It is self-evident that the bond could not consist of anything that has to do with the soil or is connected with the Land of Israel, because that might immediately lay the Jews living outside the Holy Land open to the charge of dual allegiance. The common denominator must, therefore, be something that is not dependent on residence in the Jewish national home, something that can be cultivated and have validity anywhere and everywhere on God’s earth. Such a bond of Jewish unity is the Torah. So long as this Torah is studied and observed, Jewry lives. Once it is abandoned, Israel dies. “Our people is a people only by virtue of its Torah,”[^9] declared Saadia Gaon more than one thousand years ago. And he was right. Having his home within the historic boundaries of the land once inhabited by Israel’s ancestors, even when this is coupled with speaking the Hebrew language, is not sufficient to make a person a Jew. The Canaanites, that preceded the Israelite occupation of the Holy Land, and the Arabs, that succeeded it, had lived in that country longer than the present Jewish population without losing their Canaanite or Arab identities. And the Moabites, Ammonites, Edomites and Phoenicians spoke an idiom that was closer to the language of the Scriptures than the picturesque but grammatically unhebraic speech of the street-urchins of Tel-Aviv or the truck-drivers of the kibbutzim, and yet they were never included in the community of Israel. Unless Israel will resume the historic role, with which it was once credited by the prophets, unless the ancient hope is realized that “from Zion will come forth Torah and the word of God from Jerusalem”,[^10] there is danger that in the course, not of centuries but decades, Israel might lose the attachment of the Diaspora. A situation in which Jewish religious tradition would be upheld in New York and Baltimore and spurned in Jerusalem and Haifa is as incongruous as it would be intolerable. If the two branches of the house of Israel are to remain bound together and not break up into two different and distinct parts, there must be something that they have in common and that something is the Torah. To be sure “without flour there can be no Torah.”[^11] Without the satisfaction by Diaspora Jewry of material needs of their brethren in the Land of Israel the former can expect no return from the latter in the form of inspiration. It is equally true, however, that “without Torah no flour will be forthcoming.”[^12] Without the strong spiritual bond that is represented by the Torah, tying them to the Jewish homeland, there would be but little desire on the part of Diaspora Jewry to supply to the Yishub in the Holy Land the flour of which it stands so greatly in need.

Sh’mini ‘Azeret and Simchat Torah, therefore, complement each other. The former is the festival pointing to the land of Israel in which it originated. The latter is the “second day added and observed by Diaspora Jewry.”[^13] Both together assure the existence and survival of our people and its faith, of Jewry and its spiritual culture. In order to make certain that “Reuben live and die not,”[^14] it is necessary to fulfill the condition upon which that result hinges, namely that “the Torah bequeathed unto us by Moses become the inheritance of the congregation of Jacob.”[^15]
