GOD AND PEOPLE

The book of Ruth, which is read on the second day of Shabuot, carries us back to the early years of Israel’s settlement in its national home. The tale that is told in it concerns an event that is supposed to have taken place in the fields of Bethlehem, Judah, during the days of the Judges, that is before the establishment of a kingdom. A unified nation with a strong centralized government was still a thing of the future, waiting for a David, who was not born before another three generations had come and gone, to take it in hand and whip it into shape. Nevertheless the character of what was later to be known as “the Jew” was already pretty clearly defined, even if it was not yet fully formed. All this is indicated in what the little scroll from the division of the Bible called the K’tubim or “Writings”, that is read before the lesson from the Pentateuch, relates about Ruth, the Moabite widow of a Judean nobleman, who, after her conversion to Judaism, married her dead husband’s kinsman, Boaz, and thus became the ancestress of Israel’s ideal king David.

What did this daughter of an alien people do in order to prove her complete identification with the family of her departed Jewish spouse? By what means did she effect her integration or merger with those whom she embraced as her own? Was it done by the mere change of habitat? Did she become a Jewess by simply moving physically from her native city in Moab to Bethlehem? No, it wasn’t as easy as that. Ruth’s admittance into the fold of Israel was the result of her declaration to her mother-in-law Naomi, who had tried to persuade her widowed daughter-in-law to return to her home, that she had made up her mind that thenceforth her -— that is Naomi’s — people would become her people, and Naomi’s God — that is the God of Israel — would also be her God. “Wherever you will die, I will die, and there will I be buried. May the Lord do thus unto me and more. Only death shall separate me from you”.[^1]

“Thy people shall be my people and thy God my God.”[^2] In Hebrew this sentence consists of only four words, but what a world of meaning is contained in these words! How many questions would be answered and how many difficulties be resolved if their full implication would be recognized and the thought behind them would be implemented and acted upon!

One of the chief causes of misunderstanding, producing conflict among Jews in our day, is the difference in their conception of the nature of Judaism. A large number of Jews put all the emphasis on the national or ethnic bonds that bind Jews throughout the world together. What makes a person a Jew, they say, is not the fact that he holds certain views about God, that he belongs to a synagogue, that he has been married by a rabbi and observes the Jewish dietary laws. You can be a very reputable and accepted member of the Jewish community, aye of the Jewish people, such is their sincere conviction, without fulfilling all these conditions. As proof they point to a large section, perhaps the majority of the Jewish citizens of modern Israel, who do none of the things enumerated and whom no one, with the possible exception of such extremists as the Neturei Karta, would on that account read out of the Jewish fold. And who would repudiate — even though they were not religiously observing Jews —- men of the calibre of a Brandeis or Einstein, or, to distinguish between the living and the dead, a Yehudi Menuhin and a Bernard Baruch? Jewishness is, therefore, aver the advocates of ethnic or national Judaism, not a matter of belief, the practice of certain rites or the adherence to a specific code of laws, but of descent or the feeling of belonging, of the recognition of certain bonds of racial affinity, the acknowledgment of a common past, an indivisible present and a joint future. To show that they are one, Jews must do certain things together, such as speak the same language, support the same charitable institutions, belong to Jewish organizations like B’nai B’rith or the National Council of Jewish Women, work for the upbuilding of Israel etc. If they do that, theology, the views they may entertain about God or the religious traditions of the Bible and the Talmud, are of but minor importance.

On the other hand there are in the lands of the Diaspora, notwithstanding the existence at present of the state of Israel, many proud, intelligent and conscientious Jews, who persist in the opinion that, outside of the borders of the Jewish national home, the term Jew can have no other connotation than that of membership in a clearly recognizable religious community. An individual may be more or less observant. He may subscribe to the Orthodox, Conservative, Reform or Reconstructionist version of Judaism. He may attend synagogue only three times a year, or every Sabbath or every day, or pay only lip-service to the Jewish religion. Or else he may consider himself Jewish merely because his parents professed the Jewish belief. Yet, if he were to be called upon in the United States of America to fill out a questionnaire, he would unquestionably put down under the rubric of nationality the word “American.” The only heading under which his classification as Jewish could figure would be that of religion, even though he be in reality religiously an atheist or agnostic.

It is this difference of interpretation that is responsible in a large measure for the friction between American Zionists on the one hand and spokesmen of Israel, such as Prime Minister Ben Gurion, on the other. To the latter Judaism is first and foremost a matter of nationality. They look upon all Jews of the Diaspora as potential citizens of Israel and lose no opportunity of pleading with them to immigrate into and settle in the Jewish homeland. Nor can one blame them for feeling and behaving as they do. The infant Jewish republic, that is struggling so desparately for its security and survival, cannot afford to overlook any accretion of strength from the outside. While, of course, every Jew seeking a haven of refuge in Israel, no matter how destitute or economically undesirable he may be, is received with open arms, it 18 immigrants with means and skills and know-how that are sought by it most eagerly.

Jews living in the democracies outside of Israel, on the other hand, however deep their attachment to Zion may be, feel that politically they must give priority to the lands of their residence. What distinguishes them from the rest of the citizens of the countries, in which they have their homes, is their religious culture. Jewish life in the Diaspora, social as well as religious, naturally revolves around the synagogue. It is, therefore, the religious bond that is the strongest. And to obtain the religious inspiration they crave for from the Jewish homeland, they do not feel that it is necessary for them to pull up their stakes from the places of their birth or residence, where life is easy and pleasant and food is abundant and opportunities for making a livelihood are plentiful, in order to brave the austerities and privations of a little country with limited resources. As for carrying out their vows and fulfilling their duties as Jews, they believe that that could be accomplished by occasional pilgrimages to the Holy Land, which always benefits from the visits of tourists and thrives by the revenues they bring into its coffers.

The truth of the matter is that they are both right and both wrong. They are right insofar as that which each group stresses and regards as all-important is an essential element of that complex organism that is called Judaism. The national aspects are much more pronounced in the way of life that is known as the Jewish religion than in its daughter faiths, Christianity and Islam. Judaism is primarily the religion of the Jewish people, whose history st sanctifies and whose past is surrounded by it with a halo of glory. It was entirely natural that the days observed as religious festivals during the close to 2,000 years of Jewish homelessness in the lands of the Jew’s dispersion should be celebrated as national holidays in the rebuilt Jewish homeland. Again it cannot be denied that no matter how secular the modern Jewish commonwealth may endeavor to be, its civilization is the outgrowth and heir of the predominantly religious culture that preceded it, whereby the character of its people was molded for more than three millennia. It is impossible — and it must therefore not be attempted — completely to shake off the past. What Saadia noted more than 1000 years ago still holds true today. “Our nation is a nation only because of its Torah.”[^3]

Nationality and religion, people and God, are therefore the two strands, the web and the woof of which Judaism is woven. To eliminate either would be disastrous, for the fabric would thereby be destroyed. Without the help of the Diaspora Jewry, in which the belief in Israel’s God is strong, the state of Israel could hardly survive. It must, therefore, cultivate that which means the most to Diaspora Jewry. It must supply that religious inspiration which would bind this indispensable member of the family close to it. On the other hand, without the unifying force that is represented by Israel’s nationhood, Diaspora Jewry is liable, by the force of circumstances, to disintegrate and break up into so many separate and distinct fragments, which would, by their very fragmentization, be doomed to eventual disappearance. The only bond to hold them together is the feeling of kinship fostered by attachment to a virile, dynamic national center to which they can all look with pride.

A complete Judaism is, then, one that embraces God as well as people. “Thy people shall be my people and thy God my God.” Only he who accepts both is a full Jew. Whoever fails to meet these requirements, whatever his contributions may be in the one or the other respect, no matter how great his fame and how extensive his accomplishments, is only a partial, marginal, borderline Jew.

This is what Ruth taught us long, long ago. It is still as valid now as it was in the eleventh or twelfth century before the common era, when, to use the phraseology of Scripture, the Moabite wife of a Jewish nobleman forsook her father and her mother and the land of her birth to join a people she had not known previously in order to seek refuge beneath the wings of the Lord God of Israel.[^4]
