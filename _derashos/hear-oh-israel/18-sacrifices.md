SACRIFICES

The contents of the opening chapters of the book of Leviticus have for the average intelligent person of our day, whether he be Jewish or gentile, but little genuine appeal. The age of animal sacrifices, of the burning on the altar of the flesh of beasts as an expression of devotion to God, is long already past so far as the civilized human world is concerned. Nearly 19 centuries have elapsed since the last offering of this sort was made by Jews in the Temple of Jerusalem and long before that time, according to the testimony of Scripture, the prophets of Israel had deprecated this form of worship. Samuel reminded Saul, after the latter’s campaign against the Amalekites, that obedience of the commandments of the Lord was preferable to sacrifices.[^1] Isaiah branded offerings brought by hands filled with blood as “an abomination, intolerable in the sight of God.”[^2] The psalmist indicated how senseless it was to attempt to bribe with gifts of bullocks or he-goats Him to whom belonged “every beast of the forest and cattle upon a thousand hills.[^3] Besides what does a spiritual being like the Creator and ruler of the universe need them for? “Do I eat the flesh of bulls or drink the blood of he-goats’”[^4] Yet it must be noted that the laws concerning the sacrifices were just as much part of Israel’s Torah as the “golden rule” of “love thy neighbor as thyself’, and the offering of the pascal lamb was as integral an element in the observance of a holiday like Passover as as the eating of the Matzot and the avoidance of what was leavened. How is this discrepancy to be explained? Does it not point, as modern Bible criticism insists, to the existence of two diametrically opposite and conflicting conceptions of how God could best be served, that on the one hand of the priests with their emphasis on ritual and on the other hand that of the prophets with their stress on ethical behavior, their tireless denunciation of every violation of justice and their unflagging zeal in furthering man’s moral progress?

The answer given by that profound Jewish thinker, one of the greatest perhaps of all times, Moses Maimonides, who, unlike Rabbi Joseph Karo, the author of the Shulchan ‘Arukh, included in his code “the Strong Hand” even the now obsolete laws of the sacrifices, the answer given by him in the 32nd chapter of the third part of his “Guide of the Perplexed” was that the divine lawgiver proceeded, in His training of the people He had selected to be the bearers of His name and the transmitters to the world of the true belief in God, as a wise pedagogue would in the upbringing of an untutored child. Man, He knew, was a creature of the habits. Education was, therefore, necessarily a slow and long-drawn-out process. In order to break an individual, be he young or old, of the bad habits, to which he has grown accustomed, and instill in him more desirable traits, what must be avoided at all costs is the mistake of going suddenly from one extreme to the other. The transition, to be successful, must be gradual, step by step. That was exactly the method followed by Him who gave the Torah to Israel.

At the time when the Torah was promulgated at Sinai, observes Maimonides, the mode of worship prevalent throughout the human world was that of sacrificing animals on the altar. Had the Israelites been forbidden to serve their God in this universally accepted manner, the effect that such an order would have had on them would have been like the reaction of an edict in our day prohibiting prayer and fasting and other such religious practices. It would have been regarded as equivalent to the abolition of religion altogether. To prevent such a negative result and at the same time wean the Israelites away from their idolatrous practices, the heavenly master, who did not need oblations and holocausts, ordered the Israelites to transfer the homage they used to pay to the deities of the heathen pantheon to Him. “If any person among you wishes to make an offering,”[^5] then let it be “unto the Lord ” “For whoever sacrifices to (any other) gods shall be utterly destroyed.”[^6] “The sanctuary that you will erect must be dedicated to Me.”[^7] “The priests (too) are to minister to Me.”[^8]

To further prove his point concerning the method adopted by God in the task of training Israel, Maimonides calls attention to the many limitations placed on the sacrificial cult. Prayer, which after the destruction of the Temple of Jerusalem superseded completely the previous form of worship, as Hosea said “So let us compensate with our lips for bullocks,”[^9] could be recited by anybody at all times and in all places. “In whatever place I will cause My name to be mentioned will I come to thee and bless thee[^10] However the performance of the sacrificial service was restricted to a single family, that of Aaron, to one central locality, Jerusalem, and to the minimum fixed by law. The daily sacrifice consisted of but lamb in the morning and one in the late afternoon.[^11] As for the socalled atonement sacrifices demanded of individuals, such as the “sin-offering” and the “guilt-offering,” they did not really “atone” for — that is literally “erase” or “wipe off? — guilt, because they applied only when sins were committed by mistake, erroneously, unwittingly and without intent. They were invalid where the trespass was deliberate, presumptuous, with full knowledge of the implications and consequences.

The purpose of the ritual was to rouse man from his lethargy, to make him aware of his responsibilities, to touch his soul, for, as the psalmist noted. “The (real) sacrifices to God are a broken spirit.”[^12] That it was not the quantity of flesh, the lavishness of the holocaust, that counted but the impression on the human personality was indicated by the least costly and most modest of all offerings listed in the Torah, the “meal-offering.” The expression employed there is not “if a human being offer” but “if a soul offer a sacrifice.”[^13] The reason for this phraseology is given in the Talmud.[^14] Who is it that cannot afford to do better than a meal-offering? Obviously the poor man. Nevertheless, since for him it is undoubtedly more of a hardship to give up the little measure of flour which he needs to satisfy his hunger than it is for the rich man to offer a bullock, “I account it unto him,” says God, “as though he had sacrificed his very soul to Me.”

In short the sacrificial ritual that obtained during the time of the existence of the Temple was, in the opinion of Maimonides, a sort of preparatory stage leading to the devotion of the heart, which was the end in view, as the sages of the Talmud observed: “The Merciful desires the heart.”[^15] It is furthermore remarked in the Mishnah[^16] that the application of the identical characterization “sweet savor” to the animal\*[^17] sacrifice as well as the meal-offering[^18] proves that it is not the amount of the sacrifice that matters but the dedication of the heart. Finally ist should be noted that the injunction of “And thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart,”[^19] which is interpreted by the Mishnah[^20] as meaning “with the two (opposite) inclinations (of man’s nature)”, is followed by that of “and with all thy soul.” That is to say that in the love of God it is the attitude of the worshipper, his relationship toward his heavenly master, his readiness to surrender his very life for Him that is aimed for and, therefore, of chief importance. This was a lesson that Judaism was successful in teaching to a large portion of humanity.

But there is also a third rung in the ladder of divine worship, which he, who pretends to be a true lover of God, must climb to prove his sincerity. He must evidence his love for his Maker by what he is ready to give up of his substance, not for publicity or the gratification of personal vanity, but because unselfish giving is a divine imperative. There are many individuals, who with their lips will profess adherence to the highest principles, the noblest ideals, but to act on these principles, to voluntarily relinquish some of the advantages they possess in order to prove that they really mean what they avow, that is another matter. They resemble the young man, who expressed his devotion to the girl he was courting in the following words “Darling, I love you, I adore you. I would go through fire and water for your sake. I’ll see you tomorrow evening if it doesn’t rain ”

There are, of course, among all races of man persons who have reached this highest pinnacle of virtue, who are dedicated body and soul to the furtherance of the wellbeing of their fellow-men. Every generation has its Moses Monte-fores, its Florence Nightingales and its Albert Schweitzers. However so far as lifting the rank and file, the masses to such an eminence is concerned, only Judaism — this may be said without braggadocio — has been successful. We Jews have learned the meaning of “with all thy substance.”[^21] Nowhere in the world is there a people that has been educated to give as we Jews have. And when a Jew doesn't respond to an appeal for help by those in distress, there is reason to entertain suspicion about the purity of his descent, for Jews, who are the offspring of Abraham, are supposed to be “merciful, the children of the merciful.”[^22] Hardness of heart is not a Jewish trait.

To sum up, then, though prayer stands above animal sacrifices, its chief value lies in its serving as a spiritual discipline, preparing the worshipper to act upon the convictions that have come to him and, by setting his record straight, achieve complete reconciliation with God. As Rabbi Amnon of Mayence expressed it so well in his popularly accepted poem for the Mussaph service of the High Holidays, “to avert the evil decree, repentance and prayer must be combined with acts of righteousness,” with tangible evidence of man’s concern with the lot of his fellow-travellers on earth.
