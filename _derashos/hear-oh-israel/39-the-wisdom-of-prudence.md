THE WISDOM OF PRUDENCE

The book of the Hagiographa, the third division of the Hebrew Scriptures, that is prescribed by tradition as part of the reading on the Sabbath of the intervening days of Sukkot is the scroll of Ecclesiastes. The author, who tells his readers how he had, as king of Jerusalem, savored everything life had to offer,[^1] reaches the gloomy conclusion that it was all worthless, “vanity of vanities”.[^2] But if that was the case, why did he take the trouble to write his book’ In the eyes of a person who denies the value of all values what advantage could there be to seeing himself in print? The only explanation possible is that the expression “vanity of vanities” that the writer uses is not to be taken literally, but is to be construed rather as an hyperbole, an exaggeration for the sake of effect. Not all things man does are useless, worthless, vain. Certain activities in which he engages are, on the contrary, very much worthwhile. Greed and ambition, the mad chase after wealth and power for their own sake are, to be sure, as he points out, as futile as the pursuit of the wind.[^3] Does it not happen ever so often that the riches, which a man has labored hard to accumulate, are passed on to unworthy hands that squander them all too quickly’[^4] And as far as honor and fame are concerned, is it not known to all that “the paths of glory lead but to the grave”? This measuring rod is, however, not to be applied to the normal pleasures of life. For they are satisfying. There is no need to apologize for eating one’s bread with joy and drinking wine with a glad heart.[^5] As for finding happiness in the company of a beloved mate, that is man’s destiny and the reward for his toil beneath the sun.[^6] Furthermore, even if it be granted that the importance of all human accomplishments has been overrated, that no man is justified in taking himself too seriously,[^7] there are differences of degree that must be noted. Thus, for example, the difference between wisdom and folly is as great as the distinction between light and darkness.[^8] A good name is more desirable than the most fragrant ointment.[^9] The day of death, when man’s mission on earth is ended and it is possible to pass judgment on the value of the sum total of his life, affords more cause for rejoicing than the day of birth, when the future is still a mystery.[^10] That is why it is more rewarding to visit a house of mourning, where the entire career of the deceased can be evaluated, than to participate in a wedding feast, that celebrates the beginning of an epoch of existence.[^11] For the completion of an task has vastly more significance than its initiation[^12] Many noble projects are begun but only comparatively few are carried to a successful conclusion, and it is only the reaching of the goal that renders the initial efforts worthwhile. A job abandoned before it is finished is like a bridge brought as far as midstream. That is to say it is as worthless as if it had not been started at all.

The most important piece of advice that Ecclesiastes has to offer to old as well as young is to look forward, to foresee the consequences of their deeds, to forestall disaster by timely precaution, to be provident by being prudent. Remember there is a price-tag on whatever you do. “If you make a promise, you are expected to fulfill st.”[^13] If you follow your impulses, doing what comes naturally, pursuing the path of least resistance regardless of what may be involved, “you will eventually be brought to judgment.”[^14] No one escapes retribution forever.

These are the truths that Ecclesiastes had an opportunity to see confirmed. Because he had reached a ripe old age he was able to bear witness to their validity. He felt it gave him the prerogative — nay it imposed upon him the obligation — to advise those younger than himself. And although from the tone with which he sets out he sounds like a cynic, who ridicules everything in human life, lumping together man’s virtues with his vices, his concluding statement corrects whatever negative opinion one may have formed of his views. “In sum,” he says, “having heard everything, fear God and keep His commandments, for that is man’s whole duty.”[^15] There is a law of compensation that operates in the world. Whatever man does is appraised by the Supreme Judge, even everything hidden, whether it be good or evil. As Akabiah the son of Mahalalel expressed it, although man’s origin is humble and his destiny is decomposition and decay, yet must he give an accounting of his life before the king of kings, the Holy One Blessed Be He.[^16] It, therefore, behooves man to see to it that his attire is always spotless and his head is well anointed with ol”[^17] for he never knows when the day of reckoning may come on which he would have to answer for himself.
