THE TRAGEDY OF DEATH

With the end of Genesis, the book of “Beginnings,” the story of the lives of the Hebrew Patriarchs reaches its conclusion. The termination of man’s existence on earth is death. It is “the way of all the earth,” as David expressed it to his son Solomon.[^1] The first of men recognized, once he had eaten the forbidden fruit of the tree of knowledge, that he was but dust and bound, when his time came, to return to the dust.[^2]

In the mind of the average person death figures as the greatest of all misfortunes. No human being in his right senses is really ever content to die. “I shall not die but live,”[^3] exclaimed the psalmist. And it was undoubtedly this feeling about death that caused the coiners of the titles of the divisions of the Torah that refer to the two sections that speak of the decease of the matriarch Sarah and the patriarch Jacob as “the life of Sarah”[^4] and “Jacob lived”[^5] rather than “the death of Sarah” and “Jacob died.” Death is, then, not a prospect to which the normal individual, who is not weary of life, looks forward with any degree of complacency, notwithstanding the assurance that “the death of His saintly ones is precious in the sight of God”,[^6] and even though, as the Midrash explains, the statement made at the end of the first chapter of the book of Genesis: “And God saw all that He made and behold it was very good”[^7] was intended to include death. The reason why even those, who might be expected to view death with, at least, equanimity, are so anxious to push the day of their demise off as far as possible is not that it is easier to live than to die, but because death spells the cessation of all activities and what follows is a complete blank. Yet since it is the inevitable fate of all who are born into this world to die,[^8] death has to be taken philosophically and due preparations have to be made for the eventuality.

“Why should we weep that man must die?” asks the poet.

> “The noblest songster of the gale
> Must cease when Winter’s frowns appear;
> The reddest rose is wan and pale,
> When Autumn tints the changing year.”

However it is not so much on account of its inevitability that man should accept, if not cheerfully, then, at least, with serenity the fact of his physical mortality, as because of the conviction, that was shared by Plato and other outstanding thinkers, that his soul is immortal.

> “Why should we weep for those who die?
> “They die to live — they sink to rise;
> They leave this wretched mortal shore;
> But brighter suns and bluer skies
> Shall smile on them for evermore...
> “The soul, the eternal soul, must reign
> In worlds devoid of pain and strife,
> Then why should mortal man complain
> Of death, which leads to happier life?”

It should be borne in mind furthermore that while the life-span of the individual is of limited duration, the race does not have to disappear. Man’s offspring, that are an extension of him, take the place left vacant by their progenitor. “Instead of thy fathers shall be thy children.”[^9] So long as the latter are alive and they continue the traditions of their forbears, those from whom they are sprung may also be accounted as living.[^10] That was why, so long as he believed his beloved son Joseph to have been torn to pieces by a wild beast, the patriarch Jacob grieved and refused to be comforted. Once, however he learned that “Joseph was still alive,”[^11] he felt that his life’s mission was fulfilled. “It is sufficient,” he exclaimed. “Now I can die.”[^12] And why was he content to give up the ghost once he learned that Joseph was alive? Because, says the Midrash, he was thereby assured of dying only once, in this world, and not twice,[^13] like him who leaves no offspring to continue his work after he is gone. It becomes less painful to heed the summons of the Master of Fate when one can feel convinced that the time he spent on earth was not a total waste because he has been provided with a successor and heir.

There exists, then, a mandate upon man to utilize to the utmost the brief allowance of time, of indefinite duration, that is placed at his disposal, in anticipation of what is certain to follow. Every human being is duty-bound, while he is able, to set his house in order and charge his children, as our forefather Jacob did before he closed his eyes forever. He may not be any more successful than Jacob was in piercing the veil of the future and revealing to posterity what is to transpire “at the end of days.”[^14] He can, however, by giving them the benefit of his experience, guide and direct them, warn them against the pitfalls of life and thus enable them to avoid repeating the mistakes he himself may have made. The last will and testament of the dying are a sacred trust. “There is a moral duty incumbent upon the heirs to fulfill the wishes of the maker of the bequest.?[^15] Scrupulous adherence to the provisions of a will grants to the departed a measure of immortality while confirming the executors in their position of trust. “The Lord swore unto David... If thy children will keep My covenant, and my testimonies that I shall teach them, their children also shall sit upon thy throne for evermore.”[^16]

This is the lesson which the contemplation of the ever-recurring phenomenon of the end of man’s existence on earth ought to teach. It was in this respect that he, who had the reputation of being the wisest of men, considered a visit to a house of mourning so very much worth while[^17] and on this account, too, that the day of man’s death was preferred by him to that of his birth.[^18]
