THE TORAH AS A BASIS OF JEWISH UNITY

A little over a millennium ago the spiritual head of the largest Jewish community in the diaspora and the foremost Jewish thinker, Bible exegete and Talmudist of his time, Saadiah ben Joseph, wrote in his “Book of Beliefs” that Israel was a nation only by virtue of sts Torah.[^1] This opinion, expressed as a simple matter of fact by the celebrated Gaon of Sura, is today challenged in many Jewish quarters. Whereas at one time the authority of the Torah was supreme in Jewish life and the scrolls of the Law in the Holy Ark constituted the Holy of Holies of every synagogue, there exists in this twentieth century of ours the strange apparition of a Judaism without Torah and of Jewish houses of worship employed in the main for purposes other than instruction and prayer. Ask some of our Jewish educators, who occupy places of prominence in the Jewish national revival movement, what they understand by Jewish culture, and the answer you will get will be that by that is meant Jewish art, music, poetry — everything except religion. Visit certain of the gorgeous edifices, dedicated by Jews to the name of God, on the occasion of a social function or of an address by a well-known political figure — and he does not necessarily have to be of the Mosaic persuasion — and you will find the auditorium filled to capacity, But, oh, if you should chance to drop in at a time, when just an ordinary Sabbath service without any celebration, such as a Bar-Mitzvah, happens to take place, you would be able to count many an empty pew.

What is the cause of this profanation of Jewish culture and of the secularization of the synagogue? Is it that our generation is less innately devout than its predecessors, that there is no more need for that deep abiding faith, that once characterized the Jew, in a force other than the blind elements of nature and the base lusts and evil machinations of human beings, a power that makes for justice and righteousness in the world? That could hardly be, for if even only a part of what our chaplains report to us about the behavior of our fighting men in the foxholes is true, and we believe the tales of unparalleled fortitude exhibited by our brethren in Europe under the severest trials that have ever tested the characters of human beings and their ideals, we would have to admit that the religious urge is still pretty strong. What, then, could have happened? Have ritual and ceremony, perhaps, become obsolete and lost their attraction? Then how shall one explain the fascination exercised by the complicated formalities of fraternal orders upon many Jews who are no longer bound by the Jewish religious traditions?

The reason for the change in attitude with regard to the place of the Torah in Jewish life on the part of a large number of modern Jews, who have by no means severed their connections with the Jewish community, but, are, on the contrary, otherwise loyal sons of their people, must therefore be sought elsewhere. It will be found to be due largely to the theory, that has gained currency in the civilized society of our day, that religion is a purely private affair, a matter for the individual only, a relationship between man and his Maker (den adam lammakom).[^2] That being the case, religion can have no place of importance in a national culture, which consists of ideas and attitudes and customs and manners characteristic of the entire people. In fact to speak of a Jewish religion would, under such conditions, be a misnomer. There is no such thing. There is only a religion of Moses, of Isaiah, of Ezekiel, of Rabbi Akiba, of Maimonides and Mendelssohn and Mordecai Kaplan. Also if this conception of the nature of religion is correct, there is no need for regular periods of prayer, for weekly, aye daily, divine services. Men would have their devotions when they felt like it, when their spirit would move them, not otherwise.

The idea that a person’s religious belief is a matter of his private conscience, with which no outside force must be permitted to interfere is, of course, one of the fundamental principles of democracy, which regards freedom of worship as an inalienable right of every human being. To question its validity would be to cast doubt on the basic thesis of the democratic philosophy of life. On the other hand, it is equally certain that the Torah, that was given to Israel at Sinai and which served throughout the ages as the cornerstone of Jewish religious life, offered no such latitudes to its adherents. It consisted of a rigid code of commandments and prohibitions, defining exactly what its devotees were expected to do and what they were bidden to avoid. It constituted the direct antithesis, in other words, of the idea that religion is a private affair, a matter of the individual conscience, for legislation is unnecessary where the conscience is the sole and final umpire. Does this mean, therefore, that there is no room for Israel’s Torah in a democratic milieu, that religious liberty and the authority of the Law are mutually exclusive concepts, that could not be upheld at one and the same time, that either the one or the other must abdicate because “two sovereigns cannot make use of one crown?”[^3]

It would seem at first blush as though there were no escape from this dilemma. One must either accept in its entirety, as Jewish Reformists have done, the principle of freedom of conscience, and thereby automatically repudiate the binding power of the Mosaic legislation, or one must reject it. No third choice is possible. Further investigation, however, reveals that this conclusion is not at all necessary. Its acceptance as an inevitability is due to confused thinking on the subject, which a study of the history of the concept of freedom of conscience alone is able to clear up.

The author of this idea, as is well-known, was none other than the great Marrano philosopher, who lived in Holland approximately three hundred years ago, Benedict Spinoza. Spinoza, it will be recalled, was a victim of religious intolerance. Although he never formally apostasized — there is no evidence of his having undergone baptism — he was stamped as an heretic by his fellow-Jews in Amsterdam and publicly ostracized from the Jewish community of that city. It is interesting to note, however, that it was not on account of his unorthodox views that he was read out of the fold. The rabbis who pronounced the interdict against him were not concerned about his inner private thoughts. What worried them was his outward behavior, his conduct in public, the fact that he never put in an appearance in the synagogue, that he did not observe the Jewish festivals, that he violated the dietary laws, that he had cut himself loose from Klal Israel.

But was not this a bit of hypocrisy on the part of the Amsterdam rabbis? Not at all. It was entirely consistent with traditional Jewish policy. Intellectually there had never existed anything approaching unanimity among Jews, and rightly so, because every person’s thoughts are his own, depending in their nature on his education and his mental equipment. What man thinks in his mind is not subject to external control.[^4] Ideologically contemporaries like Moses Maimonides of Fostat and his critic, Abraham ben David of Posquieres, or to use another example, Abraham ibn Ezra of Spain and Rabbenu Tam of northern France, lived worlds apart. Yet all of them were recognized by posterity, at least, as the official spokesmen of Judaism and the authoritative interpreters of its traditions. Why? Because however much they may have differed from one another in theory — and the differences between them were considerable — in practice there was little to distinguish between them. The philosopher as well as the legalist, the grammarian like the Tosafist, all obeyed the same basic law. And that was the way it had been since the very beginning, since the birth of the Jewish religion. The Agada[^5] notes that whereas in describing the encampments and the wanderings of Israel’s ancestors in the wilderness Scripture almost invariably employs the plural form wayis‘u wayachanu,[^6] “and they departed and they camped” — because the Israelites were, as their descendants still are today, a people of individualists, always disagreeing and quarreling because each had its own ideas[^7] — in connection with the giving of the Torah at Sinai the singular is used: wayichan Yisrael, “and Israel encamped”,[^8] i.e. as one man. Why? Because the Torah was the instrument that united all Jews. It was the bond of unity among them, and it was destined to function in this capacity for a long time to come, and, by virtue of playing this part among them, to keep the house of Israel from disintegrating and falling apart. For evil days came upon the Jewish people. After having lived in the Promised Land, except for a brief interruption of seventy years, for more than twelve centuries, Jews were scattered and dispersed all over the world, so that there was hardly a country on earth that did not have its contingent of them. What force held these fragments of a once sovereign and more or less united nation, that had now neither government nor army nor a territory of its own, together? What made them one, so that no matter what the land of his origin may have been, whenever a Jew came to a Jewish community, he felt welcome, at home? The answer is “the Torah”, the fact that all Jews abided by the same code of laws, said the same prayers, followed the same rites and practices. This unity Spinoza had by his nonconformism threatened to destroy. His toleration within the Jewish community of Amsterdam would have been a disrupting influence. That is why it was felt necessary in his case to adopt the drastic measures that were used to oust him.

In the eyes of his modern sympathizers Spinoza has become a martyr and a hero, because he wanted to be honest with himself, to live in accordance with his convictions, whilst his persecutors are condemned as scoundrels and bigots and hypocrites because they wanted him, the great thinker, the protagonist of freedom of thought, to follow their ancient, rigid, superannuated code and, by so doing, live what, considering his personal views, was a lie. But if one were to analyze the situation in the light of the aforementioned circumstances, would one conclude that they were really so guilty? Is it not of the very nature of law to make those who are subject to it do what is often contrary to their personal wishes and likes? How could any group function smoothly, how could those who are by the force of circumstances compelled to live together ever get along if “every man did what was right in his own eyes,”[^9] if on account of the lack of definite rules of demeanor under given conditions each followed his own impulses without considering his neighbor’s interests? Would it redound to public welfare, for example, to have different speed limits for different motorists depending on their intelligence? Would not traffic jams and accidents be the result if traffic lights were not heeded by bus drivers and college professors alike? Mind you, the men who put Spinoza in the ban did not ask him to give up his philosophical speculations. All they demanded of him was not to deviate from the time-honored practices of the Jewish people, which for the sake of Israel’s unity all Jews had to obey.

The mistake Spinoza made — and this is the source of confusion in this matter — was that he failed to distinguish, as Judaism had always done, between thought and action, between belief and practice, that he labored under the illusion that the freedom of the one implied necessarily the freedom of the other. This identification in his mind of freedom of action with freedom of thought may have been due to the influence upon him of Christian theology, the reaction of years of living under the banner of Catholicism on the part of his Marrano parents. For in Christianity it was always belief that was primary, of chief importance. What was stressed was not practical mizvot but knowledge of the catechism, not the execution of commandments but the acceptance of certain dogmas about God and the Son of God. The constraint of conscience was, therefore, one of the foremost tasks of the organized church. For the sake of its very survival it was essential for it to keep its affiliates from entertaining views that it considered heterodox. Here, then, Spinoza pointed out — and rightly so — that one cannot control a person’s thoughts, because there is no way of telling what goes on in his mind, wherefore freedom of thought was a natural human right. If this is true, if it is admitted, as it must be, that a person’s thoughts are his own, and religion is identical with belief or chiefly a matter of belief as it is in Christianity, then, religion, too, must be a strictly private affair with which society or the community has no right to interfere.

All this, however, does not apply to Judaism by which it is not so much what a person believes as what he does that is considered important. In this field, in the sphere of action the freedom that might be allowed in the domain of thought cannot be granted, for unless a person follows certain rules, he is bound to get into conflict with his fellowmen.

That a distinction must be drawn between freedom of thought and freedom of action, that variation in the one case may be fully compatible with uniformity in the other seems to have been instinctively felt by the Jewish rank and file in the past. There is the familiar story told about two ghetto freethinkers, who debated the question of the existence of God and arrived at the conclusion that there was no such being. In celebration of their success in their battle against traditional belief one of them proposed a toast. He was just about to lift the glass of water to his lips without pronouncing the benediction prescribed by rabbinic law, when his comrade stopped him, saying: “Nu, and what about a Broche?” “J don’t understand you,” replied the other. “Haven’t we just decided that God doesn’t exist??? “What does that have to do with it?” came the rejoinder. “Whether there is a God or there isn’t, ohn a Broche trinkt a Goy”. In other words whoever does not comply with the Jewish practice of uttering a blessing before partaking of food puts himself outside of the Jewish fold.

The great mistake, then, that is made by many otherwise loyal Jews of our time, who repudiate the authority of the Torah in Jewish life on the ground that religion is a private affair, a matter of the individual conscience, the freedom of which dare not be restricted, is that they confuse action with thought, and consequently would accord to the former, which must be regulated to prevent clash and conflict, the same treatment as the latter, which is not subject to control. Herein lies the chief weakness of Judaism in our day. The confusion and chaos reigning in Jewish life in our time is due in the main to the breakdown of authority, to the fact that so many Jews make Shabbos for themselves, that instead of one Torah there are many Torot, that in place of one Shulchan Arukh there are any number of widely divergent codes of behavior, bearing out the complaint of the prophet “For according to the number of thy cities are thy gods, o Judah.”[^10]

Such a condition is not conducive to Jewish survival. If Jewry is not to disintegrate it must have a united front. This sort of unity is, by the very nature of things, impossible in the intellectual or the ideological field. It is feasible only in the realm of action. The salvation of Israel lies then in the reestablishment of the authority of the Law. When God revealed himself at Sinai, observes the Midrash, every Israelite had an experience of divinity in accordance with his capacity[^11] However when rules were laid down to guide Jewish conduct no distinctions were made between one Jew and another. “As for the congregation, there shall be one statute for both you and for the stranger.”[^12] This must be Israel’s standard today. The Torah alone can serve as the basis of Jewish unity.
