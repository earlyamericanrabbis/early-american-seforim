“AND YE SHALL PROCLAIM LIBERTY”

The Seder ceremony ushers in the oldest Jewish holiday, a festival going back to the very beginning of Israel’s history as a people. The antiquity of Passover is attested by the fact that it was already observed by our ancestors at a time when they were still slaves unto Pharaoh in Egypt.[^1] What is striking about its celebration to this very day is the deliberate attempt that is made to preserve its archaic character, the quaintness of its ritual and the aura of the ancient that hovers about the customs connected with it. “In every generation”, so one of the teachers of the Mishnah is quoted as saying in the Hagadah, “a Jew should look upon himself as though he had personally participated in the exodus from Egypt.”[^2] And in order to get this feeling we, who are removed from those who actually took part in that historic migration by more than three-thousand years, eat the bread that these forbears of ours took along as provision for their journey.[^3] We also sample bitter herbs to remind us of the bitterness of their servitude[^4] and dip these herbs in a mixture resembling the mortar with which they were forced to work.[^5] Finally, although the age of animal sacrifices is long past, a symbolic memento of the Passover offering is laid on our tables in the form of the shank-bone of a lamb.[^6] All this is done in literal fulfillment of the injunction: “That thou mayest remember the day of thy going forth from Egypt all the days of thy life.”[^7]

Prior to our age of speed and rapid changes there was nothing unusual about such complete absorption in and preoccupation with the past. For those who regarded themselves as spiritual dwarfs in comparison with their predecessors, who said: “If the ancients were angels, we are just human beings, if they were human beings we are as asses”,[^8] it was but natural to look with admiration upon the giants of antiquity and conceive of education as consisting almost entirely of a mastery of the wisdom of the ancients and a memorization of the legacy they left. In days when technological progress was very slow and discoveries in the various fields of knowledge were a rarity, all that was necessary to keep up with the times intellectually and take one’s rightful place in the society of which one was a part was to familiarize oneself with one’s cultural heritage, with the accumulated intellectual treasures of the ages. The ideal sage of such an era was one like Rabbi Eliezer the son of Hyrkanos, who was said to have resembled “a sealed cistern that never lost a drop.”[^9]

That period is, however, over and done with. The age we live in is one of swift locomotion, of rapidly changing conditions, of continual shifts of population, of political, economic and social developments proceeding at a rate and tempo unparalleled in the annals of man. Rarely does it happen nowadays that children should reside in the house that was inhabited by their parents, still less that they wear their elders’ clothes or read the latter’s favorite books. In such an age more is required in order to get along in the world than just a retentive memory. The traits that educators must seek to make their pupils cultivate are such qualities as critical judgment, so as to be in a position to evaluate adequately each situation as it presents itself, and not accept blindly whatever is handed down. What is needed is alertness of mind for making the quick decisions that the rapidly changing circumstances may demand. More important than being weighted down by the load of tradition is it to possess inventiveness and creativity so as to meet the ever new contingencies that arise. In short only he can be successful in our twentieth century environment who is, like Rabbi Elazar the son of Arak, “a perpetually gushing fountain,”[^10] constantly bubbling over with fresh water.

If this analysis is correct, then our attempt on Passover to resurrect from the limbo of the past events that took place more than three-thousand years ago would appear to be an utter waste of time, an expenditure of energy that is pointless, futile, serving no useful purpose. However the science of biology teaches the direct opposite. It is the genes that determine the character of the individual. Prenatal influences are more important and childhood experiences make a much deeper impression on a person than the latest happenings in his adult life. “Forty days before the formation of the embryo of a child,” say our sages,[^11] “a heavenly voice proclaims: ‘The daughter of so and so shall marry so and so’.” Similarly the destiny of a people is marked out at the beginning of its history. To explain the behavior of a given ethnic group one must look into its past. Only then will its character be properly understood. This thesis has been most eloquently defended and most convincingly stated by a very brilliant interpreter of Judaism of our generation. He shows, by a quick review and analysis of Jewish history, how Jews came to acquire the characteristics that distinguish them. He indicates why, for example, they are on the whole more inclined toward philanthrophy than their non-Jewish neighbors, what is responsible for their intellectualism and other such traits.

And if it be asked: “But what is the mission of the Jew, his raison d’etre, the purpose for which he was sent into the world?”, the answer, so we would reply, has long ago been spelled out in the Torah. It is summed up in the command: “And ye shall proclaim freedom on the earth unto all the inhabitants thereof.”[^12] Strictly speaking, of course, this injunction was issued in connection with the emancipation of the Hebrew slaves at the beginning of the jubilee year, and it applied only to the limited territory or portion of the earth over which Jews had jurisdiction.[^13] However in view of the role that Jews have played in the liberation of mankind in general the broader interpretation is equally valid. By the middle of the 19th century the identification of the Jew with the longing for freedom was so complete that when Adolph Cremieux, the leader of French Jewry, founder of the Alliance Israelite and champion of Jewish rights, was asked by someone what his political affiliation and philosophy was, he replied laconically, as though it were something self-evident: “Juif, puis liberal.” That is to say: “Since I am a Jew, where else would I fit in but the Liberal Party?”

It was the distinctive nature of his history that qualified the Jew to become the champion of human liberty. Had Jews been a people that was always subjected to regimentation and enslavement, to being ruled by others — as the Russians still are today, for what difference does it really make whether one is a puppet in the hands of the Czar or of the Politburo? — they would have been unfit for the task. The liberation of slaves is never carried out by the slaves themselves. They are so accustomed to their misery that they are reconciled with their lot and do not care to exert themselves in order to extricate themselves from it. Had Jews, on the other hand been a nation that had never known what it meant to be enslaved, a nation habituated to ruling and dominating others, they would not have been qualified for their mission either. The sated has no idea of what it means to be hungry. One reason for the indifference of the civilized, democratic world toward the persecution of the Jew, twenty years ago by Hitler and now by Nasser of Egypt and Kuwatly of Syria, is that in the progressive, liberal West such behavior was as inconceivable as it was unbelievable. Only he who has himself once been a slave and has, by tremendous effort, won his freedom can appreciate fully the value of freedom. Only Israelites, who remembered that “they were once slaves unto Pharaoh in Egypt but that they had been delivered from their bondage by God”,[^14] could be appealed to to be kind, on the ground of that recollection, to the underprivileged, to the stranger and the orphan and the widow.[^15]

The lesson was one that the Jew never forgot. His position in Czarist Russia may materially not have been very much different from that of his ancestors in Egypt. Inwardly, however, he felt free, freer than his assimilated brethren in the relatively more enlightened and liberal West. He felt freer because he had not, as many of them had, sold his birthright as a Jew for a pottage of doubtful political equality. Tes status was, to borrow a phrase from Achad Haam, of “slavery in freedom”,[^16] while Ass was one of “freedom amidst slavery.”

Because this mission is not completed so long as reaction raises its ugly head — and the danger that it may is ever present —- it is necessary for those who have been selected by destiny as the guardians of human liberty “to look upon themselves in every generation as though they had personally participated in the exodus from Egypt.”[^17] Sometimes, however, they get tired of this assignment and are inclined to shake off their burden. There is a story told of a rabbi in Poland, who gave his pupil, who was departing for the completion of his education to the great Yeshibah of Amsterdam, an amulet for protection on the road against the machinations of Satan, who is constantly lying in wait for young men like him to wean them away from the traditions of their fathers. Armed with this talisman the youth passed many a danger. One day he was fascinated by music that seemed to be coming from the woods. So he dropped his amulet and followed the sweet tones till he stood before the Palace of the Magic Prince, from which they emanated. He was about to enter, when a mighty hand pulled him back. “J am your father. Do not enter. Go not after the music. The melody belongs to an alien God. Go to Amsterdam, my son and study Torah.” The meaning of the parable is clear. In the dim dawn of his history the Jew was given a mission. The sirens of the non-Jewish world are constantly trying to entice him, to draw him away from his divine calling. He follows them and little by little drops the attributes that made him outstanding. But there is an inner voice that calls him back, that says to him: “You are a Jew. You have a unique task to fulfill. Your job is not yet completed. Go ahead and do as you have been commanded. Remember that ‘in every generation a Jew must look upon himself as though he had himself participated in the exodus from Egypt.’ ”
