THE FACTOR OF GEOGRAPHY

The crucial event in the annals of the Jewish people, which is commemorated by the seventh day of Passover calls attention to the important role played by geography in the history and development of nations. That climatic conditions and the nature of the terrain they inhabit exercise a profound influence not only on the physical features of men but on their characters and their very mode of thinking as well is a point made by many historians of our time. Thus, for example, it has been noted how the rugged country, that was their home, compelled the Athenians to use their wits in order to eke out a livelihood. It was in this way that they developed the marvellous civilization, which was their claim to fame, while their neighbors, who inhabited the plains of Boeotia, which adjoined Attica, on account of the fertility of the land they possessed, never advanced beyond the stage of primitive peasants. If the accident of geography could account for so marked a difference in the cultural evolution of members of the same race dwelling as close to one another as did the Athenians and Boeotians of Greece, it must certainly have had a great deal to do with shaping the course of the civilization brought forth by the people called Israel. This thesis is supported by the Bible itself. It was their crossing of the Red Sea, toward which they had been led by Moses and which, after their safe passage through it, receded upon their former masters, so we read in the Torah, that convinced the erstwhile slaves of the Egyptians of the existence of a God who espouses the cause of the downtrodden. “Now when Israel saw the great feat which the Lord had wrought over the Egyptians, then the people feared the Lord and they believed in the Lord and in Moses, His servant.”[^1]

The unusual experience, which by the assertion of our sages was never repeated,[^2] was followed by another just as remarkable and unique, namely the revelation at Sinai. The rabbis[^3] had a great deal to say on the subject of why the Torah, which remained the foundation of Jewish life and thought for several millennia, was and had to be given in the wilderness, why it could not have been issued rather in the land which was to be Israel’s home. Whether the explanations they offer, interesting and illuminating though they be, are correct or not, this much is certain, namely that in the loneliness of the desert, where God had the people He had liberated all to Himself, there was a much better opportunity for the development of that distinctive way of living that characterized Judaism and for the cultivation of the till then unheard of belief in one supreme ruler of the universe, the creator of all things, than in crowded Canaan with its many idolatrous cults and its traditions of polytheism. Also the Israelites would never have acquired, in the environment of luxury and ease of the Canaanite centers of civilization, the hardihood they needed for the conquest of the Promised Land and their survival, later on, of the many vicissitudes of fortune that came upon their descendants. It took all the hardships, with which existence in the waterless waste was fraught, to toughen them for the task before them. Thus what might at first have seemed a punishment meted out upon them by God for their rebelliousness[^4] turned out to be in the long run a great boon. Not to the cowardly, cringing slaves, that had just been released from Egyptian bondage, but to the generation, that had been reared in the austerity and freedom of the desert and that was, therefore, prepared for it psychologically as well as physically, could there be entrusted the difficult undertaking of the recovery by Israel of the heritage of the patriarchs.

And geography continued to affect the Jew’s behavior and his philosophy of life. It did this throughout the years in which he was settled in his home country, as well as during the centuries in which he was compelled to wander about restlessly from nation to nation and from one kingdom to another. Israel’s location on the crossroads of empires endowed the seers of the Jewish people with a world-outlook just as it was the cause of the little Judean state’s serving so often as the football of rival powers. Then, when Jewish national independence came to an end and Jews were scattered to the four corners of the earth, their dispersion forced them to seek out occupations that would allow them to be mobile, liquid, that would not tie them down to a particular locality. In consequence thereof, instead of being tillers of the soil, as they had been when they were settled in their home country, they became merchants and professionals and possessors of skills that could be useful anywhere.

Yes, geography can throw a great deal of light on the psychic and social phenomena that manifest themselves in the world. It is capable of explaining many a why and how in the intellectual and moral development of individuals or national groups. It can lay bare the economic causes that made the British and the Dutch seafaring, colonizing nations and that pushed the Russians toward communism, the Arabs toward feudalism and the republics of South America toward their types of dictatorship. It is necessary, however, to beware against making excessive use of this scalpel and pushing the theory too far, because it cannot account for everything. If it did, then why did not the Arabs, who lived in Palestine a much longer time than the Jews did all told and who, before entering the Holy Land, had a training period of not 40 but perhaps as much as 4,000 years of desert life, develop Israel’s characteristics? Why did not the Moabites, Ammonites and Edomites, who were acknowledged by the ancient Israelites as their kinsmen and spoke a vernacular that was practically identical with theirs, why did not they produce prophets as the descendants of Jacob did? How comes it that with a civilization and culture, which, for all that is known to us, was the same as that of their Israelite neighbors, these peoples remained polytheists at a time when Israel proclaimed the great doctrine of monotheism? It may be claimed that it was just an accident of history that Israel’s religious evolution turned out as it did. Just as certain slight variations of constitution account for the survival of some genera of given species of animals and plants, so the combination of experiences that was peculiar to the Israelite branch of the Hebrew family tree was responsible for the particular course of development followed by its beliefs and practices. Jewish theology, however, prefers to call this seemingly fortuitous conjuncture or coalescence of factors an act of God. Even those who deny that deity had ever spoken to the Israelites in the wilderness of Sinai, are forced to admit that the composition of the Bible and the creation by Jews of Judaism are historic facts This unique contribution, Jewish tradition asserts, did not just happen. Israel was chosen for the role. This does not mean that other peoples did not have their distinctive missions to fulfill. Undoubtedly the Egyptians, Babylonians, Persians, Greeks and Romans, each had their specific functions marked out for them. So far as the proclamation of the religious and moral truths contained in the Hebrew Scriptures were concerned, however, Israel had been specially selected therefor by the Master of Fate. “Only you (said God) have I known,” so Amos testified in corroboration of this fact, “of all the families of the earth.”[^5]

To say that this was unfair, that teachings of such importance as those embraced in Israel’s Torah should have been divulged from the beginning to the whole of mankind, is, as Judah Halevi points out in his book Kusari,[^6] like asking why of all animals man alone was endowed with intelligence, why the horse and the ox and other living things, that are just as much God’s creatures as the human species, were deprived of the gift of reason.

In short Judaism insists that there must have been a divine purpose behind what befell Israel in the course of its four-thousand-year-old career. It is, of course, only in retrospect that this purpose becomes clear, that the design is recognizable, for when viewed at close range the individual events often do not make sense. One wonders, when one notes the anguish and the pain that have been the portion of Jewry for centuries, why Israel was not vouchsafed the security enjoyed by its tormentors. Why was it not granted to Jews to be free from anxiety and care, to live and laugh as their neighbors did? Why must the way of a Jewish state, that wants for nothing more than the right to exist, to this day be fraught with difficulties unknown to any other? Why are nations, much less deserving than Israel, treated with consideration while it is looked upon as expendable?

It is first when Jewish history is surveyed in its entirety, that it is realized that Israel’s via dolorosa was intended as a discipline, that just as of the ordeal of Jewry’s ancestors in the wilderness it may be asserted of the subsequent trials of their descendants: “For as a man chastises his son so does the Lord thy God chastise thee?[^7] Unquestionably our lot has been a hard one. See how much easier the gentiles have it! They pick out one person — and even he is a Jew — and have him crucified so that he might atone for all their sins, while of us millions have to be sacrificed to make up for the errors of humanity. But this is apparently the price that Jewry has to pay for its longevity. For we see that other nations, much more powerful and numerous and better favored by fortune are no more, while we are still here to tell the tale. The psalmist was right when he said of Israel: “The Lord has sorely chastised me but He has not delivered me up unto death.”[^8] We are still here because we were steeled for weathering crisis whenever it presented itself, which others were not. We were made to pass through the crucible of affliction so that we might be wiser and better than those who came before us. This is how a great philosopher-poet long ago summed up the philosophy of Jewish history:

> “Give ear, O my people, to my teaching, incline
> your ears to the words of my mouth.
> I will open my mouth with a parable, I will utter
> dark sayings concerning days of old.
> That which we have heard and known, and our
> fathers have told us...
> That the generation to come might know them,
> even the children that should be born, who
> should arse and tell them to their children,
> That they might put their confidence in God, and
> not forget the works of God, but keep His com-
> mandments;
> And might not be as their fathers, a stubborn and
> rebellious generation, a generation that set not
> their heart aright and whose spirit was not stead-
> fast with God.”[^9]

This may be but a rehashing of “old stuff” to use a colloquialism. Yet it is necessary to repeat this wisdom of our ancients, because it is only, when we give such an interpretation to what has happened and is still happening to Jewry, that its situation becomes bearable and that the desire is there to go on living as Jews. Our suffering has not been in vain. “History,” remarked Heinrich Heine, “can testify that Israel, like her ancestor in days of old, has striven with gods and with men[^10] and has prevailed.”
