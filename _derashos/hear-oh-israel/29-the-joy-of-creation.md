THE JOY OF CREATION

The Jewish New Year’s festival, which falls on the first of Tishri, is, according to Jewish tradition the anniversary of the birth of the world. How the universe, in which our earth is, as the astronomers have discovered, only an infinitesimal speck, came into being, on this point science is unable to enlighten us, for it cannot go outside of nature to peer beyond that which is. And so we are forced to apply to another source of illumination — Scripture. Scripture informs us that God created the world out of nothing, and the singular form of the verb meaning “creation”, that is employed in the sacred text, indicates that God did it all by Himself, that there was no one to assist the heavenly architect as He fashioned the cosmos out of the void. Scripture teaches also that man was the crown of God’s creation, the noblest of all His creatures, and that what distinguished him from the rest, what rendered him superior and outstanding was the fact that he was created in God’s image[^1] and formed in His likeness.

What exactly do these two descriptions of man’s nature mean? In what respect actually does man resemble his Maker? The answer that comes to mind at once is that it is in his intellect that man comes closest to the divine, in his ability to think and plan and not just follow blind instinct in the manner of the lower forms of life. That is no doubt the implication of the statement “A lamp of the Lord is the soul of man.”[^2] But there are also other points of similarity. Man, once he has reached maturity, is supposed to be able to distinguish between good and evil and to possess the freedom to choose between them. This, according to the observation of the serpent in the Garden of Eden, was a mark of divinity. “God knoweth,” he said to Eve, “that on the day on which you will eat of the (fruit of the tree of knowledge) your eyes will be opened and you will be like God, knowing good and evil.”[^3] However man’s closest approach to the status of his Maker lies in his productivity. It is when he assumes the role of “partner of the Holy One Blessed Be He in the work of creation” that he is most godlike.

> That man was intended for just such a mission is

attested by Scripture itself. When the Lord God first made heaven and earth, so we read in the second chapter of the book of Genesis, the fields were bare of shrubs and the grass had not yet grown.[^4] Why? Not only because “rain had not yet descended upon the earth”, but also because “there was as yet no human being to til the soil.”[^5] Then God created man and he completed what the divine craftsman had left unfinished.

The part played by man in building up God’s world must not be underestimated. There is the story that is told about a village preacher, who, admiring the beautiful farm, into which what was once an old abandoned homestead had been transformed, remarked to its owner: “My, what a wonderful thing you and God have done here!” “Yes,” agreed the farmer. “It is lovely. But you should have seen what this place looked like when God had it all to Himself.”

In order to realize the extent of the contribution of human ingenuity it is necessary to travel and see the world. One obtains an inkling thereof when one views the works of art to be found in such centers of culture as France and Italy and Spain. But it becomes most evident in visits paid in our time to a country like Israel, which is presently in the throes of creation, literally changing its face not only from year to year but from month to month The metamorphosis that has taken place in the landscape of the Jewish national home, during the ten short years that have elapsed since the declaration of Israel’s independence alone, seems like the fulfillment of the prophecy that “when the Lord would comfort Zion and her ruins, He would turn her wilderness into an Eden and her steppe into a garden of the Lord.”[^6] What is to be remembered, however, is that the agents employed by the divine comforter in the realization of this ancient vision are human beings, the Jewish builders of modern Israel.

To say that all is well with Israel would be a gross misrepresentation of the truth. The Jewish state’s current political problems are too well known to warrant elaboration. Also its economic difficulties are far from having been solved. Although there is presently an abundance of food in the country and the housing shortage is not nearly as acute as it was during the earlier years of record-breaking immigration, self-sufficiency is a long way off. The gap between imports and exports has been narrowed, thanks to increased production made possible by the machinery brought in from Germany on account of reparations payments and the influx of investment capital provided by Israel bonds sold in America. But a balance of trade still remains to be achieved. It isn’t the current realities that fill one with optimism regarding the future of the young republic. The sight of shops stocked with merchandise, of comfortable homes and well-kept lawns may be but an illusion of prosperity, a prosperity that may hold up as long as the flow of dollars from America and of goods from Germany will continue. Israel would have been better off if the living standards of its population were not so high, if its citizens had not been so eager as they are to enjoy the comforts of their brethren in America, if there had been fewer luxury hotels and not so many new air-conditioned office buildings. What inspires confidence in Israel’s destiny is what non-Jewish observers call “the dynamism, the creative urge” of its people, the remarkable progress the nation has made during the brief period of its existence, the desire to go forward whatever the cost. The British, with the resources of an empire at their disposal, did not dare to embark upon the operations that Israel, during the first seven years since its establishment had already brought to a conclusion — such as the drainage of the Huleh and the diversion of the waters of the Yarkon to the Negev. How it will all work out still remains to be seen.

In the meantime, in the eagerness to get ahead, mistakes are bound to be made. Israel in its experimental stage has committed many. But this was unavoidable. The difference between the Master Engineer and human planners is that the former foresees with clarity what will happen, while the latter, not knowing the complexion of the future, are compelled to grope their way. Furthermore the Omnipotent is capable of creating something out of nothing. Man, on the other hand, is forced to make use of the means already available, being limited usually to the materials close at hand, which are often far from perfect.

In its endeavor to put an end to the unhealthy conditions created by the compulsory idleness to which the new immigrants were reduced in the Maabarot, or transition camps, the government of Israel devised a new scheme for their integration in the state. It decided to send the newcomers, immediately upon their arrival, to places prepared for them in colonies planted in strategic positions near the border. It thereby hoped to achieve two purposes at one blow. One was to turn these latest settlers into self-sustaining and productive citizens as soon as possible. The second was to strengthen the country’s defenses. Well, not all the colonists-in-spite-of-themselves leant themselves so willingly to this experiment. Some even rebelled and ran away. The attitude of a goodly number is reflected in the following story that has been making the rounds in Israel. A teacher in one of the settlements, made up of recent immigrants, gave her pupils the assignment of writing a few sentences in Hebrew. One of them thereupon presented her with a composition worded as follows. Ani ba-aretz tov ba-aretz. Ou shalchu el hanegev. Translated word for word this means “I am in the land. It is good in the land. Me they sent to the Negev.” For a grown-up person, who had been in the country only a short time, to put together words of a language, in which he was still green, in such a manner was no mean achievement. But when the teacher asked the author what thoughts he intended to convey by means of his composition, she received the following answer. “Ich bin in d’rerd, gut in d’rerd. Punkt (this was what the dot, placed after the last sentence, was supposed to stand for) mich hot men geschickt in dem Negev.” (“ I am in bad shape, in a horrible shape in fact. Of all persons they had to pick on me to send me to the Negev’’).

Most of the immigrants, that have lately been coming into Israel, hail from countries where their very lives were in danger. They arrived at the shores of Israel, which offered them freedom and safety, destitute and bereft of all earthly goods. Their coming to the homeland constituted for them economic as well as political advancement. Yet many of them preferred to lounge in the cafes of Tel Aviv and wait for some lucky windfall rather than exert themselves in some partially developed rural settlement in the Negev or Galilee so as to achieve economic independence. And why didn’t this opportunity to become self-sustaining appeal to them? Because they were technically unprepared and mentally not conditioned for such a life.

These are some of the difficulties with which the architects of modern Israel have to cope. They explain why they are clamoring for the youth of America and other Anglo-Saxon countries, with their know-how and talents and skills. Unfortunately such desirable potential immigrants are not flocking to Israel en masse because there is no economic or political pressure impelling them. That does not mean, however, that colonists of this type are not to be found. Small numbers of them are encountered here and there, children of well-to-do parents, many of them possessors of college degrees, who left their comfortable homes in the States or England or South Africa to cast their lots with the other builders of the Jewish national home, isolating themselves in pioneer villages in out-of-the-way spots, where they could not enjoy even the luxury of a two-and-a-half-room apartment with sanitary plumbing, of which most urban dwellers of Israel are now able to boast.

My family and I happened to spend a night in one of these kibbutzim, which are by the way all of them without exception extremely hospitable. A stranger may drop in at any time of the day and be provided with food and lodging without charge. We arrived just in time for the evening meal. Supper consisted of hard-boiled eggs, bread and butter, cheese-cake and cocoa. Our host, a highly cultured, intelligent and jovial young man in his early thirties, apologized profusely for the lean fare. Even though meat was now plentiful and was to be obtained in the cities in any quantity at black-market prices, it was seldom seen on the tables of the farmers on weekdays. “Do you know when a Jewish farmer eats meat? Either when he is sick or the animal is sick,” so he explained to us. He had been in Israel sixteen years. All his earnings went into the treasury of the kibbutz, which paid him what he required for his personal needs and allowed him one room for himself and his wife, who was the village nurse. Their children slept in the special children’s quarters. He had had the option, before moving to Israel, to settle in America. But he did not take advantage of his opportunity. Why? For the same reason that the idealistic American youth exchanged the conveniences of an American metropolis for the austerity and hardships of rustic Israel. “Here in Israel,” they say, “life has content and meaning. We are building a state. We are creating a new world. Every day something happens that brings us satisfaction. What was it like in America? What did we do with our leisure time after work, which we did not have to do for a livelihood but merely to keep busy? One cocktail party after another, a dance, a game of bridge or canasta or gin rummy.” As I heard these arguments, I thought of the jaded lives of the pampered rich in our own great country and the tragedies resulting from the emptiness of these lives. I reminded myself of the son of Libby Holman, who, because his daily routine had no more thrills for him — he had experienced all of them already — wasted his young life on a senseless stunt of mountain-climbing, and I saw the point. I realized how much more satisfying the creative life, that these voluntary pioneers were leading, really was than that of many of their ilk in the States.

“But can’t one be productive in America also?” I asked. “Js it necessary to give up all that you have relinquished just for the thrill of creation? We have in the United States of America great Jewish institutions, if that’s what you are interested in. There is B’nai B’rith and Brandeis University and all kinds of philanthropic organizations. There are schools and synagogues and Hadassa and Zionist districts, that afford you an outlet for your energies.”

“Yes, that’s all very true,” would be the reply, “and yet there is difference. Whatever you build outside of the homeland is of only temporary duration. Look at what became of the institutions created by German or Polish Jewry! What is left of them today? But what is established in Israel shall lasts forever. Even the past comes to life here.”

I was a bit skeptical, particularly about the last remark, until I took a walk through the streets of Tel Aviv and read on the sign-posts names like Rabbi Akiba, Maimonides, Herzl, Ahad Haam and Bialik. It seemed, indeed, like the past come to life. Even the prediction made by the Talmud to the effect that “the synagogues and houses of study of the Diaspora would one day be permanently set up in the Land of Israel”[^7] seems to be having its fulfillment today. I never took this statement literally until I attended services one Friday evening in a Jewish house of worship, now located in Jerusalem, that had been moved stock, lock and barrel from Italy. It had been built originally in the obscure town of Conegliano Veneto in the year 1719. But the congregation had gone out of existence some 50 years ago. The last time the synagogue was used for a Jewish religious service was in 1918 under Austrian occupation. Since then no prayer meetings had been held in it. Then, in 1950, through the generosity of the Jewish community of Venice, the entire interior was shipped to Israel. There it is again being used by Jewish immigrants from Italy who follow the Italian form of prayer, the socalled Nusach Italki. Thus it is not only the future that is being built in Israel. The past, too, is being resurrected.

Israel is passing through the process not only of birth but of rebirth as well, and it does not come easy. Sometimes one wonders what would have happened if Moses, instead of leading the children of Israel to rocky, barren Canaan, with its scarcity of rainfall, had taken them to some fertile country like Argentina or France. But who knows whether that would have been so much better? According to Toynbee, the highest civilizations were not produced in the most fruitful regions of the earth but in rugged, forbidding places, like the hills of Athens and Judah, that by their very insufficiency of vegetation compelled their inhabitants to make use of their wits in order to eke out a livelihood.

The creation of the world was accompanied, according to the account of the Bible, by chaos “When God first created heaven and earth,” so we are told, “the earth was void and without form.”[^8] Israel presents so amorphous a picture today. It resembles a melting-pot or, if you will, a pressure cooker. Never in history has there been seen anywhere such a conglomeration of types and costumes. It has been estimated that in this little country of some 1,800,000 inhabitants newspapers are published in no less than fifty languages and dialects When one passes through certain quarters of Jerusalem, Tel Aviv or Haifa, he would think from the accents he hears that he has been translated to Bagdad or Cairo or Fez. Let him go elsewhere and his ears catch sounds of Hungarian or Roumanian or Polish. There is almost as much German spoken in modern Israel as Hebrew. A characteristic anecdote is related about Nahartah, a summer-resort on the Mediterranean settled by German Jews during the early years of Hitler. A salesman came to a store and asked to see the owner. The clerk, who had only recently arrived from Germany, stared at him in complete bewilderment. “There is a man here from Palestine,” she called up to her employer, “and I don’t know what he wants. He speaks nothing but Hebrew.” No wonder that at the last elections to the K’nesset no less than seventeen distinct political parties vied for the votes of Israel’s citizens. It will take time before all these divergent elements will be fully integrated. The foremost unifying factors, that will eventually make order out of the present chaos, are Israel’s public schools and its army. In the meantime the work of creation and building is continuing full force, and there is reason to believe that the ultimate result of Israel’s constructive accomplishments will be the peace it needs so desperately, as the sages have said: “Read not ‘W’rab sh’lom banayich’ ‘Great is the peace of thy children’,[^9] but rather ‘bonayich’ ‘of thy builders.”[^10]
