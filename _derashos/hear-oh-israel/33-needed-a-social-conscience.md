NEEDED — A SOCIAL CONSCIENCE

Several years ago there was published in this country 2 book by a rabbi, who was also a student of psychology, that took the reading world by storm. “Peace of Mind” by Joshua Liebman became immediately upon its appearance a best seller. It was translated into many tongues and brought its author international renown. The reason for its unprecedented. popularity was that it dealt with something that is a basic human need. Every human being longs for happiness, and happiness is first and foremost a state of mind. Material Possessions or the lack thereof, physical health or illness and other accidents of fortune or nature often profoundly influence this state of mind. Its greatest disturber, however, is the conscience. “Every man,” said Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, “has a paradise around him until he sins, and the angel of an accusing conscience drives him from his Eden.”

Now, though it may be disturbing to man’s peace of mind, awareness of sin is also a sign of mental and moral maturity. Before Adam had tasted of the tree of knowledge, he was like a child that is unable to distinguish between night and wrong. Only after he had partaken of the forbidden fruit did he recognize his nakedness and experience the feeling of shame.

The extent of the sense of sin is, of course, variable. Hardened criminals, who are capable of taking human life without a twinge of remorse, lack it altogether. On the other hand there are persons oppressed by the weight of their iniquities to the point of morbidity. The classic example is that of Cain, who, after he had murdered his brother Abel, became “a fugitive and vagabond”[^1] on the face of the earth. His consciousness of the ghastly crime he had committed robbed him of all rest. These are the extremes. Most human beings fall between these two poles. “We have neither the tranquility of the wicked,” said Rabbi Yannai, “nor the sufferings of the righteous,”[^2] No normal person is entirely free from scruples, just as there is no mortal who can claim to be without blemish, although not all will admit it. It takes a certain amount of courage voluntarily to acknowledge guilt. King David rose immeasurably in moral stature, when, after the chiding he had received from prophet Nathan because of what he had done to Bath Sheba, the wife of Uriah the Hittite, he confessed his crime, saying: “I have sinned against the Lord.”[^3] By this confession he almost attained the position of a saint. For since “there is no man on earth so righteous as to do only good and never sin”[^4] he, who after having made a mistake, shows himself genuinely penitent, comes closer to perfection than one who has never had such an experience. That is why the teachers of the Talmud placed the penitent above perfect saints.[^5] At all events confession of sin is the sine qua non, the indispensable condition for self-improvement.

But what is sin? How far does the accountability for sinful acts extend? Should it be limited only to the individual who commits these acts, or does it also involve his family, the community of which he is a part, the society to which he belongs? Certain categories of human beings are, we know, ruled out from the start. “If you have to have an accident,” says Jewish law, “don’t have it with an imbecile or a minor, because they are legally not responsible.” “If they do injury to others, they are exempt from penalties. Others, however, who run into them are obligated to pay.”[^6]

But what about the rest of humanity? Common sense dictates that crime or sin are very personal matters to be charged only against those directly implicated or concerned. “There is no transfer of authority or power of attorney in transgression,”[^7] says Jewish law. A gunman who kills for hite or a Nazi stormtrooper, who with his own hands delivered Irving human beings to the gaschambers, cannot extricate himself from blame by saying he was only executing orders, although those who made use of their services are not free from guilt either. “The soul that sinneth,” declared Ezekiel,[^8] “shall die.” Fathers must not be made to suffer for the wrongs of their children nor children expiate the misdeeds of their parents[^9] Nothing is more repugnant to impartial justice than the imposition of collective penalties on an entire community for the wrongs done by a few individuals. “Wilt thou be angry with all the congregation when one man has sinned?”[^10] asked Moses and Aaron when God threatened to destroy Israel on account of the rebellion fomented by Korah.

Ideally speaking, then, no person can be held responsible for: anything beyond his own deed. “Every man shall pay the penalty of his sin,” declared Moses.”[^11] "This does not, however, seem to have been the view of the authors of the traditional Jewish Day of Atonement liturgy. As must perforce be noted, all the confessionals in it, the ’Ashamnus as well as the ‘Al Chets are couched, not in the singular, but in the plural. And when the actual contents of these confessionals are analyzed, when the sins of which we accuse ourselves are individually considered by us, we are appalled. Are we, who beat our breasts in contrition, really guilty of such grave offenses as treachery, robbery, perjury and violence? Only if- we look upon these transgressions as the trespasses of the Jewish people as a whole, is there any justification for the employment in their avowal of the phrasing “We have trespassed, We have dealt treacherously, ‘We. have robbed We have spoken slander.”

But haven’t we just reached the conclusion that by right every individual should be held responsible only for himself and that it isn’t fair to make him accountable for what someone else may have done or have failed to do? Indeed we have, and that would have been entirely in order if men had lived like hermits, each one by himself in a world of his own. Then the actions of others outside of themselves would have been no concern of theirs. This is, however, not the case. Like most other living creatures man is, as Aristotle described him, “a social animal.” He cannot exist in total isolation. Wherever he is found, he constitutes part of a group. So long, therefore, as he has any power, influence or say at all, whatever happens in his group is his business, and it is not within his competence to disclaim all responsibility for what goes on by saying he did not know about it. The greatest tragedies in human history are the result of just such an attitude of aloofness, of the failure of men, in a position to make their voices heard, to assert themselves.

The destruction of Jerusalem could, in the opinion of Rabbi Johanan, have been avoided if those in authority had exerted themselves more than they did in preventing personal grievances from developing into public scandals. One of the dignitaries of Jerusalem, so the Talmud relates,[^12] had a friend by the name of Kamza and an enemy by the similar name of Bar-Kamza. By mistake his servant invited to a banquet given by his master the enemy instead of the friend. Instead of letting the slip-up pass by unnoticed, the host had the unwanted guest thrown out, whereupon the latter went and maligned the Jews before the Roman government. One incident led to another, the matter ending with the siege of Jerusalem and the burning of the Temple. The original cause of the catastrophe, according to the Talmud, was the fact that not a single one of the distinguished citizens of Jerusalem present at the function uttered a word of protest against the indignity shown to an innocent man. Because of their acquiescence in his humiliation they were deserving of the fate which overtook them and their fellow townsmen.

Yes, when outrages are committed in broad daylight and the responsible leaders of a community do nothing to check them, they become accomplices, accessories to the crimes, almost as guilty as the actual perpetrators themselves. It is on this count that the intellectuals of Germany, its scientists, university professors and clergymen, who remained silent when Hitler and his henchmen did their dastardly work, must be charged with part of the blame for what transpired in their country. It was their duty to prevent what their social conscience must have told them was wrong. Since they failed to do this, they must share in the guilt even though they may with their own hands not have aided and abetted the fiends in their fiendishness.

But the responsibilities imposed by a fully developed social conscience do not consist only of the checking of abuses but also of creating such conditions of wellbeing and fostering such standards of morality that the commission of crimes becomes a rarity. There is an ordinance in the Torah to the effect that, if the body of a murdered man is found between two settlements and the identity of the murderer cannot be established, the elders of the city, closest to the place in which the victim was discovered, perform a special ritual, in the course of which they must solemnly declare: “Our hands have not spilled this blood nor have our eyes witnessed (the commission of the crime).”[^13] “But is it conceivable,” ask the teachers of the Mishnah,[^14] “that the elders of the court of a city be shedders of blood? What, then, is the meaning of this strange declaration and why were they compelled to make such a deposition?” The reason, explain the Tannaim, was to make it crystal clear that it was not through their failure to provide him with food that the man found dead had died, nor was his death brought on by their negligence in furnishing him an escort for the journey.

The moral to be derived from this interpretation is that as long as there are in the world human beings in need and we have it in our power to fill that need, so long as men are oppressed and we possess the means of liberating them from the hands of the despots that hold them enthralled, there is an obligation upon us to help them in their plight. And if we are remiss in extending this assistance, we are sinners against them as well as against God. Closing an eye to the troubles of persons in distress and failing to give them the lift that would extricate them from disaster is just as much sin as hurling them into it deliberately. “Thou shalt not stand by,” warns the Torah, “while thy neighbor’s blood is being spilled.”[^15] If these rules apply to total strangers, how much the more so must they hold in the case of one’s own kith and kin? “From thine own flesh thou must not hide thyself,” said Isaiah.[^16]

More than four decades have elapsed since the duty of taking care of our unfortunate and sorely tried coreligionists and kinsmen abroad devolved upon us American Jews. Ever since the first world war, during which we attained our maturity as a community and achieved the economic independence that enabled us to assume the responsibility for the weaker members of the family of Israel, have we been carrying the burden of keeping alive and maintaining the impoverished and decimated Jewish communities of the Qld World.. We acted like dutiful grown-up children supporting their aged parents who, were no longer able to care for themselves. We did this.even though we had but little hope for their recovery, out of a-sense of filial reverence. Then in the year 1948 we were blessed with an addition to our family. “A child was born unto us. A son was given to us,”[^17] replacing the parents that had in the meantime passed to the beyond. His name was called “Israel.” At the moment he is still unable to stand on his own feet unsupported. Like his parents before him he needs the help of his older brothers. How much longer he will require their assistance cannot be foreseen. This much is certain, however, namely that notwithstanding the problems he faces and the handicaps he must still learn to overcome, his situation is far more hopeful than was that of his predecessors in Europe, Africa and Asia. In his favor are such assets as his youth and his dogged determination to make good. Every day finds him getting stronger and more self-reliant. In the meantime, however, while he is undergoing his apprenticeship in national economy and internal and external politics, his necessities of a livelihood must be assured. The stream of United Jewish Appeal dollars must continue to flow into the young Jewish republic to provide food and shelter for hundreds of thousands of recent immigrants who are not yet self-sustaining. But these subsidies, while they may be almost sufficient to tide the nation over the present emergency, are not enough for: building a sound future. To build such a future the state of Israel must receive assistance in the form of credit, loans for capital investments that will enable it to exploit its own natural resources and in the course of time not only repay what it has borrowed but also supply it with a regular revenue for its subsistence. The chief means of raising the sums required for this purpose, for paving the roads on which to transport the copper from the mines of Etzion Gaber and the mineral wealth of the Dead Sea, for purchasing ships that would cut down the cost of imports, for laying the pipes needed to irrigate the Negev, which in turn would make it possible to release funds for the acquisition of industrial machinery, the chief means of raising these sums that was adopted by the government of Israel was the floating of Israel Bonds.

But why bring up these matters this evening? In what way are they related to the theme of the Day of Atonement? Isn’t it a profanation of the sacred to inject so commercial a note into the solemnity of the holiest night of the year, a night dedicated to fasting and prayer and the confession of sin and its forgiveness? A glance at the Haftarah of tomorrow will convince even the most fastidious and exacting stickler for propriety that an appeal for subscriptions to Israel Bonds is fully in keeping with the ideology as well as the exalted mood of a holiday like Yom Kippur. The prophet describes in this Haftarah a typical Day of Atonement scene of his time, more than twenty-five centuries ago. Jews were assembled, as they are nowadays on this Sabbath of Sabbaths, in their houses of worship. They were fasting, their heads bent like a reed as a sign of contrition, while sackcloth and ashes were spread underneath them to indicate their sorrow over their sins.[^18] By reason of all these evidences of remorse they felt certain of pleasing the Almighty and of obtaining His good-will and favor. But they were completely disabused of their expectations by the prophet. God was not interested in mere tokens and gestures. He demanded more tangible, concrete proof of compliance with His laws. “This is the fast of my choosing — to loose the bonds of wickedness. . . to let the oppressed go free... to share your bread with the hungry, and bring the homeless poor into your house, when you see the naked, to cover him and hide not yourself from your own flesh.”[^19] If the pledge of outright gifts of charity, which are to some extent degrading to the recipient, constitute a proper occupation for the Day of Atonement — Zedakah is supposed to be one of the three means of averting the evil decreed for man on this sacred day of the year — then the practice of the highest form of philanthrophy, which, according to Maimonides,[^20] is that which enables him to achieve economic independence, is all the more in keeping with its lofty purposes. Israel is today in need of such assistance and we have it in our power to render it. It is not alms that it is asking from us, but a show of confidence by investing some of our savings in its commerce and industry in return for immediate dividends and eventual repayment of the principal. There is, of course, as in every business venture, the question of the safety of the undertaking and the msk involved, concerning which no one can give any definite assurance. It is certainly not beyond the realm of possibility that the entire Israel experiment may — heaven forbid — turn out to be a fiasco and all the substance, effort and energy sunk into it prove to be an utter loss. But if that should ever happen, our conscience will at least be clear. We American Jews, who will have done our utmost to prevent it, will have the satisfaction of being able to declare “Our hands have not spilled this blood and our eyes have not seen.” It was not we who were responsible for Israel’s debacle.

However such an eventuality is very remote. On the contrary Israel is, notwithstanding all the obstacles and stumbling-blocks standing in her way, making tremendous strides forward, and whoever contributes, by the purchase of Israel bonds, to her progress and has a share in her growth and development, will be entitled to the honor of being called, as the prophet expresses it in the Haftarah of tomorrow “a repairer of the breach, a restorer of streets to dwell in.”[^21] He will have the joy also of “feeding on the heritage of our forefather Jacob and riding on the heights of the earth.”[^22]
