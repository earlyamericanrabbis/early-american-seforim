THE SIN OF PESSIMISM

One of many interesting observations made in Shakespeare’s masterpiece Hamlet is that “there is nothing either good or bad but thinking makes it so.” This observation, put by the greatest playwright into the mouth of the unhappy prince of Denmark, is also the conclusion one would reach by reading the Bible’s account of the varying reports brought back by the scouts sent by Moses to spy out the land promised to Israel as its inheritance. The expedition consisted of twelve men, each one a leader of his tribe[^1] All twelve were confronted with the identical set of facts. There was no disagreement among them in regard to the physical conditions of the country they had explored. They admitted without dissent that it was, as they had been told by God, “a land flowing with milk and honey.” Nevertheless their reactions differed. Ten of the twelve were of the opinion that an invasion at that time by the Israelites of the territory inhabited by the Canaanites and the other six nations would be a most hazardous undertaking, an adventure which might result in the total destruction of the invaders. The other two, on the other hand, namely Joshua, who was destined to be the successor of Moses as the leader of his people and the commander of the hosts of Israel, which a generation later actually carried out the occupation of the Holy Land, and Caleb the son of Jephuneh of the tribe of Judah, favored an immediate attempt to seize the promised heritage. They did not deny that the country might be stoutly defended by its occupants. The ultimate outcome, however, they felt convinced, would be in Israel’s favor. “If God desires to give us this land, we have nothing to fear from its present inhabitants. In spite of their vaunted might they will be our bread.”[^2] As Scripture records, the masses accepted the pessimistic view of the majority of the scouts, so that the Israelite invasion of Canaan was postponed forty whole years.

Now the success with which the delayed invasion met proves that the pessimists had been wrong, and the lesson derived therefrom is that the socalled stubborn facts are not as stubborn as they are often presumed to be. At all events the state of the human mind, reacting upon the problems they pose, is as important in their resolution as the problems themselves. There was nothing in the annals of the Canaanites to justify the supposition that there had taken place any radical change in their military situation during the 40 or 38 years, that intervened between the date of the dispatch of the first reconnaissance mission by Moses and the invasion of Canaan under Joshua. They were still in possession of the fortifications they had before as well as of the weapons of warfare in which the invaders were woefully lacking. It is definitely known, however, that there had developed a decided improvement in the psychological readiness of the Israelites; and it was this readiness that spelled the difference between defeat and victory.

The statement of Hamlet “there is nothing either good or bad but thinking makes it so” is, of course, as it stands, a bit of sophistry, and it was with his tongue in his cheek that the prince uttered these words when he did. For there certainly are some things that are worthless from every point of view, just as there are others that are on the whole beneficial, useful and constructive. It is undeniable, however, that what men think about the situations that face them has a great deal to do with their happiness or unhappiness over them and with their success or failure in coping with them. This does not justify a surrender to a completely unwarranted optimism, which has been defined as that attitude which enables “a tea kettle to sing though in hot water up to its nose.” Nor would it endorse the point of view of the victim of a flood who, when the water of the river that had overflowed its banks reached above the windows of his house, consoled himself by saying that his windows needed a washing anyhow. The instances we have in mind are those — and they are in the majority — in which the possibilities of being confident or worried are more evenly divided. For example, when a cup is half filled, the pessimist will remark that it is half empty, while the optimist will declare, with equal propriety, that it is half full. Herein lies the difference between the two philosophies. The representative of the one looks always at the dark and sordid side of existence, and curses the day on which he was born. The advocate of the other regards its bright, pleasant aspects, picks out the good from the evil and is grateful and happy to be alive.

Judaism’s preference of the optimist over against the pessimist is made clear not only by what the Torah has to say on the subject of the “scouts,” but even more so by the first remark attributed to the Creator upon His completion of the work of creation. “And God saw all that He made,” says the Bible,[^3] “and behold it was very good.” The use in this statement of the word “all,” commented Israel’s sages,[^4] indicates that death, too, is to be included among the things that are good, and there are indeed occasions when this greatest of all misfortunes that can befall living beings seems welcome.

This predilection on its part for the cheerful over against the gloomy frame of mind should not be taken to mean that Judaism wants its adherents to close their eyes to the existence of evil in the world. To pursue such a course would be mere self-deception, playing the ostrich. It would serve as the greatest encouragement to corruption and tyranny and vice, whereas the Torah has commanded repeatedly the eradication of evil wherever it might manifest itself.[^5] What is intended is that in exterminating what is reprehensible and harmful, sight should never be lost of what is good and wholesome, that in the surgical operation, that must at times be performed, care be taken not to cut out the healthy tissue together with the diseased portion, or, to use a colloquial metaphor “not to throw out the baby with the wash.”

The world we live in is a mixture of good and evil, and we are called upon to take this mixture and make the most of it. The failure to do this, the cowardice they displayed when they permitted their fears to drown out their hopes, was the fault found with the scouts who brought back the pessimistic report and the people that followed them in their point of view. It was the cause of the postponement for an entire generation of Israel’s entrance into its Promised Land. It takes a certain amount of courage to be optimistic. The ten scouts and their followers lacked that courage and they were punished for it. For want of courage betokens want of faith, and that is, of the sins of which man can be guilty against God, the most serious. It is, as Joshua and Caleb had aptly warned, nothing short of mutiny. “Only rebel not against the Lord.”[^6] Since God is goodness itself, and whatever He does is, as Rabbi Akiba stated, for man’s benefit,[^7] to doubt the eventual fulfillment of God’s promises is equivalent to denying the very existence and reality of God.
