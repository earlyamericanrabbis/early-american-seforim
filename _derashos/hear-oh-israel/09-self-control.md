SELF-CONTROL

The personality of Joseph, who is introduced to the readers of the Pentateuch in the section called “Vayesheb”, is unquestionably one of the most fascinating in the whole of biblical literature. What makes Jacob’s well-favored and accomplished next-to-the-youngest son so attractive and appealing is his bigness of heart, his readiness and willingness, when he has become mighty and powerful, to forget and forgive the wrong done to him in his youth, when his fate was to some extent in their hands, by his older brothers. In rabbinic tradition, however, Joseph’s chief claim to fame was due to another trait of his, namely his ability to resist temptation. This is implied in the epithet, that is usually attached to his name, of “hazaddik”, “the righteous.”[^1] That Joseph should have been magnanimous toward his own flesh and blood, though they had once intended to harm him, at a time when he possessed the power of revenge, did not surprise our sages. After all he could afford, because of the position of eminence that he had attained, to be generous toward his former enemies. What seemed marvellous to them was his ability, when he was young and handsome, to muster sufficient moral strength to withstand the seductions and allurements of sirens like Potiphar’s wife, that lie in wait for the young and inexperienced, seeking to lead them astray, that he should have done this in an environment like the Egypt of his day, deprived, by his distance from the parental home, of the protective guidance and restraining influence of a wise and loving father. That was a feat of the first magnitude. “Joseph put to shame the libertines,” says the Talmud[^2] He gave the lie to the licentious, who attribute their deviation from the path of virtue to the weakness of the flesh.

The power of self-control has always evoked the admiration of men. Israel’s sages were not alone in their praise of him who succeeded in “subduing his impulses.”[^3] The Stoics, too, regarded the conquest of the passions as the foremost of human virtues. They agreed with the proverbist that the exercise of restraint upon one’s self was a greater act of heroism than capturing a city.[^4]

Now it cannot be denied that constancy in heeding the injunction of “And do not go astray after your hearts and after your eyes”[^5] requires an iron will. Nevertheless it might well be asked whether the practice of continence for its own sake is either wholesome or beneficial. Hasn’t modern psychiatry demonstrated the fact that many of the neuroses and complexes, from which human beings in all walks of life suffer, that cause them to become queer, are due to suppressed desires, and that the only way to restore them to normalcy is to release the pent-up emotions and satisfy the unanswered needs?

The truth is, so far as the religion of Moses is concerned, that notwithstanding the rigid discipline it prescribes for its adherents — and let it be recalled that the prohibitions are in the ratio of about 3 to 2 over against the positive precepts, to be exact 365 against 248[^6] — it ascribes no particular virtue to self-denial as such. For a human being voluntarily to deprive himself of the most delectable pleasures of life, just in order to prove how much punishment he can take and how much he can do without, is not saintliness in the view of normative Judaism. On the contrary the Nazirite, for example, who foreswears the use of wine is, so the rabbis observe, called in the Torah “a sinner against the soul”,[^7] and the confirmed bachelor, by remaining in his single state, negates the divine purpose inherent in God’s creation of man male and female.[^8]

What, then, is it that is demanded, if it is considered equally reprehensible to either suppress desires or to yield to them? The answer given by most thoughtful minds, by philosophers Jewish as well as non-Jewish, is to put reason in charge, to permit the intellect to dominate the passions, to let the judgment decide, upon due deliberation, by the weighing of pros and cons, whether the claims presented by the impulses are to be accepted or rejected.

It is this procedure that constitutes the difference between civilization and primitivism, that marks off the person of breeding and culture from the ignoramus and the boor. ‘The civilized man, much as he may love his freedom, realizes that, in order to attain his objective, he must follow certain rules. He cannot always do what he likes at the moment when his spirit moves him thereto. Often he must bide his time until the opportunity presents itself, and there is always the unavoidable necessity of giving up one thing for the sake of another that is more valuable. The savage, on the other hand, is unrestrained, reckless. He lets himself go whenever he feels like it, regardless of the effect upon the bystanders and without considering the consequences. Momentarily he may feel more satisfied than his more civilized well-bred brother. It is so much more difficult to keep quiet when the blood is boiling and the instincts and feelings are aroused calling for retaliation. In the long run, however, maintaining a cool head and figuring out the most effective method of dealing with a given situation gets one much further in achieving his goal.

Joseph’s constancy, his steadfast adherence to the principles he had brought with him from home, as Jacob, according the rabbinic expositors, was able to acknowledge when the glad tidings reached him that “Joseph was still alive”,[^9] — that is to say that “he had persisted in his righteousness — may be traced ultimately to his wisdom, which impressed even Pharaoh, the king of Egypt. Unfortunately wisdom is as rare as it is elusive. “Wisdom whence cometh it,” asked Job, “and which is the place of understanding?”[^10] To know how to live wisely is a matter of experience. That takes time. “Der Sechel kumt mit die Yohren” says a Yiddish proverb. Often men wake up when it is too late. It is this fact that prompted the remark that “youth was wasted on the young.”

But there is no guarantee either that experience will inevitably lead to wisdom. “Not every one that engageth overmuch in business groweth wise,” said Hillel.[^11] For those, who boast of their superior wisdom because of the fact that they had seen much of the world, one might point to the horses that used to pull the carriages, which served as means of communication in the days before the invention of the steam-engine and the automobile. “Oisgefohren a ganze Welt und geblieben der selber Ferd.” “Though they had travelled through the entire world, they still remained the horses they were to start with.”

To make up for the shortcomings of human reason men have sought as guides the teachings of religion. “The beginning of wisdom is the fear of God” said the psalmist.[^12] Joseph gave as his reason for repulsing the advances of Potiphar’s wife his fear of God.[^13] Also he ascribed whatever knowledge of the future he might have possessed to the source of all knowledge. “Not mine,” said he, “is the ability to foresee the things to be. God will answer the peace of Pharaoh.”[^14] Similarly Israel’s wisdom was due, according to Moses, to its possession of just and righteous laws. If others will testify concerning Israel “Surely this great nation is a wise and understanding people”,[^15] it is because they ask themselves “What great nation is there that has statutes and ordinances so righteous as all this Torah?”[^16]

Which religion represents the ultimate truth may be a subject of controversy. This much, however, is certain, namely that without laws and regulations of one type or another there can be no civilization. Man would become, if left to himself, the hopeless slave of his impulses, the victim of whim and caprice, and such a life could lead only to chaos, destruction and death. That is why self-control, the submission to discipline, is a sine qua non of existence itself. This is one of the first lessons that those who are born into this world must learn if they are to achieve any success at all in the course of their career on earth.

> “Your task to build a better world,” God said.
> I answered, “How?...
> This world is such a large, vast place,
> So complicated now,
> And I so small and useless am,
> There’s nothing I can do.”
> But God in all His wisdom said,
> “Just build a better You!”
