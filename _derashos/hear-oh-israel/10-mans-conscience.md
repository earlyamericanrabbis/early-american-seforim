MAN’S CONSCIENCE

In the section called “Vayiggash” the absorbing story of Joseph and his brothers reaches a dramatic climax. The opening scene pictures Judah, the lon among his brothers — “a lion’s whelp is Judah”[^1] — who had some twenty-two years previously made the proposal for the sale of Joseph into slavery, plead with the very same Joseph, whose true identity was, of course, unknown to him while he was making the plea, for the freedom of Joseph’s younger brother Benjamin. It required more than ordinary courage for a mere commoner, an alien in a foreign country, who had come to Egypt for favors, to dispute the justice of a charge made by the redoubtable vice-roy of the mighty empire of the Nile, in the political hierarchy of which the latter occupied a position equivalent almost to that of Pharaoh himself.[^2] If Judah measured up fully to the requirements of his assignment, it was because he was convinced of the justice of his cause and its being worth every risk. Had he not vouched for the safety of his youngest brother to their sorely tried and grieving father? Unless, then, he did whatever was humanly possible to bring Benjamin back safe and sound, he could never forgive himself.

But there was yet another factor that emboldened Judah to take the stand he did. That was his belief that, in spite of the cold formality and the stony exterior presented by the grain-dictator of the world before him, even this hard-boiled official had to have a heart that could not remain callous to the plight and anguish of a parent bereaved of his offspring, and his apparent indifference was but a pose that his office caused him to assume. Regardless, therefore, of the seriousness of the crime that Benjamin might have committed —— granted that it was not just a frame-up but that he was really guilty —- it was inconceivable to Judah that the allpowerful potentate of Egypt, from whose will were dependent the life and death of thousands of human beings, would be party to the destruction of the bonds of a closely knitted family and bring disaster thereby upon an old man, who had suffered so much already. This was the plane on which Judah took up the defense of his brother Benjamin.

The implication of this apologia as transmitted by Scripture is quite obvious. It assumes that in the breast of every human being there sits a Supreme Court called “conscience”, that urges him to do what is morally right and commendable and that would castigate him and give him no rest if he were to violate the highest principles of ethics. The problem is only how to reach this God inside of man, how to penetrate the armor of indifference and apathy with which the human heart is so often hedged about, so as to establish contact with the soft inner core and thus obtain a sympathetic response. Some men’s hearts are encased in so thick a crust that nothing short of dynamite will crack it. The trouble with others is that their conscience is asleep and needs to be awakened, aroused from its slumber before they will do what duty and propriety demand of them.

How valid is this assumption? What proof is there to support the thesis that human beings are endowed with a faculty like conscience and what is the nature of this faculty? Is it something instinctive, native, inborn, or is it an acquired characteristic, the result of culture and training?

When we think of the cruelty displayed throughout the ages by members of the human species towards their fellows, the absolute lack of compunction with which they have gone about snuffing out the lives of others created in their own image, we wonder whether there exists such a thing as conscience, or whether what goes by that name is not merely a figment of the imagination. Can it be said that the brains, that invented the gas-chambers of Auschwitz and Maidanek, or the beasts in the form of men and women, that administered the tortures at Dachau and Buchenwald, possessed that delicate and sensitive organism that is referred to as a conscience? And what happened to the conscience of the great Christian world while these things were going on? Why did the 600,000,000 professors of the gospel of brotherly love maintain dead silence while the blood of 6,000,000 of their Jewish brethren was being shed’? The answer that Hitler’s henchmen could not legitimately be cited as an example of typical human behavior, because they were, after all, abnormal, completely devoid of any feeling of guilt when their victims were Jews, is not enough of an explanation. It does not account for the acquiescence in the crimes of the “lunatic fringe” on the part of the 80,000,000 presumably healthy-minded Germans. True, mob psychologists may tell us that, what is regarded as sin or atrocity when perpetrated by an individual, loses that character in the eyes of the public when carried out en masse, because the mantle of numbers grants immunity from accountability. However, by the standards of absolute justice murder is murder, whether it be committed by the few or the many.

The truth of the matter is that conscience is not a gift of heaven presented to every human being at birth. Had this faculty of evaluation originated so automatically, the members of primitive societies would not have been so savage in their behavior toward each other and cannibals would have been aware of the reprehensibility of the act of devouring their fellows. Also if men had been endowed by the Creator with an unerring instinct for telling apart right from wrong and a native inclination toward the good coupled with an abhorrence of evil, there would have been no need for moral education. All a person would have to do, whenever a choice would have to be made, would be to let his heart decide for him, to follow blindly the dictates of his conscience. But if he did that, he would be as apt to be mistaken and wander off the path of virtue as not. For man is a creature of habits. Hardened criminals have been known to commit murder without being the least bit disturbed. Once a person has become accustomed to violating the law, says the Talmud,[^3] what is forbidden seems permissible to him.

Though a disposition may exist in many human beings to be kind and sympathetic and generous, the content of men’s moral judgments, the valuation they place on acts as good or evil, is something that has to be acquired, studied, learned. Perhaps Socrates was a trifle too optimistic when he thought that knowledge alone, mere acquaintance with the nature of virtue, was sufficient incentive for virtuous conduct. Yet it is certain, as Hillel observed, that “an uncultured person cannot be truly sin-fearing nor an ignoramus properly pious.”[^4] Man is incapable of distinguishing between good and evil until he has eaten of the tree of knowledge. This point is made crystal clear by what the Bible relates about the experience of Adam and Eve in the Garden of Eden. The awakening of conscience, the feeling of guilt when a wrong has been committed, such as failure to live up to a divine command, coincides with the birth of intelligence in the human brain.

Of course there is also the danger of going to the other extreme. When the weight of guilt becomes too oppressive, as it did in the case of Cain, who, after he had slain his brother Abel, exclaimed: “My iniquity is too great to be endured,”[^5] it can do such damage to the personality of the sin-laden soul as to bring about its disintegration. To prevent such an eventuality God’s pardon becomes an absolute necessity. This accounts for the longing, so prevalent in our day, for peace of mind, for the strong desire on the part of the present generation of men to liberate themselves from the guilt-complex, by the abolition of the whole concept of sin, so that they might, since they cannot completely rid themselves of sin, at least sin with impunity, “eat their cake and have it.”

It is the supreme achievement of Judaism to have been able to steer a middle course, to have developed the human conscience in such a way as to make man accept his responsibilities without carrying his sense of guilt to excess. It cannot be expected, such is its hypothesis, for mere mortals to do no wrong. Joseph’s brothers were no exception to the rule, and Scripture makes no attempt to conceal or belittle their crime. “To err is human.” Considering the fact, however, that it is impossible for man in his weakness to keep from straying, once the brothers of Joseph had reached the point, after what they had done, of confessing. “But we are guilty concerning our brother,”[^6] they had risen to the greatest moral heights attainable by man.

This is, to be sure, a goal still to be achieved. The Talmud relates, that, when Rabban Johanan the son of Zakkai was requested on his death-bed for a parting blessing to his pupils, the sage responded with the wish that “the fear of heaven might be as great upon them as the fear of men.” “Ts this the best you can say to us?” asked the startled disciples. “Yes”, replied the dying master, “for when a human being commits a transgression, what he is usually concerned about is that his deed might remain hidden from the eyes of men.”[^7]

It was on this basis that Judah made his plea on behalf of Benjamin. What he feared the most was not incarceration in a foreign land, or any punishment physical or mental that might be inflicted upon him, but the prospect of remaining a sinner against his father all his life. He hoped that the vice-roy of Egypt, in whom he did not recognize his own brother Joseph, would understand his position, and, sympathizing with him, accede to his petition of clemency. What a wonderful world ours would be if the differences among individuals and nations, their claims and counterclaims, could be settled on such a basis!
