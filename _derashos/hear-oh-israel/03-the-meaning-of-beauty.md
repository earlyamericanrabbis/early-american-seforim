THE MEANING OF BEAUTY

The aforegoing discussion revolved around the problem of evil. It attempted to answer the question of how a God, who desires only the wellbeing of His creatures, could have allowed tendencies to manifest themselves in them that had to lead inevitably to their destruction, as happened to the contemporaries of Noah, “the generation of the deluge”, whose depravity had reached such depths that the Creator could no longer tolerate their existence. Now the opposite of wickedness is goodness, and the most common synonym of the adjective “good” is the term “beautiful.” The ancient Greeks had one word for both concepts. To kalon leant itself to the rendering of “the good” as well as “the beautiful”. In the Hebrew Scriptures the two ideas are more clearly marked off from each other, the word tov (“good”) being used generally to designate moral excellence, while yafeh (“beautiful”) is employed to indicate physical attractiveness, that which appeals to the aesthetic senses, notably the sight and the hearing.

That beauty plays a most important part in the human economy is a point that does not have to be labored. The entire cosmetics industry is dedicated to it. The original motivation in the making of clothes by men was, according to the anthropologists, decoration rather than utility. The definition most frequently given of art is that it is “the creation of the beautiful.” So dominant a force in human life cannot be disregarded without disastrous consequences. For just as attractive landscapes, elegant attire, a handsome face and shapely figure arouse the admiration of the beholders, so ugliness and untidiness produce revulsion and disgust. “Woman,” say our sages, “was created for beauty”,[^1] and it is a positive duty for a wife to be well groomed and look her best, not only to win the approval of her friends but to retain the affection of her husband. Scripture seems to go out of its way to call attention to the fact that the mothers of our race, the matriarchs Sarah, Rebecca and Rachel were each of them outstanding specimens of feminine pulchritude. Thus for instance Abraham is reported to have said to Sarah, when they arrived in Egypt: “I know that thou art a fair woman to look upon.”[^2] Also Moses was enjoined to make for the priests of Israel special vestments “for honor and for glory”.[^3] But if that is really the case, why does the proverbist, in the chapter in which he portrays the ideal wife, declare: “Grace is deceitful and beauty is vain”, and what prompted the remark of Rabbi Simon that “he who interrupts his study of the Torah, in order to express his admiration for a beautiful tree or field, commits a sin deserving of the death penalty”?[^4]

In order to reconcile this seeming contradiction it is necessary to analyze more fully the significance of the term beautiful, to determine what it is exactly that is meant when it is asserted that a certain object or individual possesses the attribute or quality of beauty or pulchritude. As the Greek Plato and Aristotle understood it, the essence of beauty resides in symmetry and order, in regularity and unity of design, in gracefulness of form and rhythmical, harmonious expression. Beauty of this type, being completely objective, detached from individual human feeling and, therefore, presumably of universal validity, would not require any justification in utility. “We do not blame rubies and emeralds,” said John Ruskin, “because we cannot make them into heads of hammers”. Even so would it be silly, when we see a beautiful painting or statue, to ask what purpose it serves. This is the theory that underlies the slogan of “art for art’s sake.” It was also this desire to satisfy the aesthetic senses exclusively that impelled the talented Greeks, with their fertile imagination, to dedicate themselves not merely to the creation of works of external beauty but to the actual deification of pulchritude, making all other human values subordinate to it. In their exaltation of beauty they went so far as to sacrifice conjugal fidelity, the sanctity of the home and family solidarity and other virtues, that are the very foundation of the stability and survival of the human race. Everything was forgiven to the creators of things of beauty.

This conception of beauty was rejected by Judaism. Beauty, it said, is not something purely objective. It is primarily subjective, in the eyes of the beholder, because without someone to admire a work of art, the effort put into creating it would be wasted. Furthermore standards of beauty vary from time to time, from country to country and from person to person. Also if the proposition “a thing of beauty is a joy forever” be accepted as true, then the beauty to which reference is made could not have been simply

> “The show of shapely limbs and features. No.
> These are but flowers
> That have their dated hours
> To breathe their momentary sweets, then go”’.

The beauty that is perennial is that which reveals something less transitory than itself, that makes men dream of what charms life beyond the fugitive moment which seduces them.

> “Tis the stainless soul within
> That outshines the fairest skin.”

Genuine beauty, that is more than just skin deep, is that “which the soul can feel after the senses have perceived it. For though the body tires, the soul never wearies; the more it admires, the more it is exalted.”

For all the compliments they paid to the aesthetic achievements of the artistic Greeks, to which they referred as “the beauty of Japheth”,[^5] the teachers of Judaism could not go along with them in the excessive attention the former gave to the outward form. In opposition to the Greek gospel of the “the holiness of beauty,” the followers of the religion of Moses proclaimed the doctrine of “the beauty of holiness.” “Worship the Lord in the beauty of holiness”[^6] enjoined the psalmist. That is the implication of the remark of the proverbist “Grace is deceitful and beauty is vain. A woman that feareth the Lord, she shall be praised.” In the same vein has it been said: “Beauty of form may affect the mind, but then let it be understood that it is not the mere shell we admire, we are attracted by the idea that this shell is only a beautiful case adjusted to the shape and value of a still more beautiful pearl within. The perfection of outward loveliness is the soul shining through its crystalline covering.”

By all means let us surround ourselves with beauty. It is essential for our contentment and satisfaction. “Three things set a human being’s mind at ease,” says the Talmud,[^7] “a beautiful home, a beautiful wife and beautiful clothes”. Let us beware, however, of making beauty an end in itself to which all else is rendered subservient. Let us remember always that “sf virtue accompany it, beauty is the heart’s desire, if vice associate it, is is the soul’s purgatory. It is the wise man’s bonfire, and the fool’s furnace.”
