FOR WHOM WAS THE TORAH MADE?

The date of the Feast of Weeks coincides, according to our tradition with the most important religious experience of our people, the giving of the Torah at Sinai. The circumstances surrounding and the effect produced by this event are fully described in the special pentateuchal lesson read on the holiday and are, therefore, hardly in need of repetition. The theophany at Mt. Sina: was, however, followed by an episode the details of which are a bit obscure and on that account worth considering. After the decalogue had been proclaimed amidst thunder and lightning and volcanic convulsions that filled the hearts of the listeners with awe and trembling, Moses, we are told, ascended to the top of the mountain and tarried there for forty days and forty nights.[^1] What did he do during that time? Scripture tells us he went to receive the tablets of stone and be instructed in the laws and commandments of God.[^2] But why should it have taken so long? If ordinary every-day Jews can complete Tikkun (an abridged version of the Torah) in one night of Shabuot, why should not a few days have sufficed for a man of Moses? intellect?

A fantasy of our sages[^3] offers a cue to the solution of this problem. The acquisition of the Torah by Moses did not come about so easily, they inform us. As the lawgiver entered the heavenly portals, there was a stirring and rushing among the angels. Cherubim and seraphs came flocking from all sides to the Throne of Glory to protest to the Almighty against the sequestration of the priceless treasure. “Wilt Thou give away this gem which Thou keptest with Thee for 974 generations, before the world was created, to mere flesh and blood?” “What is man that Thou shouldst remember him and the son of man that Thou shouldst be mindful of him?”[^4] “Why waste Thy perfect law on so sinful a creature? He will desecrate it. He will treat it with contempt. Better let it remain here. Give it to us. We will carry it out faithfully.” Moses was terrified when he heard these words, but the Eternal remained unmoved in His determination. “Answer them,” He commanded Israel’s spokesman, and Moses, seizing hold of the Throne of Glory to reassure himself, read out the first commandment: “I am the Lord thy God who brought thee out from the land of Egypt.” “Well now,” the heavenly ruler, turning to the angels, asked the celestials, “have you gone down to Egypt? Have you ever been slaves to Pharaoh? Why, then, should the Torah be yours?” “What else is written there??? Moses continued “Thou shalt not have another God.” “Do you perchance dwell among the heathens that you should require such a warning?” Moses: “Thou shalt not take the name of the Lord thy God in vain.” God: “Is there any giving or taking among you?” Moses: “Remember the Sabbath day to keep it holy.” God: “Do you perform any physical labor that you should be in need of rest?” “Again what is written in it?” “Honor thy father and thy mother.” “Have you parents that you should be bidden to honor them?” “What else?” “Thou shalt not kill. Thou shalt not commit adultery. Thou shalt not steal. Thou shalt not bear false witness... Thou shalt not covet... .” “Is there any jealousy or passion among you?” Thus the angels were beaten in every quarter and they had to admit at last that God was right, that the Torah was not made for angels,[^5] but for human beings with human wants and human shortcomings.

I cannot believe that this homily represents nothing more than a flight of rabbinic fancy. It seems to me, rather, that our sages meant to voice in this poetic form an opinion on an important problem that may have given them as much concern in their time as it worries us today. Religion, especially the Jewish religion, imposes rather heavy burdens on those that profess it. There are so many duties to be carried out and so many pleasurable pastimes that must be avoided. “This you must not say and that you durst not do. Here you may not eat and there you are forbidden to go. Restrain your appetites! Check your desires!” To do justice to all these injunctions one would have to be an angel, and angels do not exist on earth. What is the consequence? The Torah is violated right and left, and people become hypocrites because they profess to abide by commitments they could not possibly ever live up to. Would it, therefore, not be better to reduce the demands, to leave the Torah in heaven and give men a less burdensome Law? The answer to this question is that the Torah was not made for angels. Perfect beings do not require instruction and admonitions. They do what is right of their own accord. It is the poor sinful mortal, whose tendency it is to go astray, that must be supplied with guides and standards. Just because he is so weak does he need something strong to keep him straight.

The attitude of our sages in this matter assumes considerable interest when brought into relationship with the moral conditions of our own time. There is manifest in this age of speed and locomotion a strong inclination towards change in all fields of human endeavor. A proposal has even been made by a certain prominent scientist to recast the Ten Commandments. One of the reasons given by him for such a move is that the original decalogue is antiquated, that it is not obeyed anyhow, and that there is no sense, therefore, in keeping it on the statute books. Better give men a law they will follow than one they honor only in the breach. There might have been some merit to this argument if its basic assumption were correct that only modern men do not observe the Ten Commandments. If we take the word of our teachers for it, however, the ancients were not blameless in this respect either, and if that is so, the abolition of the Ten Commandments would not render humanity much of a service. For if, with the standards being what they are, the results are so poor, how much poorer would they be if theory would be levelled down to practice? The ideal must always be higher than reality. To make the potential correspond to the actualities of life would be to humor man’s lowest instincts, to drag him down to the mire instead of lifting him up to heaven. “The perfect law was not made for blameless angels but for sinful men.”

This argument applies with even greater force to modern Jewish life, especially as it manifests itself in America. Judaism has a double enemy to combat here. It is not only the general trend of modern times that it has to resist but also the strong assimilationist tendency of the American environment. The problem of observance has for these two reasons become a very vexing one. Even in congregations that consider themselves Orthodox, at least ninety percent of the membership fails to comply with the most essential externals of the Jewish religion. The question is on this account often raised whether it would not be better to lighten the burden, to make the yoke easier, so that the people would keep a little at least and not go away altogether frightened by the weight of their obligations. That has been the argument of all reformers, whether they belong to the extreme radical or the more conservative school. Our sages evidently did not share this view, for they expressly stated that the Torah was not intended for the ministering angels but for human beings, and if it was meant for the latter, the Maker, knowing how men are constituted, “for He knoweth our inclination, He remembereth that we are dust,”[^6] could not have expected perfection. Why, in that case, were the standards set so high? The reason is a psychological one. “He who would fly far must hitch his wagon to the highest star.” So long as we feel that we are still far from the ideal, there is a chance that we may make an effort to approximate it. Once, however, we have persuaded ourselves that we have reached the top, our race is over. The illusion of perfection is the greatest hindrance to its attainment. This is another reason for continuing to profess the Orthodox ideal notwithstanding its constant violation in practice. The stamp of approval conferred upon the repudiation of the traditional code by Reform Judaism of the first or second degree may flatter the vanity or soothe the conscience of the violators. It certainly has not made for greater Jewish loyalty, for stronger adherence to Judaism’s teachings. Orthodox Judaism asks a lot and so it gets at least a little bit. Reform Judaism is content with little and the consequence is that gets practically nothing.

It is mistaken policy, therefore, to relax standards. In order that progress may be possible ideals must be kept high. Else complacency and self-satisfaction set in and man stagnates morally and religiously. Let our goal, then, be the maximum and let us sincerely try to reach it, and leave the rest to God, and we will at least attain the minimum. This, too, is what the psalmist had in mind when he said: “Who can discern errors? Clear me from hidden faults. Keep back Thy servant also from presumptuous sins, that they may have no dominion over me, then shall I be faultless and I shall be clear from great transgression.”[^7]
