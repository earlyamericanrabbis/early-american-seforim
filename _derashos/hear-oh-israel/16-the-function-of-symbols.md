THE FUNCTION OF SYMBOLS

After the revelation at Sina: and the laws promulgated there Scripture makes mention of a covenant between God and Israel[^1] whereby the latter pledged itself to fulfill faithfully the obligations it had assumed. But what means were there for assuring the observance by the party of the second part of the conditions laid down by the party of the first part as the basis of their union? The answer to this question was the tabernacle, that Israel was ordered to erect in the wilderness. “Let them make Me a sanctuary,” said the Eternal, “that I may dwell in their midst.””[^2] By the establishment of a central house of worship, in which the presence of God would manifest itself through the regular services held in it, the loyalty of the worshippers to Him would be strengthened and their attachment to His Torah assured.

That God’s dwelling in Israel’s midst could not have been intended to be taken literally but rather in the figurative sense, in which it is rendered in the Aramaic version or Targum of Onkelos, who translates “that I may cause My Presence to abide among them,” is made evident by the remark of Solomon when he dedicated the Temple he had built in Jerusalem: “Behold the heavens and the heavens of heavens cannot contain Thee, all the less this house which I have built.”[^3] The tabernacle was, therefore, not held to be the actual abode of Israel’s God. What did He, to whom belongs “the earth and the fullness thereof, the world and all its inhabitants,’[^4] whose glory fills all space,[^5] “whose throne is the heaven itself while the earth serves as His footstool,”[^6] need a home for? Only in a symbolic sense, then, on account of the significance attached to them, on account of the impression made by them upon the human mind, are certain places designated and considered holy. Our forefather Jacob inferred from the inspiration he had received there that the spot, in which he had spent the night on his way from his parental home to Aram Naharayim, could be nothing else than the site of a house of God. “Verily how reverent is this place! This is nought but a house of God and yonder is the gate of heaven.”[^7] Thus, too, the sacred status enjoyed by the Temple of Jerusalem was due not to any inherent sanctity possessed by the location but to the fact that the place had been selected by God as the spot to which He desired to affix His name.[^8]

When one computes all the effort, talent and wealth that have gone into the erection of temples, synagogues and churches, when it is noted that there are human settlements in the world, in which the majority of the population is literally starving, while the houses of devotion are filled with priceless treasures, one is forced to conclude that the sentiment of piety, that leads human beings to such creativity and sacrifices, must be powerful and deepseated and that the creation of these symbols must fill a basic human need. In the eyes of the sophisticated, of persons who look upon themselves as emancipated from the superstitions of formalized religion, all this may be “much ado about nothing.” “If,” say they, “the millions, nay billions, expended by men on synagogues and mosques and churches, would have been invested in hospitals, the draining of swamps and the improvement of the living conditions of the masses, if the genius wasted on the making of murals and frescoes, on the fashioning of arches and pillars of religious shrines, to which men repair to confess and kneel and pray, could have been harnessed to the solution of some of humanity’s most pressing problems, what a wonderful world ours would be!”

The mistake that underlies this kind of wishful thinking is that it refuses to reckon with realities, to take human beings as they are constituted rather than as they would like them to be. The ethical teachings of religion, because of their bearing on the character of man and on his relations with his fellow, may be far more important than ritual and prayer and awe-inspiring sanctuaries and artistically executed, expensive paraphernalia of worship. The fact remains, however, and the evidence in this direction is overwhelming, that men cannot live nor be molded by abstract doctrines alone. They need for the formulation of their thoughts and even more for the arousing and expression of their emotions concrete and visible symbols. How indispensable symbols are can be gauged by the fact that language, whereby man makes articulate and is thereby able to communicate to others what he thinks, is nothing but a collection of symbols. There is really no earthly reason why the French should use the combination of sounds that make up the word “pére” in speaking of a father while the Germans express the very same idea by means of the term “Vater”, except that that happens to be the accepted practice in their respective nationalities. Nor is there anything in the marks or signs called letters that causes them to be pronounced as they are but the valuation arbitrarily put upon them by the specific usage of a particular dialect. Language and writing serve, therefore, primarily as reminders evoking certain definite trends of thought. Their function is not very much different from that of the fringes mentioned in the third paragraph of the Shema’,[^9] concerning which it has been said: “And ye shall see them and remember all the commandments of the Lord”’,[^10] or from that of the phylacteries, which were to be donned so that “they might be a sign on thine hand and a memorial between thine eyes that the law of the Lord might be in thy mouth.”[^11] The flag of a country is such a symbol. It may bear no resemblance whatever to what it stands for. But by the association of ideas, when the citizen of the country, that it is supposed to represent, looks at it, he is reminded of his duties to that country and of the importance for his own survival to stand together with his compatriots against the common enemy and discharge other obligations for the protection of public welfare.

Aside from these mnemonic symbols, the function of which is to suggest, there are others that help clarify thought by similarity or analogy. Just as picture writing — hieroglyphs — preceded alphabetic script, so are parables older than arguments. From the fascination that television has on our people young and old — and there are homes that will dispense with a telephone but would not do without a television set —- we realize how much more powerful an effect is produced by the pictorial representation of an idea than by a description in mere words. This method of driving home certain concepts is of particular importance in the realm of religion. “In man’s relationship with the Great Eternal all human words and expressions, however beautiful and perfect are altogether inadequate to express his innermost feelings.” The essence of God’s being, so it has been admitted by the profoundest and most advanced religious thinkers, can never be grasped by mortal man. “Man cannot see Me and live,”[^12] Moses was told when he asked to be shown God’s glory. And Moses Maimonides explained the statement “And thou shalt see My back but My face shall not be seen,” to mean that God’s existence and potency can only be inferred by man from His activity. It is not subject to direct apprehension. The best that can be asserted about the Eternal is what He is not — that is to say that He is not dead nor ignorant. The closest that mortal man can approach to Him is by analogy. Thus the Kabbalists stated that corresponding to whatever is to be found on earth there is something in heaven. “The kingdom of heaven is organized along lines similar to those of earthly regimes.”

This is the cue to the explanation of the significance of the ritual objects constituting the furniture of the tabernacle. They all had some symbolic meaning. The candlestick (Menorah) stood for enlightenment, knowledge. Just as light enables man to see, so does what was previously incomprehensible to him become clear through knowledge. God is the fountain of knowledge. “He giveth wisdom unto the wise and knowledge to those endowed with discernment.”[^13] The table with the showbread was meant to indicate that God was the ultimate source of man’s sustenance, as the psalmist expressed it: “The eyes of all wait upon Thee and Thou givest them their food in due season.”[^14] And so for the other appurtenances.

Symbols are, then, indispensable. The need for them to express our highest emotions is inwoven with human nature, and indifference to them is not, as many have supposed, a sign of enlightenment and spirituality. The only thing to be guarded against is the confusion of these symbols with the reality for which they stand. That was why Judaism in its earliest days already forbade the representation of deity by any tangible, concrete form, why the second commandment of the Decalogue, which is really the first, because the first is but a statement, not a command, enjoins: “Thou shalt not make for thyself any graven image nor any likeness of what is in heaven above or on the earth below or in the water underneath the earth.”[^15]
