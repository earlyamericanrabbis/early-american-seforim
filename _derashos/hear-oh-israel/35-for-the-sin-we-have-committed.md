“FOR THE SIN WE HAVE COMMITTED”

One reason for the perennial freshness of our Bible, for the fascination that this book has exercised upon the minds of men throughout the ages, is the fact that the incidents that it relates are not events that just happened, but rather patterns of behaviour typical of human beings at all times and in all places. The ritual of the Day of Atonement is, according to what the Torah tells us, supposed to have been instituted on account of a tragedy that occurred in Israels noblest family. On the day on which Aaron and his children were consecrated into the priesthood, his two oldest sons, Nadab and Abihu, offered on the altar incense for which they had no authorization. A fire from God consumed them and they died.[^1] This is the account given by Sacred Wnt.

Why did Nadab and Abihu perish at the very moment in which they were being showered with all the honors that young men like them could ever expect? What was the cause of the sudden and tragic disaster that swept them away from life so prematurely? Our sages, piecing together all the data found in Scripture, arrived at the conclusion that the two oldest sons of Aaron paid the price of their recklessness, their impatience, their conceit, and recklessness, impatience, and conceit are characteristics of youth.[^2] “Young men in the conduct and manage of actions,” it has been said, “embrace more than they can hold, stir more than they can quiet, fly to the end without consideration of the means and degrees; pursue some few principles which they have chanced upon absurdly, and that which doubleth all errors, will not acknowledge or retract them, like an unready horse, that will neither stop nor turn.”

What makes young people behave in this manner? The answer is simple enough to figure out. It is exuberance of energy coupled with inexperience, the combination of an excess of physical reserve and ignorance of the facts of life. It is easy to be bold and courageous when one does not realize the dangers lurking ahead. It does not take much effort to be an optimist for him who has never tasted the bitter fruit of disappointment and frustration, with an overabundance of resources, a person can afford to be wasteful. Knowledge, on the other hand, causes man to exercise caution, while want impels him to practice thrift, if not actual parsimony.

This does not mean that the qualities youth has to offer are not desirable On the contrary, they are among the most valuable assets at the disposal of humanity, without which progress, in the best sense of the word, would be utterly impossible. If mankind had to depend on its elder statesmen alone, on the Nestors and the Baruchs and whatever their names might be, nothing would, for all their wisdom, ever get done. The trouble with men of age is that they “object too much, consult too Jong, adventure too little, repent too soon.” Take the French Revolution, one of the greatest things ever to have happened in human history, an event that brought democracy to Europe and gave mankind “the rights of man.” It has been asserted by historians that the way for this great upheaval had been paved by the vision of such old men as Diderot and the encyclopedists, and Voltaire and Rousseau. But it was necessary for the Mirabeaus, the Dan-tons, and Robespierres, none of whom lived beyond the age of 42, to come along in order to translate this vision into reality. The last absolute monarch to rule over Turkey was Abdul Hamid. The overthrow of his decadent despotism in favor of a constitutional government was effected by the party that called itself the “Young Turks.” This was no accident. We must, therefore, not berate youth too much for its natural weaknesses which are its greatest strength. Youth’s enthusiasm, daring, and zeal are the dynamos that keep the world moving. However, like all power, this force too must be harnessed so that it does not run wild, and, like an unbridled current, overflowing its banks, leave havoc and destruction in its wake.

Now, ordinarily this does not pose too much of a problem. The aberrations of youth usually right themselves of their own accord. It is related that the Old Tiger of France, George Clemenceau, one of the great statesmen of Europe of the period of the First World War and the era following it, was informed one day that his 21-year-old son had joined a radical movement. Instead of being upset by the news, the veteran political leader took it rather calmly. “But, M. Clemenceau,” said the bearers of the tidings, surprised at this reaction, “aren’t you going to do something about the matter? Don’t you consider it serious enough for you to inject yourself in it?” “No,” replied the elder Clemenceau. “At 21 it is natural to be radical, and my son is behaving as any normal youth of his age would. But you come back to me in, let us say, ten years from now. If you will tell me then that he is still connected with this movement, I will be alarmed.”

Time generally checks the impetuosity of youth, and the practical wisdom that comes with age produces sobriety. However one cannot always depend on this automatic correction of youth’s errors. Sometimes children playing with fire not only get their fingers burned, but, as happened in the case of Nadab and Abihu, they lose their lives with it. Such tragedy can be averted only by precautions taken long before their possible occurrence. What might happen has to be forestalled. It is too late to check it when the damage is already done. In short, if we want our most precious possession, the children with whom God has blessed us, as a means for continuing our existence on earth after we are gone, to escape the pitfalls, moral as well as physical, that might ruin them for life, they must be forewarned and thus forearmed against such perils betimes by the proper discipline.

Exactly what does this entail? First on the list of requirements must be put the parent’s personal conduct, the example he sets for his children. This is clearly indicated in the biblical text. Although it was the youth, Nadab and Abihu, that had committed the folly for which they made expiation with their lives, it was their father, Aaron, who was enjoined not to enter the sanctuary too often so that he might not die.[^3] Apparently his previous practice was the indirect cause of their undoing. There is good ground for this suspicion, because children are their parents’ most persistent imitators, watching their elders with greater scrutiny than the latter would ever imagine. No gesture, no turn of the head or look of the eyes eludes them. They are not deceived by anything their parents might do, for they see right through all sham and pretense. That is why what their parents are is far more important to children than what they tell them, why the pattern of living the elders present in their person influences their offspring much more profoundly than any formal instruction.

> “How shall we teach
> A child to reach
> Beyond himself and touch
> The stars,
> We who have stooped so much?
> “How shall we tell
> A child to dwell
> With honor, live and die
> For truth,

> “How shall we say
> To him, ‘The way
> Of life is through the gate
> Of love’,
> We who have learned to hate?
> “How shall we dare
> To teach him prayer
> And turn him toward the way
> Of faith,
> We who no longer pray?”

Yes, parental example is of tremendous importance in the guidance of children.

Next to it is the content of the program of education. One of the most important obligations a father has toward his son is, according to the Talmud, to teach him a profession or trade that will enable him to stand on his own feet economically and not be dependent on others for his livelihood.[^4] A parent who fails in the fulfillment of this duty is considered as contributing personally to his son’s delinquency. However, vocational training and the acquisition of personal skills can, and often must, wait until the youngsters are old enough to enter high school or college. There is one kind of discipline, on the other hand, that cannot be postponed, but must begin in earliest childhood if the youth is to grow up into a well-integrated, wholesome personality. That discipline is character training, the development of ethical standards, the formulation of a sense of spiritual values, of a healthy attitude toward the world and fellowman — in short, the child’s religious and moral education that is comprised in the term “Torah.” “As soon as a child is old enough to speak” say our sages, “his father is obligated to teach him Torah. If he neglects this obligation, it is as though he were digging his child’s grave”[^5] This must not be looked upon as an exaggeration. A study made of the family history of a group of schizophrenics in one of our well-known mental institutions revealed the startling fact that many of the patients had, during the first 18 months of their lives, been deprived of that parental care, love, guidance and feeling of security, that are essential for the normal psychic evolution of the individual human being. Mind you, the first 18 months of their life! Who would ever have believed that infants of that age are aware of what is going on about them? Yet, Dr. Lauretta Bender of Bellevue’s Children’s Psychiatric Service, on the basis of two decades of experience, assures us that emotional starvation, during this earliest formative period in the child’s life, accounts for a large percentage of that mental imbalance later on, that necessitates the confinement of those suffering therefrom to the psychiatric wards. This must have been what our sages had in mind when they stated that one of the antidotes, equipped with which Aaron was to enter the sanctuary in order to prevent a recurrence of the tragedy of his children’s death, was Torah,[^6] their religious and moral education.

In imparting these fundamentals to their children, parents must be consistent and firm. They must be sure in their own minds as to what they want. The greatest mistake they could make is to lay down different standards for their youth than they have for themselves. Let me illustrate what I mean by showing how this rule would affect our coping with two major problems that confront the Jew of today.

One of these problems is that of defection from the Jewish fold. As the spiritual leader of a congregation of the prominence of Beth Tfiloh, parents often turn to me with the troubles they have on this score for advice and guidance. “Why should it have happened to us?” I was asked recently by a father and mother who came to me in their desperation. “What did we do to deserve it? After all, our child has been brought up in a Jewish home, and while we may not have adhered to every letter of the law, we have been living a good Jewish life, better than the average. What made him take it into his head to do what he did?” I listened to them sympathetically. I agreed with them that they were blameless. Why hurt them at the moment when the pain and ache were keen, and I was unable to help them because it was already too late? In my heart of hearts, however, I felt that they might not have been entirely without guilt. For although it was true that they personally had been quite observant and their household was as they described it, they had not been sufficiently strict in their demands of their child. They had, for example, sent him to non-kosher camps and non-Jewish boarding schools, and been lax with him in other respects. Under the circumstances and because of the fact that Jews are 2 minority, the result was almost a foregone conclusion.

The other question is that of how we may build up loyalty among our children to the Synagogue which we, their parents, have built with so much love and devotion. Is this to be accomplished only at the price of lowering the standards or can it be done without such cost? There was a time when Jews were proud to follow in the footsteps of their fathers, realizing, as they did, the great value of the wisdom of the past, of the spiritual treasures created and accumulated by their predecessors in the course of the ages; and they expected their offspring to do likewise. “In place of thy fathers,[^7] said the psalmist, “will be thy children.” \* Nowadays, Jewish parents often let their children, who cannot claim to possess the knowledge of Judaism their elders have, to set the pace for them. Why? It must be due either to the fact that the elders are no longer sure of themselves, because they have lost that indomitable imperturbable faith that characterized their forbears, causing the latter to make every sacrifice, aye, to suffer martyrdom rather than surrender a single iota, of their religion. Or if these modern Jewish parents, though strict with themselves, are lenient where their children are concerned, it must be because of the assumption that the younger generation is not made of the same fibre as its predecessors. But that is doing our youth a grave injustice. It is an under-estimation of their calibre. Aye, it is equivalent almost to slander. For, truth to tell, the present generation of Jews is just as capable of enduring privation and hardship as those that came before it. Our young people have proven it by the heroism and courage they displayed in the last two wars. The fault, if any, lies not with them, but their elders who treated them as though their children were softies. Such treatment is neither necessary or beneficial. Nothing is more effective in rendering man tough and resistant to temptation and danger than the assumption of real responsibility.

To return to the case of Nadab and Abuhu, their offering of unauthorized fire on the altar of God, which brought the ire of the Lord upon them, is to be traced to their father Aaron’s softness. Unlike his more intransigeant brother, Moses, Aaron was a compromiser. When, during Moses’ absence, the Israelites requested that Aaron make them an idol that they could gaze upon with their eyes and worship saying: “This is thy God, oh Israel!”[^8] Aaron complied with their request. It may have been his intention, as the Midrash interprets his act, to stall for time.[^9] The fact remains, however, that he acquiesced, which was a sign of weakness on his part, and this weakness was reflected in the conduct of his children that led to their misfortune.

Please forgive me, friends, for being as blunt a I am. After all, today is Yom Kippur, a day that calls for honesty and frankness with ourselves, so that we may be in a position to make up for our shortcomings, “for the sins we have committed.” One of these is that we have failed by our lack of firmness and our inconsistencies to inculcate in our children that strong attachment to our traditions that our fathers tried to implant in us. If we want to save them for ourselves as well as for our deathless faith, we must begin now to mend our ways. I do not pretend that every effort we are making in this direction will be successful. Even the Salk serum is not guaranteed to be 100% effective in preventing the outbreak of pollo. Nevertheless, it is prescribed to all parents for their children, because it is certain that it will at least reduce the incidence of this dread disease. The same holds true for the measures we take for the spiritual well-being of our youth. If we will enter the sanctuary of their lives armed with such assets as (1) the best possible personal example, (2) a thorough moral and religious training starting in earliest infancy, and (3) with consistency and firmness, in all our relations toward our offspring, we shall succeed, in a large measure, in achieving our goal. We cannot fail if we understand the nature of our task and strive to fulfill it with unflagging zeal and determination. We may be confident that our children will not let us down, but prove equal to the challenge, as our youth always has been in the past.
