WHY WAS THE WORLD CREATED?

The aim of this series of discourses is to spell out the answers suggested by the Hebrew Scriptures and the traditional Jewish commentaries on these Scriptures to sundry philosophical questions posed by the inquiring human mind. The order followed will be that of the weekly readings from the Five Books of Moses that are still current in the majority of Jewish synagogues throughout the world today.

Although Judaism has produced no philosophical system in the strict sense of the term and no systematic presentation of Jewish thought was attempted before the tenth century of the common era, there exists nevertheless some justification for the characterization of the Jews by Theophrastus of Eresos, the pupil of Aristotle, as “a race of philosophers”. Such a conclusion would seem warranted by the reflections scattered throughout the works of Israel’s Sacred Writ as well as the later rabbinic literature on subjects with which the thinkers of mankind have grappled since the golden era of philosophy in ancient Greece.

The first of these problems to claim the attention of the earliest sources of Jewish thinking was that of how the world, of which man is a part, came into being. Who made it? What did he make it from and what was his purpose in creating it? And if it be asked: “What does it really matter? Outside of satisfying his curiosity how does it affect man’s happiness and well-being to obtain the answers to these questions?”, the reply is that the whole of human conduct, man’s behavior toward the being called “God” and toward his human neighbors hinges upon it. If; for example, it were to be disclosed upon investigation that all that can be comprehended of the universe, in which man is but an infinitesimal speck, came into being by mere chance, that there was no intelligent force operating with intent and purpose in the birth of the cosmos, the logical consequence of such a disclosure would be for each individual to exert every effort to increase his personal power and gratify his private ambition, to grow as big as he possibly could regardless of how it would affect his fellows. There would be, after all, nothing to stop him except the miscarriage of his plans. Where there is no supreme judge demanding an accounting of him for his deeds, the only sin man could commit would be that which he perpetrates against his own self. The crime of crimes would be lack of success, failure to serve his selfish interests. It has been reported of the surviving Nazis in Germany — and there are countless numbers of these deluded followers of Hitler, who have forgotten nothing and learned nothing since the Fuehrer’s defeat and death, moving about free and unmolested — that the only fault they could find with their fallen idol and the sole feeling of guilt they carry on their consciences is that of having lost the war. Had they won — such is their firm conviction — they would have been hailed as heroes and all the charges against them for their alleged atrocities would have fallen to the ground. The Romans were right when they said: “Vae Victis” (“Woe unto the vanquished”). There is no greater disgrace than that of being defeated. Such is the reasoning of all those who recognize no restraining power other than their own advantage. The cause, according to the psalmist, of the reprobate’s wickedness is his atheism. “The despicable one says in his heart- There is no God.”[^1]

It is for these considerations that the idea that the world evolved accidentally, blindly, without anyone directing the process of its emergence, giving the orders and issuing the commands, is rejected outright and from the very start by Israel’s Torah. “God created the heaven and the earth”?[^2] is its first and fundamental affirmation. Whether He created them out of nothing or He took formless matter, that had been in existence since eternity, and molded it into the present shapes, as a potter fashions his clay, is not clear from the biblical text. The most that could be gotten out of it for certain is that “when God first created heaven and earth, the earth was void and without form”.[^3] The rest is a matter of speculation, and either deduction is possible, although nearly all the Jewish philosophers favored the theory of creatio ex nihilo, that is that the world was created out of nothing, no being except God alone having been in existence before He made heaven and earth. They felt obliged to adopt this hypothesis in order to be in a position to explain the possibility of miracles. A God, who possesses the power to create something out of nothing, would have no difficulty in performing marvels that contravene the ordinary laws of nature. But if miracles could be shown to be relatively unimportant, or to be merely a matter of the evaluation of events, quite natural in and by themselves, and man be cautioned against relying too much on their being wrought for his sake, then the need for assuming that the world had to be created out of nothing would no longer be so urgent. That was why creatio ex nihilo was not considered by Maimonides as one of the assumptions by which Judaism stands or falls,

Judaism does, however, as the Bible already indicates, insist that the world which the divine Creator had brought into being, was a good world. “And God saw everything that He had made, and, behold, it was very good.” This is the observation with which the first chapter of Genesis concludes and the basis for the comment of the psalmist: “Let the Lord rejoice in His handiwork”.[^4] To be sure there exist differences between one locality and another in climatic conditions, fertility and natural resources. The surface of our planet is composed of oceans, deserts and forests, dry, arid, barren patches of land and well-watered, fruitful regions, with abundance of vegetation. All in all, however, the earth started out, according to the account of Scripture, by being a paradise, that would have produced enough to satisfy all of man’s wants had he not reduced its potentialities by his sinfulness, greed, lust, lack of self-control, envy and inability to obey God’s commands. It was on account of man’s shortcomings that the earth’s rich soil began to grow for him “thorn and thistle”,[^5] and that he was thenceforth able to earn the bread he ate only “by the sweat of his brow”.[^6]

Why did the Creator go to all this trouble? Of what use to Him was this world that He had brought into being? What did He expect from the inhabitants thereof? The answer of the prophet Isaiah was: “The people which I formed for Myself will relate My praise.[^7] According to the psalmist nature itself was a continuous tribute to the Maker of all things “The heavens declare the glory of God, and the firmament proclaimeth His handiwork”.[^8] And in the words of the Hebrew liturgists, the celestial spheres, as they whirl endlessly through the infinite space and all animate creatures, by virtue of the miracle of life that they exhibit, glorify God, saying “Blessed be the Lord from His place.”

The Bible’s basic assertion is “God is good”. He is interested in the welfare of His creatures. He wants to take pride in His handiwork. He is most reluctant to let man, whom He had created in His own divine image, die. That is why He issued to the ancestor of the human race orders as to how to conduct himself. But Adam, like his children after him, failed to live up to what was expected of him. “And they, like Adam, transgressed over the covenant”.[^9] That was the cause of his downfall and also of their punishment.

The story of the creation of the first ten generations of man, as narrated in the opening chapters of Israel’s Sacred Writ, may not exactly tally with the latest theories formulated by the standard bearers of modern science. It is also possible that it was never intended to be taken as literal truth but rather as a poetic expression of certain religious and moral teachings. However it be, it is a fact that no thinker of either our own time or of ages past has really ever fully penetrated the mystery of existence. For all the advances that have been made in the knowledge of the world he lives in, modern man is still almost as much in the dark as regards its underlying nature as his predecessors were. At one time, for example, all of reality was supposed to have resolved itself into the four elements of earth, water, air and fire. Then it was discovered that there were many more basic substances of which the physical world was composed. Not long after that it was found out that the so-called “molecules” or tiny masses were made up of still smaller “atoms” or indivisible particles. But soon the seemingly indivisible atoms were shown to contain neutrons and electrons and protons. The world of mass turned out to be a world of energy. Finally a little over a decade ago came the startling as well as frightening discovery of nuclear fission.

Apparently we have not yet reached the end of our story. There may be more to come and no one can foretell what it may be. Amidst all the revolutions and revisions in man’s theorizing, however, certain truths have remained unshaken — those which were enunciated more than three-thousand years ago in our Torah, such as the belief that the world God made was a good world, and the contrast between the perfection of God and man’s shortcomings, between the Master’s might and the weakness of the servants. Verily “all flesh is grass, and all the goodliness thereof is as the flower of the field. Grass withereth, flowers fade, when the breath of the Lord bloweth upon them — surely the people grass. Grass withereth and flowers fade; but the word our God — that shall stand forever.”[^10]
