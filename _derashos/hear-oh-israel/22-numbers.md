NUMBERS

The title of the fourth book of Moses is “Numbers,” and lists of numbers or statistics of the numerical strength of the tribes Israel make up the bulk of the contents of its opening chapters. Generally speaking it is hard to conceive of reading matter more wearisome or boring than numbers, dry arithmetical figures without anything to enliven them. Even the names in the telephone directory are more interesting than this conglomeration of colorless digits, because they are apt at least to give us some information about the persons that bear them. Thus for example such appellatives as Berliner, Hamburger, Pariser, Wiener, and Warshawsky are indicative of provenance, while surnames like Baker, Miller and Smith must have been originally designations of the practitioners of certain handicrafts. But numbers — they can stand for anyone or anything and, therefore, nothing. Nevertheless from Pythagoras in ancient Greece, and his Egyptian and Babylonian predecessors, to Albert Einstein of our own time, numbers have exercised an enormous fascination upon thinking minds, so much so that they believed that the whole of reality could be described in terms of mathematical formulae. The differences in color, texture, odor, sound and feeling that the material world presents to our senses, so our most advanced scientists tell us, are at bottom due to nothing more than the varying speeds of protons, electrons and neutrons, of which the socalled “elements” are made up. Mathematics or numbers seem to be the key to the understanding of almost everything that transpires in our universe, from the amount of energy required to move a specific mass a given distance to the temperature necessary to sustain life in a particular animal or plant organism. It is no exaggeration to say that in virtually every step we make we become involved in mathematics. Our physical as well as our mental health depends upon our observing a strict quantitative balance between our needs and their satisfaction, Whenever we go to excess in one direction or another nature takes its revenge on us. Too high a caloric intake by overfeeding is as injurious as a diet of an insufficient number of calories producing diseases of malnutrition.

In short, whithersoever we turn in this material world of ours and no matter what we do, we cannot get away from dimensions and measurements, which means, in final analysis, numbers. It ought, therefore, not to be surprising that in religion, too, numbers should play an important part, especially religion as it is understood by us Jews, in whose conception it is not merely a matter of belief but primarily one of conduct, behavior, action — and human acts are, as we know, performed at a definite time and a specific place with measureable means. Thus, for example, the dues to be remitted to the Temple sanctuary were fixed by the law of the Torah to half a shekel per person. Of the days of the week the seventh was consecrated as the day of rest. Every tenth animal born was declared holy unto God, to whom were also dedicated the first-born of man and beast and to whom were offered up the first fruits of the field The Mishnah lists only five exceptions to the rule, five instances in which no limits were set, namely in the size of the corners of the fields to be left to the poor, in the amount of first-fruits to be delivered to the priest in the Temple on the Feast of Weeks, in the number of animals to be sacrificed during the visit of the Holy Place on the pilgrimage festivals, in the practice of philanthrophy and the study of the Torah.

Judaism has often been criticized for its adherence to these limitations. It has been charged with being overlegalistic, concerned too much with the letter and not enough with the spirit of the law. The letter ties men down to time and place. The spirit does not recognize such restrictions. It breaks fetters. It bursts through all boundaries. “The letter,” it has been said, “confineth, enslaveth. The spirit maketh free.” Judaism does not reject these accusations. It pleads guilty to the charge. “It is true,” it admits. “I do attach great importance to compliance with the letter. I don’t expect my followers to go ‘beyond the line of the law’, and the reason why I don’t is because, being mortal creatures whose life is at best of muted duration, it would be preposterous to demand of them to behave like beings possessed of infinite power.” God may be able in a waterless waste by means of His miracles to feed an entire people. “Is there aught impossible for the Lord?””[^1] Human beings can accomplish this only by adequate preparation and long-time planning, requiring a great deal of effort and foresight, and even then the extent of their accomplishment is limited by their numbers. Only in the Messianic future, when the present finite world order will be replaced by another which knows of no restriction or boundary, will the point be reached when it can be said: “And the number of the children of Israel will be as the sand of the sea that cannot be measured or counted.”[^2] This stage has not yet been reached. The world we live in is not of such a nature. It is comparatively small in size and those who reside in it are even smaller. “The doctrines I teach,” says Judaism are intended for this world. Hence they must be cut according to the capacity of those who inhabit it. These people must have containers to catch the precious wine of the spirit that is poured out on them. Without containers, without vessels called “the letter” (of the law) the spirit would evaporate, run afoul, wasted and unused. Pure spirit unconfined and untrammeled by the letter may have its place in the world everlasting. It cannot thrive in the here and now, because it needs a body to cling to, even as a flame must be attached to a wick soaked in oi! in order to burn.

Judaism has always been conscious of man’s limitations, and has, therefore, been careful not to press him beyond the confines of his physical endurance. Thus, although the Torah has stated explicitly that any person who does not fast on the Day of Atonement, the holiest day of the year, which is still scrupulously kept by the masses, “would be cut off from his people,”[^3] the rabbinic interpreters of the Law permitted an individual, whom fasting would render violently ill, to take the amount of food absolutely necessary to prevent such an impairment of his health.[^4] Philanthrophy is known to be one of the foremost virtues of Jews as a group. Yet the rabbis, who did all they could to encourage its practice, cautioned those who were inclined to be generous, not to go overboard in their liberality. “He that gives charity on a large scale should not give away more than one fifth.”[^5] There is no virtue in stripping oneself to the point of becoming beholden to the goodwill of others. The pious Jews of Eastern Europe were certainly very conscientious about the observance of the Sabbath and would shrink with horror from the slightest violation of its laws, one of which forbade ordering non-Jews to carry out for Jews certain chores that they themselves were not permitted to perform. Yet they not only allowed but especially engaged gentiles, known as Shabbos goyim, for the express purpose of keeping their furnaces burning on Sabbaths during the cold winter days, so that they might not freeze to death. Many towns in the Jewish Pale of Settlement in Russia were inhabited mostly by Jews and the few gentiles that lived there were nearly all of them kept for this kind of service. Once a visitor came to such a place from Berlin, the capital of Germany. Anxious to learn something about the situation of their brethren in that great metropolis, one of the townsmen asked him how many Jews there were in Berlin. “One hundred thousand,” was the reply. “And what is the total population?” “Two-millions.” “I can’t understand this,” thereupon remarked the native. “What for do they need so many Goyim?”

Even in the punishment of the refractory Judaism warned against excess. Before the penalty of flogging was administered upon the individual who had incurred it, the culprit had to pass a medical examination so that it might be determined whether he was physically in a condition to take his punishment. If the estimate of the physicians turned out to have been wrong, the flagellation had to be stopped before it reached dangerous proportions. All that the law authorized was the castigation of the culprit, not his death or his maiming for life.[^6]

Numbers are of the utmost importance in everything pertaining to man’s existence in this world, not only in meeting the family or national budgets, but in the fulfillment of obligations to God and fellowman. That is why in the wilderness of Sinai Moses was enjoined to take up a count of the children of Israel. Only in the hereafter will figures no longer be necessary. It was concerning that time that the prophet Hosea spoke when he predicted’ “and the number of the children of Israel will be like the sand of the sea that cannot be measured nor counted.”[^7]
