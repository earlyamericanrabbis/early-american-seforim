WHAT DO WE WANT FROM LIFE?

The popularity of the solemn festival that we are today observing stems chiefly from the fact that its ideology is so close to the innermost desires of our hearts. Rosh Hashanah is concerned with nothing less personal than our destiny, our fate, and who can remain indifferent when these are hanging in the balance? So we batter the gates of heaven, we pray, just as we greet one another on this day, that we may be inscribed “for a year of health, prosperity and peace.” But what we ask for above all else is life itself. “Remember us for life, oh King that delightest in life, and inscribe us in the book of life, for Thy sake, oh God of life!” It is a prayer that requires no justification because the urge that prompts it is so natural, so self-evident. We want to live because, as Ecclesiastes put it,[^1] “the light is sweet, and it is good for the eyes to see the sun, and... what follows is a blank.” We hope to be able to extract from the short-lived flower of our existence on earth, while it lasts, all the honey it may contain. For many of us, however, this hope is never realized. We plant our vineyard, clear it of stones and keep it well-watered, expecting that it will yield luscious grapes; but all it brings forth for us is wild berries. We beget children, anticipating the pleasures to be derived from what the psalmist has referred to as “the inheritance of the Lord”.[^2] But, instead of being the source of joy we had looked forward to, they often fall short of our expectations. The result is that we feel disappointed, cheated, betrayed; and instead of accepting our fate, making peace with our lot and thanking our Creator for the great gift of life that we did secure from Him, we complain and grumble, as Job’s wife did, when calamity befell her, of having been wronged by Providence, of having been dealt with unjustly by the Master of the Universe.

What answer does our religion give to this bill of complaints?’ How do the teachings of our faith reconcile these thoughts of man with the ways of God? Let us see what contribution the pentateuchal lesson for this second day of Rosh Hashanah can make toward the solution of this problem.

The personality with which the narrative of this morning’s reading from the Torah brings us in contact is one of the most resplendent in the whole of biblical literature. Abraham, the father of our race, was, as he is depicted in Sacred Writ, a person of the highest moral stature. Full of dignity, deeply concerned for the welfare of every human being, even the least worthy, lavish in his hospitality, he was, as his Hittite neighbors, who had dealings with him, were able to discover, truly “a prince of God.”[^3] Courageous, fearless, ready to defend the right, regardless of whether it touched members of his own family or total strangers, he was nevertheless a man of peace who would make all sorts of personal sacrifices in order to avoid a quarrel, especially with those related to him by blood and kinship. In short, he was the kind of individual to whom his Maker could point with pride and say: “My handiwork in which I glory![^4]

To this Abraham God revealed Himself when he was still in the home of his parents, saying to him: “Get thee forth from thy country, where thou wast born, aye from thy father’s house, to the land that I will show thee.”[^5] In return for the inconveniences and discomforts of the journey and the initial hardships that are inevitable in any dislocation and change of habitat, Abraham was promised that he would become “the ancestor of a great nation, that he would be blessed with material wealth and that his reputation would be enhanced.”[^6] All these glowing prospects were held out to him as a reward for his compliance with the divine command, But what actually happened? No sooner did he arrive at his destination than famine broke out in the land. Compelled to take refuge in Egypt, he had the misfortune of having his beautiful wife abducted, though she was later brought back to him. He returns to Canaan only to find his shepherds quarreling with those of his nephew Lot over pasture lands. In the interests of harmony he decides to separate, leaving the choice of location to the younger man. “Tf thou wilt go to the left hand, I will go to the right, and if thou wilt take the right, I will take the left.”[^7] But even then he had no peace, because foreign invaders attacked the city in which Lot had taken up residence, and Abraham felt impelled, out of a sense of duty, to hasten to the aid and rescue of Lot as well as of his fellow-townsmen.

All this time Abraham, who had been assured by the Almighty that his descendants would be “as numerous as the stars of heaven”,[^8] was without a son and heir who would carry his name and to whom he could pass on his fortune. Despairing of begetting a child from his life-mate Sarah, he accepts Sarah’s offer of the hand of her Egyptian maid Hagar. But the issue of this union turns out to be more of a liability to him than an asset. Ishmael, the son of the bondwoman, grows up to be a brigand, a wild man of the desert, “whose hand is against all even as the hand of everyone is against him.”[^9] Then, when in his old age he is at last blessed with an offspring from his wife Sarah, that is worthy of becoming his successor, and he pours out upon him all his fatherly affection, God commands him to take this child of his, his only one, whom he loves, namely Isaac, and offer him up as a sacrifice on one of the hills in the Land of Moriah.[^10]

Such, according to Scripture, was the life story of Abraham, “God’s favorite”,[^11] who was supposed to have been blessed by the Eternal “with everything”.[^12] It could hardly be claimed that Abraham’s was what might be called an untroubled, quiet existence, one that was free from irritations, disappointments and heartaches, and that he always had smooth sailing. His career was marked by many a trial. More than just a few stumbling blocks obstructed his passage to make him realize that this world was not all sweetness and light. And if Abraham, for all his good fortune, did not always have cause for rejoicing, why should others, less deserving than he, expect better?

We are now in a position to understand what makes it so difficult for some of us to take the hard knocks of life, why individuals, who have been on the whole rather well off, “go to pieces” as it were, when confronted with a little adversity. It is due in a large measure to the false image they had fashioned for themselves of the world, to the illusion under which they had labored that reality would always correspond to the phantom paradise in which they had been allowed to live during the years of a sheltered childhood. They forget that

> “God hath not promised
> Skies always blue,
> Flower-strewn pathways
> All our lives through,
> God hath not promised
> Sun without rain,
> Joy without sorrow,
> Peace without pain.”

That is why they lack what is required to cope with the vicissitudes of fate and the changes of fortune.

Another reason why men are so often disappointed in the realization of their ambitions and hopes is that what they attempt to attain simply doesn’t exist. One goal, which most of us, as we start out on our careers and begin to raise families that must be provided for, endeavor to reach is security. We don’t look for riches. “Just give us enough, great God, to satisfy our wants, and let it be regular, certain, sure!”? — a seemingly modest request, one the Almighty really ought to grant. Our forefather Jacob, our sages observe, longed for such security. But did he get it? No. When, after having fled from the wrath of his brother Esau, who had threatened to kill him, and after having been cheated repeatedly by his uncle Laban with whom he had taken refuge, he had looked forward to settling down to a little tranquility in the land of the sojourning of his fathers, he was jolted out of his dream of peace by the disturbance caused through the disappearance of Joseph.[^13] And so it is with everything in life. There is no certainty, no guarantee that any situation will remain fixed, that the safest investment will perpetually yield dividends. There is only one type of security that man can hope for in this world — the ability to face insecurity, to adapt himself to the changing circumstances of time and tide so as not to be overwhelmed by their flood.

A third cause of unhappiness is the fact that we often complicate our lives by reaching out for things we consider essential to our wellbeing, but which in reality only stand in our way and prevent us from paying attention to what counts the most. How much, actually, does a human being require to feel reasonably contented? “Bread to eat and raiment to put on”,[^14] a roof overhead, a family to share life with, the society of a few friends, time for contemplation and reading, and enough exercise and relaxation to keep in trim. That does not normally call for more than average exertion. Yet there are many persons who are not satisfied with so modest a goal. Whether it be to gratify their vanity or feed their ambition, they utilize every ounce of energy they possess and every spare moment at their disposal to multiply what are only the tools of life. The result, very frequently, is that instead of having these tools work for them, they become slaves of their tools. What is even worse is that in their preoccupation with providing superfluities for their wives and children they have no time left for cultivating the company of these beneficiaries of their toil. The consequence is that they never get to know them. When finally the hardworking fathers and husbands do wake up, it is usually too late. They have forfeited their most precious possession, the love and attachment of those who should be closest to them, their own flesh and blood. For what? For a lot of unnecessary responsibility and worry. For as our sages have so aptly said in the Chapters of the Fathers: “the more property, the more care”,[^15]

The only way to remedy this evil is to follow a recipe prescribed by a woman, who, just because she was reared in surroundings of riches and luxury, had come to realize what a hindrance the burdens that wealth imposes can become to the true enjoyment of life. When Anne Morrow Lindbergh, the daughter of one of the most distinguished of America’s diplomats and the wife of the famous flier, who certainly lacks for nothing that money can buy, wants to go for a real vacation, so that she can be by herself and free from all interference and annoyance, she moves into the simplest little cottage by the sea that does not have any of the mechanical gadgets of modern life, not even an electric kitchen stove. She loves, so she tells us, this primitive retreat of hers, because it has taught her the art of shedding, “of how little one could get along with rather than how much”. And she is absolutely right. The smaller and more unpretentious the home, the less the upkeep and the less annoying the help problem. The fewer clothes we have in our wardrobe, the less do we have to send to the cleaner.

There is finally the unhappiness that is brought on by the frustration due to the lack of opportunity to develop our talents, to give full expression to our personalities, to our inability, because of circumstances, to do what we are best fitted for by serving in the field in which we could have made our greatest contribution to society. The correction of such maladjustments is, alas, not always feasible. Some day, perhaps, when the machinery of society will have been perfected, we may get around to it. This may be the era that our teachers had in mind when they said: “There is no human being that does not have his hour (of glory).”[^16] In the meantime we shall have to be reconciled to the fact that, as the world is now constituted,

> “Full many a gem of purest ray serene
> The dark unfathomed caves of ocean bear,
> Full many a flower is born to blush unseen
> And waste its sweetness on the desert air.”

But whether he occupy his rightful place or not, whether he has been successful in realizing his life’s ambition or he has failed in the attainment of his aspirations, there is one sphere in which every human being, regardless of rank or station, has a chance to fulfill his mission in the world and reap a rich vintage of genuine satisfaction, and that is in living a life dedicated to the performance of duty and to bringing a measure of happiness to his fellow-travellers on earth. “Who is the man that desireth life, that loveth days that he may see good therein?” asks the psalmist. His answer to the question is: “Guard thy tongue from evil and thy lips from speaking guile. Turn aside from evil and do good, seek peace and pursue it.”[^17] “Fortunate,” he declares elsewhere, “4s every one that feareth the Lord, that walketh in His ways. When thou eatest the labour of thy hands, thou shalt be happy and it shall be well with thee.”[^18]

This is, according to our sages, the purpose of man’s brief sojourn in the here and now, and in this respect the short time spent in this world is more valuable than the eternity of the hereafter. Although man’s earthly abode is only a vestibule over against what it to follow, it is only in this temporary lodging that he is granted the opportunity to redeem himself. “A moment of repentance and good deeds in this world,” said Rabbi Jacob, “outweighs the entire life of the world to come.”[^19] What difference does it make, once the limited span of existence allotted to us is over, what our material fortunes have been during that infinitesimal interval in the eternity of time?’ Blessed is he whose life is so planned that, looking forward to its inevitable conclusion, he can say’

> “When I pass on, I hope to leave behind
> Not worldly things for some unworthy hands,
> But grateful mem’ries in the heart and mind
> Of fellowmen to whom I have been kind.
> For life is given us to mold a way
> Of happiness for others we have known —
> Unselfishly and true, from day to day,
> To help them bear their burdens as we may.
> There is no death for those of us who know
> That what we do in life lives on and on;
> We reap, the Good Book says, just as we sow;
> Let’s plant our seeds of love before we go.”

When, then, we pray to God for life today, let us bear in mind what life in this world is capable of offering us and what it cannot give us. Let us remember that there is no cloudless, carefree existence, that every personal history has its lights and shadows, its joys as well as its sorrows, and that, as Job told his wife, we must accept the evil together with the good. Let us realize also that complete security is in this uncertain temporary dwelling of ours, that we call the earth, an unattainable goal, so that we do not make the futile attempt of achieving it, but consider rather how best to weather the storm. Furthermore, let us refrain from increasing our cares by needlessly complicating our lives, and recognize that, whatever disappointments we may have experienced in the fulfillment of the desires of our hearts, there is one field in which success can always be ours, namely, in making other human beings happy.

> “These, then, are the gifts I ask
> Of Thee, Sprit serene.
> Strength for the daily task,
> Courage to face the road,
> Good cheer to help me bear the traveler’s load,
> And for the hours of rest, that come between,
> An inward joy in all things heard and seen.
> These are the sins I fain
> Would have Thee take away:
> Malice and cold disdain,
> Hot anger, sullen hate,
> Scorn of the lowly, envy of the great,
> And discontent that casts a shadow gray
> On all the brightness of the common day.”

What do we want from life? Just this. If that is what we look for, life, despite its uneveness, its instability and insecurity, can be sweet, and we shall not be disappointed, but on the contrary, grateful to God for the gift of it.
