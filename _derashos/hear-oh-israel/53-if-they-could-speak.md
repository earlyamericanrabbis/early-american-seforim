IF THEY COULD SPEAK

Once more we are assembled in the hallowed precincts, in which our beloved departed sleep their eternal slumber. We have come to spend a few moments of communion with them. To those who never knew them and are lacking in imagination our conversation may appear to be completely onesided. “From the voiceless lips of the unreplying dead,” so Robert Ingersoll put it as he spoke at his brother’s grave, “there comes not a word. Yet,” he added, “in the night of Death Hope sees a star and listening Love can hear the rustling of a wing.” Just as a loving mother divines, without its being formulated into articulate speech, what ails the infant she carries in her arms, so devoted children, whose hearts are filled with loyalty and reverence for the memory of their parents that have passed on to the world of eternity, recognize, without being told, their unspoken wishes and dedicate themselves to the execution of their charge. “Hearken my son,” says the proverbist, “to the instruction of thy father and forsake not the teaching of thy mother.”[^1]

The most natural request that could be made by those, who by the will of the Master of Fate, were compelled to relinquish the reins of office and desist from their labors on earth, is that their successors continue the work they had begun, that their unfulfilled hopes be realized by their heirs, that the tasks they were forced to leave unfinished be completed by those that come after them. This is the first duty which filial piety would impose upon children to whom the lives of those from whom they were sprung were dear, so dear that they would bend every effort to bring to fruition the promise that inhered in them. Did not our sages say that “whoever is fortunate enough to leave behind him offspring that walk in his footsteps is accounted as though he were alive ?”[^2] And is it not the wish of every parent, when he closes his eyes forever, that somehow or other through his own flesh and blood the things he loved might continue to be treasured and cherished ?

Another admonition to those, who are able with their minds’ ears to hear what their predecessors, who have exchanged their transitory abode on earth for a more permanent home in the world everlasting, might say to them, is to cease pursuing vanities, which can at best be theirs only for a while.

“Behold,” said Ibn Gabirol, writing in sunny Spain nearly 1000 years ago,

> “No jot with thee will stay
> Of all the glory now so great.
> Strangers shall seize thy loved estate
> And empty shalt thou go away.”[^3]

“When you will be where we are now,” they would say to us, “the villas you erected for your residence, the equipage in which you moved about, the extensive lands you possessed, the sumptuous banquets in which you participated, the power you wielded over your contemporaries — all these will no longer be of any consequence. Here the greatest of the great is equal to the smallest of the small. There is no distinction between master and slave, between nobleman and commoner. Only what you did for others is remembered. Only the contributions you made to the wellbeing of posterity are recalled. Only your constructive, worthwhile teachings lend importance and weight to the personality that was yours. Everything else perishes. Man’s foibles and follies are buried with his bones. If, therefore, you are anxious to have a portion of you survive the grave, if you wish to make certain that, when your Master summons you, you do not die altogether, then devote the time allotted to you on earth to the pursuit of goals of everlasting value, to the practice of virtues the capital of which ‘remains intact for the world to come’,[^4] such as kindness to fellowman, the furtherance of knowledge and the fostering of peace in the world. Then will you be elegible

> ‘to join the choir invisible
> Of those immortal dead who live again
> In minds made better by their presence, live
> In pulses stirred to generosity,
> In deeds of daring rectitude, in scorn
> For miserable aims that end with self,
> In thoughts sublime that pierce the night like stars,
> And with their mild persistence urge man’s search
> To vaster issues.’ ”

Finally the message that we might carry away from our association with those who have preceded us to what is our own inevitable destination is one of manly courage, of fearless tenacity, of dignity and pride. “If you have lived well, if you have done your full duty to God and fellowman, if you have stirred up no rancor and left no aching heart, but you have, by your conduct, made many grateful for the gift of you, then you have nought to fear when your career comes to 2 close.”

> ‘What is death to him who meets it with an upright
> heart?
> A quiet haven, where his shattered bark
> Harbours secure, till the rough storm is past,
> Perhaps a passage overhung with clouds,
> But at its entrance, a few leagues beyond,
> Opening to kinder skies and milder suns
> And seas pacific as the sould that seeks them”

If such be the lessons that our contact with our departed has imparted to us, then the time we have lingered in their company has been time well spent and we will have borne witness to the truth of the observation of the Preacher when he said: “It is better to visit a house of mourning than one of feasting, for the former (calls attention to) the end of all men and the living has something to take to heart.”[^5]
