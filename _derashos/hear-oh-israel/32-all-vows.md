“ALL VOWS”

Yom Kippur is referred to in the Torah as “the Sabbath of Sabbaths”,[^1] the most sacred festival of the Jewish calendar year. Since it is a day that is intended to be dedicated to spiritual regeneration, to a complete overhauling of man’s way of life, to his closer approach to God and the fulfillment of his mission on earth, one would have expected the exercises of this holiday to be initiated by a series of resolutions to carry out the lofty purposes for which it was instituted. Instead of that the synagogue service of the eve of the festival is inaugurated by a repudiation of vows, an advance declaration that whatever promises might be made by the worshipper during the twelve month period about to commence shall be considered “null and void”, that the obligations he might enter into shall not be binding. Nidrana la nidre we’esarana la ’esare ush’vu‘atana la sh’vu‘ot.

The impression created by this bald statement among those, who were unfavorably disposed towards Jews to begin with, was that it represented a deliberate attempt on the part of the latter to extricate themselves from involvement in any kind of responsibility. It was in order to scotch this charge that the Jewish religious Reformers of the nineteenth century decided to expunge Kol Nidre from their prayer-book. Nothing could be further from the truth than the premise on which this deletion was based. Even a superficial acquaintance with the Bible and the most casual glance at the sections of the Talmud dealing with oaths and vows should suffice to convince the most skeptical that, far from trying to shirk responsibilities, there is not a people on earth with a deeper sense of obligation than the Jewish. Awareness of his duties is something the Jew is taught almost from his birth. The Torah, with which he is made acquainted when he has barely learned to read, is full of “commandments,” and, as soon as he is old enough to understand their implications and possesses sufficient intelligence to distinguish between right and wrong and make a proper choice, he becomes Bar-Mizvah, that is accountable for his actions. He does not wait until he is fully grown. At the beginning of his adolescence — the age of thirteen for boys and even earlier for girls — does he become obligated to fulfill all the precepts of the Law.[^2] And the obligation remains with him throughout life, until the day he dies.

It could not be otherwise. Responsibility is the natural outflow of intelligence. Only the intellectually immature, that is young children who have not yet reached the age of intelligence, or the mentally defective or unstable are free from obligation. All other persons are accountable for what they do or fail to do. “Asleep or awake”, such is the categorical thesis of Jewish law, “an adult human being, (since he) is (considered) forewarned (and must, consequently always be on the alert, is liable for the consequences of his actions or his negligence)”.[^3] If he places an obstruction in a crowded thoroughfare and a passerby collides with it and has an accident, or if he digs a ditch in public domain and neglects to put up a danger signal and somebody falls into it and gets hurt, he that is the cause of the obstruction is held responsible.[^4] If a man’s ox or donkey, because he was improperly supervised, does damage to the property of a neighbor, the owner must pay.[^5] If a person omits to put a fence around the roof of his own house and someone falls off and loses his life, the guilt is on the head of the landlord.[^6] Thus life is replete with obligations and man must be perpetually on the lookout that he does not overstep the bounds, for ignorance of the law is no excuse. Nor is it necessary to make a formal verbal declaration of the assumption of obligations, as some youngsters do at their Bar-Mitzvah — which a good many, by the way, promptly repudiate again in practice — for most of our obligations are automatic. They are imposed on us without our being consulted by the society of which we are a part or they are inherited from the past. By reason, for example, of living in a country and reaping the benefits of that residence it becomes our bounden duty to pay taxes and render military service.

The automatic acquisition of obligations takes place even in the realm of religion. Some persons may, of course, deliberately and of their own volition adopt a creed not followed by their predecessors and forbears. Most men, however, are born into the faiths to which they adhere. According to the Talmud the agreement entered into by their ancestors at Sinai is still binding upon Jews today. They are mushba‘im w‘om’dim, that is, “foresworn.”[^7] This is psychologically speaking quite correct. Before children have a chance to think out matters, like belief, independently, their minds are made up for them by the parental influences, to which they are subjected from their birth, influences they can never shake off completely when they are grown, however hard they may try. .

The story is told about a Polish nobleman, in the days when Poland was a mighty empire, who boasted to his friends about the loyalty to him of the Jewish manager of his estate. “My Jew Moshka,” he said, “is so thoroughly devoted to me that there isn’t anything he would not do to please me. I am sure that, even if I were to ask him to give up his religion, he would not refuse me.” To prove his boast the nobleman called in Moshka and presented him with his demand. Moshka hesitated a little at first. How could he do such a thing as to abjure the faith of his fathers? But when he saw that his master was adamant and that there was no means of dissuading him from his plan without incurring his ill-will and thereby jeopardizing his — that is Moshka’s — livelihood, he complied, and he and his wife allowed themselves to be baptized.

A short time after this had happened the nobleman became conscience-stricken over the unfair advantage he had taken of his Jewish employee. And so he decided to permit the recently converted Moshka to become a Jew again. Moshka, overjoyed at the good news, could hardly wait to pass it on to his wife. The latter, however, instead of being elated over the nobleman’s change of heart, seemed rather lukewarm about the turn of events. “What’s the matter, Mariashe?” asked her husband. “Aren’t you happy about what I told you?” “Yes and no,” answered Mariashe. “You see it is now very close to Passover, and Passover is such a difficult and expensive Jewish holiday. Won’t you, therefore, please go to the Poritz and ask him whether we can’t remain Christians for just a few more weeks until Yontev is over?”

No, one can’t so blithely shake off the heritage of the past. Of course, there have been Jews that attempted it, by declaring certain injunctions of the Torah — if not all of them — as no longer operative or binding. They thought that by giving themselves a new label they were released from the commitment made by their forefathers. Their reasoning reminds one of that of a certain non-Jewish professor at one of our universities, who had scheduled a seminar, in which the entire student body was expected to participate, for the second day of a major Jewish festival. When one of the students, who happened to be an observant Jew, told the professor he would have to miss that session because his attendance on the particular day, on which it would be held, would constitute a desecration of the holiday, the professor replied very naively: “Then why don’t you join Rabbi X’s congregation? They keep only one day.” It was as simple as all that.

This is, however, not the standpoint of Jewish tradition. According to it the Torah could not be abolished or changed, wherefore any attempt to do away with a portion or portions of it has no validity. “Whosoever swears to nullify any commandment of the Torah,” says the Talmud, “takes a vain oath.”[^8]

However it be, it is clear from what has been said that the sense of obligation, particularly in traditional Judaism, is very strong. What, in that case, could have been the intent and purpose of the solemn repudiation by Jews, on the eve of the holiest day of the year, of all vows and promises? The object was to check Jews in their zeal and prevent them, in their enthusiasm, from overstepping the bounds and charging themselves with excessive restrictions, which they might not be able to keep, with the result that the name of God would thereby desecrated. “It is better,” warned Ecclesiastes, “that thou vowest not, than that thou vowest and dost not pay.”[^9] Since a man’s word ought to be his bond, he must be careful about his utterances, and the best precaution against the generation of false hopes is to desist from making promises lightly. “And if thou wilt stop vowing, there will be no sin in thee.”[^10]

However to adhere to such a rule of avoiding the assumption of fresh obligations is virtually impossible. After all, every venture undertaken, every new step made in life entails the acceptance of responsibilities or of promises to pay. When a man marries, whether he has an assured income or not, he undertakes to support his wife and family. A commercial transaction almost invariably takes the form of a stipulation to deliver certain goods at a specific time in return for payment to be made before or after shipment. The building of institutions, the setting up of governments — these and countless other enterprises of a public or private character start out with promises to achieve certain objectives agreed upon. If there were no such thing as credit and all business had to be done on a cash basis, our economic system would never have developed beyond a very primitive level of barter. Marriages would have had to be postponed indefinitely if it were necessary to wait for a dowry large enough to take care of all eventualities, nor would most synagogues or schools have been built, if they had to be erected without a mortgage. Therefore, Rabbi Judah was right when he stated that, better than vowing without making good one’s pledge and than not obligating oneself altogether, was it to promise and pay. “He that vows and pays is preferable to both.”[^11]

This brings us to a subject that ought to be discussed on this most sacred night of the year, when we are alone with our conscience, when we face our Maker without camouflage or pretense, when we are eager to be completely honest with ourselves. What I have reference to is the greatest single Jewish undertaking of our time, namely the state of Israel, and the relationship to this undertaking of us, the Jews of the American Diaspora. After nearly two-thousand years of gypsy life, of twenty centuries in the course of which Jews were at best tolerated guests in other peoples’ homes, Jewry has taken advantage of the opportunity that presented itself of setting up house for itself again. The establishment of the independent and sovereign commonwealth of Israel, which was: the aim and object of five decades of Zionist endeavor, of propaganda and colonization and political maneuvers and diplomatic overtures, the establishment of this commonwealth was from the start a gamble. No one, not even its most ardent proponent, was able to state in the early days with any degree of assurance whether the experiment would be a success and how long the state would last. After all, the odds against its coming into being were very great. The Arabs were bitterly opposed to it. The former trustee and guardian, Great Bn-tain, didn’t want it, and did nearly everything within its power to prevent its birth. The United States of America was not too enthusiastic. In fact the then Secretary of State George Marshall told Moshe Sharett, who for a short while later on served as Israel’s prime minister, in no uncertain terms that if the Yishub dared to declare Israel’s independence, all funds contributed for Palestine by American Jews would be frozen. As for Russia, Zionism had been stamped by it as a capital crime, imperialist counterrevolution and, therefore, treason against the Socialist Soviet Republics, as far back as the year 1926. The 650,000 Jewish inhabitants of Israel had no well-trained regular army, no arms except those that had been brought in illegally from the outside; and the income from the United Jewish Appeal conducted in America amounted to only a few millions of dollars a year. The chief equipment with which they were armed, as they set out to repel the attacks of the military forces of the seven Arab nations surrounding them, was faith and the secret weapon called “Em B’rerah”, “no choice.” Then, before the shooting war with the Arabs was concluded, the unprecedented tide of immigration, which within three short years doubled its Jewish population, swept over the little country. Even well-established governments, with long experience in ruling and provided with unlimited resources, would have had a hard time trying to cope with such a situation, all the more so a little state that had just been born and had enough to do merely to keep afloat.

It is true that the wealthy Jewish community of America came to the rescue of the infant republic, that the sums collected for building up Israel’s economy and strengthening her defenses were greater than Any ever raised before. Yet even that did -not suffice. And so the plan was conceived of floating an Israel Bond issue for a loan to the Israel government to be repaid in twelve to fifteen years. To make certain that the largest number of prospective investors might be reached, appeals on the High Holidays were inaugurated, Kol Nidre night being preferred because it is an occasion when Jews are more in a mood for complying with their moral obligations than at any other time. And so tonight Israel again turns to us to ask our help.

We know that the reaction of many of our worshippers, even of those who cooperated willingly before, may be one of apathy, if not actual belligerence. “What?”, they will ask. “Once more? When will this ever end?” And there are those who are beginning to agree with the Council for American Judaism and other foes of Zionism and Israel, who say: “We told you so. This venture of yours was pure romanticism, an unpractical scheme that could never work out. At all events, who told these people to go into all this? They should have left well enough alone. Now that they have their difficulties, let them work them out themselves. Why bother us?”

These questions might, however, be countered by another question, namely: “But what else could they do? Who would have given refuge to the 900,000 Jews that found an asylum in Israel since the establishment of the state? The United States, which limited its quota so drastically that under a revised plan five whole refugees were admitted into the country in one year? England, which has had to export hundreds of thousands of its citizens on account of its internal economic crisis? Australia, which barred its doors to Jews, or Russia which lets nobody in or out? Or should they perhaps have been content to submit to the benevolence of such magnanimous Arab rulers as Ibn Saud, in whose oil-rich country millions of Mohammedans are being permitted to starve? The only chance of survival for the Jews of the Yishub at the time of the expiration of the mandate, as well as for the later settlers, was the declaration of a state. They had to take the chance whatever the price or the consequences if they wished to go on living.

“But granted all this to be so, why should American Jews be concerned? It is the problem of the Israelis. It was they who needed and wanted a state, not we. Let them, therefore, do the best they can and leave us alone.” A very good argument, indeed. And yet, although we did not assume the obligation and did not as a group underwrite the state, the responsibility for helping maintain it is nevertheless ours. For if the Israel experiment, God forbid, were to fail, it would not be Israel alone that would suffer. Jewish prestige everywhere throughout the world would fall. Jewish honor, Jewish dignity would be bankrupt. And there would be no way of escaping the stigma. What could or would we do? Try to be gentiles for a while, like the wife of the Jewish agent of the Polish nobleman in the story I told before? Even that wouldn’t work. There were in Germany Jews who more than a century ago thought they could run away from their Jewish responsibilities by a formal ext from the community of Israel, when a Hitler came along, and, uncovering their family records, made them pay the price of their Jewish descent. To be sure, there must have been in Nazi Germany some persons with Jewish blood in their veins whom Hitler missed; and not everywhere are Hitler’s methods used. But what Jew, with any feeling of pride or self-respect, even if he could get away with it, would want to take this way out from his Jewish obligations?

The only alternative is to do our share, just as a loyal husband stands by his wife when she becomes desperately ill, whatever expense the cure of her illness may entail. And let us not make up our minds that investing in Israel Bonds is like throwing money to the wind. It is true that our State Department, which could with one directive, one stroke of the pen, have saved Israel millions of dollars that now have to be spent on its military installations and the maintenance of an army, is not easing the situation. Israel might net have required our assistance so desperately if Mr. Dulles had not been so hell-bent on wooing the Arabs, that need American good-will and gold more than America needs their oil. But we need not fear for the safety of our investment. Israel will not default. One reason for assurance on that score is that its government consists of people who have been reared in responsibility. They know that the statement “our obligations are not binding” apples only to agreements made with oneself not with others. Besides they are farsighted enough to realize that they would be jeopardizing their entire future by any breach of faith, As a matter of fact Israel has made enormous strides forward in the direction of economic self-sufficiency. It is raising a large proportion of the food it consumes. It is exporting in ever increasing amounts the products of its industry and the output of its mines, and transporting these goods in its own merchant marine. Every year sees it closer to its goal. But it will take time before it will be able to go its own way alone. In the interim we must hold out to it a helping hand. If we do, we will have a share in Israel’s triumph and reason to rejoice over its success.

Secondly, whether Israel lives up to our expectations or not, there is an ancient oath, that binds us, from which we have never been released. Nearly twenty-five centuries ago, the Jewish exiles sitting by the rivers of Babylon lifted up their right hands and swore. “If I forget thee, oh Jerusalem, may my right hand wither.”[^12] Faithfulness to that pledge made possible the establishment of the second Jewish commonwealth, and it has not lost its validity in spite of the nineteen centuries of subsequent exile. ‘ .

Thirdly, though men, who are weak and mortal, may because of their frailty and the brevity of their life, disappoint us, God, whose power is limitless and whose existence is everlasting, never does. Nezach Yisrael lo y’shakker. “The Glory of Israel will not he.”[^13] We have God’s word for it that He will “no more give Israel’s corn to be food for its enemies; nor shall strangers drink the wine for which it will have laboured; but they that have garnered it shall eat it, and praise the Lord, and they that have gathered it shall drink it in the courts of His sanctuary.”[^14]

Israel desires no alms, no gifts of charity, only a chance to earn its bread and enable its citizens to dwell each one beneath his own fig-tree and vine with none to make them afraid. In the words of Saul Raskin, they say every day.

> “God, I pray Thee, grant Thy people
> Just their daily bread,
> Not the bread of strife and friction,
> Not the bread of sad affliction,
> Tearless daily bread.
> Not the bread by slaves desired,
> Not the bread by shame acquired,
> Honest daily bread,
> That they may no longer gather
> Crumbs from wealthy tables, Father,
> Give their daily bread.
> God, I pray Thee, grant Thy people
> Shelter and a home;
> Not a home that swords acquire,
> Not a home of blood and fire,
> Just a peaceful home;
> That they may not ever wander,
> Torn and rent in parts asunder,
> Tramp the earth and roam,
> That their bond be never shattered,
> That they be no longer scattered,
> God, oh bring them home.”
