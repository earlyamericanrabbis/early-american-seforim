WHAT PRICE INTERMARRIAGE?

The problem of intermarriage is for Jews concerned about Jewry’s future as old as the Jewish race itself. It presented itself when the first child born of a Jewish father and mother was confronted with the task of choosing a mate; and it is just as burning a question today as it was when our forefather Abraham, nearly 4000 years ago, dispatched his trusted servant Eliezer to Aram Naharaim to select from his kinsfolk there a wife for his son and heir Isaac who had become of age.[^1]

Matchmaking was never a simple feat. So remarkable did it strike our sages that two human beings, who started out as total strangers, should cling to each other all their lives, that they attributed this miracle to none other than the Almighty Himself, and said: “It is the Holy One Blessed Be He that provides man with his mate.”[^2] A Roman matron once inquired of a Jewish savant what God had been busying himself with since He had completed the creation of the world. “Joining couples,” was his reply. “Is that all? That is easy. I can do it too,” said the enterprising woman. And she at once set out to team up the numerous maidservants employed in her household with her male domestics. On the morning after her experiment in copulation she found, to her consternation and dismay, the couples she had mated all beaten up and bruised. There wasn’t a man or a woman among the lot that had not sustained some injury.[^3]

No, finding a life-partner is not as simple a matter as fitting a garment. Had it been, our law-courts would not have been kept so busy putting asunder that “which the Lord hath joined together.[^4] The reason why divorce is so common a phenomenon in modern human society is that husbands and wives are not to be gotten made-to-order like suits or dresses, but have to be accepted ready-made. Herein lies the crux of the difficulty in match-making in general. And when a Jew marries, there is the added precaution, that must figure in his choice of a partner, of safe-guarding the way of life the propagation of which is his mission on earth. If Judaism is worth keeping, then it is worth perpetuating, and it won’t be perpetuated where one half of the household is for it and the other half is against it. “A house divided against itself? as Abraham Lincoln was wont to remind his audiences in his efforts to maintain the integrity of the United States of America, “cannot stand.”

This is, in fact, the philosophy underlying Judaism’s pronounced opposition to the intermarriage of Jews with members of other creeds. This was the reason why the Torah warned the Israelites against marital alliances with the Canaanites, as it is said: “Thy daughter thou shalt not give unto his son, nor shalt thou take his daughter unto thy son.”[^5] It was on this account that father Abraham took the trouble of sending all the way to Mesopotamia for a daughter-in-law, and that Isaac and Rebecca preferred to see their son Jacob leave home rather than have him marry one of the native daughters of Canaan,[^6] such as Esau had taken unto himself, and whose ways and manners were a “bitterness of the spirit”[^7] to his parents. The danger of alienation, as a result of such unions with strangers, was ever present, as it is plainly stated: “For he will turn thy son aside from following Me, that they may serve other gods.”[^8] Even a mighty and pious king like Solomon, the man who had built the Temple of Jerusalem to the honor and glory of the God of Israel, could not escape the negative influence of his foreign wives, so that in his old age he became an idolator.[^9]

Now the very fact that injunctions against intermarriage are found in the Bible and that rabbinic law does not recognize the validity of a union between a Jew and an unconverted non-Jew,[^10] itself implies that such unions were by no means infrequent, or the legislation against them would have been unnecessary. One doesn’t pass laws to cope with situations that are non-existent. And the truth of the matter is that not only in modern times, but in antiquity as well, instances of intermarriage between Jews and non-Jews were quite common. It is recorded that when Ezra, who has been called the second Moses, came from Babylonia to Jerusalem during the early days of the Second Temple, he felt compelled, in order to keep Judaism from disintegrating in the second Jewish commonwealth, to issue an edict enjoining the Jewish inhabitants of Palestine to divorce their non-Jewish wives.[^11] The operation left many festering sores, because it cut deep into the flesh of the most intimate family relationships. But why speak of what happened in the days of Ezra, when Moses, Israel’s lawgiver himself, married the daughter of a heathen priest?[^12] And what a heathen (Goy) he was, this Jethro, the father-in-law of Moses! There wasn’t, according to our sages, an idol invented by men that he had not served.[^13] And David, too, Israel’s ideal king, the founder of the Davidic dynasty of rulers and ancestor of the Messiah, he also is reputed to have had non-Jewish blood in his veins. For was he not the great-grandson of the Moabitess Ruth?[^14] And then there was Onkelos, the official translator of the Torah into Aramaic. The fact of his non-Jewish descent was indicated by the epithet “the proselyte”,[^15] that is always attached to his name. And the same applies to several others of the teachers of the Mishnah and the Talmud.[^16]

But if that is the case, why the alarm about the evil consequences of intermarriage’ If Moses and the great-grandfather of David and all the others, we have referred to, found satisfactory means of forestalling the fears and setting at nought the apprehensions entertained apropos of mixed marriages, why can’t we do the same? Why interfere with love? Why break the hearts of young people who feel that they are meant for each other and that they cannot live apart, when there exists a simple expedient for solving the problem of difference of belief? Do what was done in the case of Boaz and Ruth the Moabitess — have the non-Jewish partner converted to Judaism, and all is well. You have not only made two human beings happy, but also won a soul for Judaism.

That the Jewish religion looks with favor upon newcomers to the Jewish fold is evident from a number of sources, It is apparent not only from the warm welcome that was accorded to Ruth when she embraced the faith of her widowed Jewish mother-in-law Naomi, to whom “she was better than seven sons,”[^17] and from the place of honor assigned to her in the memory of the Jewish people. It is clearly indicated by the fact that in our chief prayer, the daily ‘Amidah, along with the “the righteous and the saintly and the elders of Israel and the remnant of its scribes”, for whom God’s mercy is invoked, there are listed also “the genuine proselytes.”[^18] “All those who sincerely trust in God,” whatever their descent or racial origin, are beleved to be deserving of His reward. And if someone were to cite in opposition to his point of view the remark of Rabbi Chelbo, recorded in the tractate of Kiddushin of the Babylonian Talmud,[^19] to the effect that converts are like a sore on the skin of Jewry that does not dissolve, this unfavorable attitude might be countered by a parable, the implication of which is the exact reverse. “Why,” asks one of our sages,[^20] “are the Levite, whose tribe represented the purest blood in Israel, and the proselyte, who is of non-Jewish extraction, mentioned in one breath?” Why should so much deference be shown to a total stranger? The reason for this is the same as that for the special attention accorded by a shepherd to a wild deer that had attached himself to his flock. When his regular sheep objected to the favoritism displayed by him toward the outsider, he replied: “Why shouldn’t I fondle him more than you’ That you should feel attached to me is only natural. I watched over you from the time you were born. I fed and cared for you. This deer, however, received none of these attentions. He grew up all by himself in the forest, and then he came to me of his own accord to join my flock. Must I not, therefore, demonstrate particular fondness toward him?” The native-born Jews are like the sheep in the parable. They should be beholden to God because God had done so much for them. He had taken them out of Egypt, fed them with manna and quail, quenched their thirst with the water of the well and protected them with clouds of glory. The gentile, who has himself converted to Judaism, comes without all these inducements. That is why he is accounted in God’s sight as highly as the Israelite and the Levite.

These arguments that might be marshalled to bolster the case for intermarriage are indeed most impressive and weighty, and they make it very difficult to comprehend Judaism’s strong opposition to it. Certainly if Jews are anxious to win the good-will of their gentile neighbors, who are everywhere, outside of Israel today, in the majority — and there are ever so many concessions to non-Jews that the Talmud recommends for the sake of maintaining amicable relations with them (mipp’ney darkey shalom)[^21] — they cannot hope to get it by snubbing them in refusing to marry their sons and daughters. It was precisely by means of this criterion that Napoleon Bonaparte sought to test the sincerity of the patriotism of the Jews of France of his time when he convoked the so-called “Sanhedrin.” “If you Jews consider yourselves Frenchmen and, by virtue thereof, entitled to the rights of citizenship in the French Republic on a plane of equality with the rest of the inhabitants of France, would you accept them as your equals by intermarrying them and would you recognize the validity of such marriages?”[^22] Of course the sole purpose of the wily emperor in subjecting the only recently enfranchised Jews of France to this test was to deprive them again of the boon of liberty that the French Revolution had conferred upon them. Whatever his intentions may have been, however, the religious leaders of French Jewry found themselves in a very serious predicament and the only way out for them at the time was to state that, while marriages between Jews and non-Jews would have to be regarded as legal, they could not receive the sanction of the Jewish religion, so long as the non-Jewish partner had not been converted to the Jewish faith. Once that detail was adjusted, no further objection could be raised. At all events the cause of good-will, so it would seem, could hardly be furthered by an out and out Jewish rejection of intermarriage with non-Jews.

What now? Where does one proceed from here? What justification is there left for Judaism’s persistence in its opposition to intermarriage? We are speaking now of Jews who take their Judaism seriously, who are not ready to abdicate, to capitulate from their ancestral creed. That its maintenance becomes difficult, if not virtually impossible, where, broadminded and tolerant of each other’s beliefs though a husband and wife may be, they adhere to two different religions, does not require demonstration. It is particularly when children are born, who have to be reared in the one or the other tradition, that conflict is bound to arise. At best the youngsters will be confused and bewildered by the contradictions between their parents. The alternative is often a split personality, leading to mental imbalance, delinquency and other such evils. Laissez-faire is, therefore, no solution. If Judaism is to be preserved, the non-Jew must be persuaded to embrace the Jewish way of life. That such an arrangement, even though it is unfair to the non-Jewish partner, is preferable to having a house divided, one that is half-Jewish and half-non-Jewish and, therefore, neither, cannot be denied. It worked very well in the case of Ruth, the Moabitess. Why not in others?

The trouble is that not all converts, who accept a faith other than that into which they were born and in which they were raised, merely in order to please a strong-willed spouse, are as dependable as Ruth was and can be trusted to persevere in their newly acquired religion. After all Judaism is not an easy faith to keep. Being a Jew is fraught in many parts of the world with serious disadvantages and grave handicaps. When, therefore, an individual was not born a Jew and would normally not have to put up with the burdens that persons of Jewish blood are compelled by society to bear, it is but natural that, when these burdens become too heavy, he should rebel against being tied down by them. That is what has, indeed, happened in the case of many non-Jewish wives of Jewish men in Germany under the rule of Hitler, even such as had been formally converted to Judaism. It is on this account that Jewish law frowns upon accepting as proselytes non-Jews, whose motive for conversion is the marriage of Jewish mates.[^23] Somehow or other it does not believe that such recruits would remain within the fold. It was converts of this type who were looked upon as an indigestible, unassimilable element, “a sore on the skin of the Jewish body politic,”[^24] a source of weakness rather than an asset to Judaism such as Ruth was, who had no such prospects in view when she declared to Naomi: “Thy people shall be my people and thy God my God.”[^25]

But let us consider even the ideal case, that in which the convert to Judaism becomes completely attached to the Jewish religion — and not infrequently it happens that these neophytes are more scrupulous in the observance of Jewish precepts than persons of Jewish parentage and descent. There is the story that is told of a Jewish young man, who, contrary to the wishes of his parents, married a gentile girl. Although they themselves were not too devout, this was more than they could bear and the only condition, on which they were willing to receive their new daughter-in-law into the family, was that she accept the Jewish religion. The young woman complied, went through all the formalities of conversion and became a Jewess in the full sense of the word, When Saturday came around and her husband, as had been his wont, was preparing to go to business, she put her foot down and stopped him. “I have been taught by the rabbi, who converted me, that Saturday is the Jewish day of rest when no work is allowed to be done. And if it is a day of rest for me, it is that for you also. From now on you are going to stay away from business on Saturdays” About an hour later the telephone rings. It was his father calling. “What’s the matter, Jake? Why aren’t you down at the place today?” “Mary won’t let me go,” replied the son. “She says Saturday is the Jewish day of rest and she insists on my observing it according to the requirement of Jewish law.” To which came the father’s rejoinder. “Didn’t I warn you not to marry a Shikse?” Even in such an ideal case, the solution is far from being a happy one, for no matter how the cloth is cut, someone must be hurt. After all, when a person marries, he does not marry one lone individual. He marries into a family, with its background and its traditions. Families mean a great deal to human beings who have standards and feelings of any sort. ‘No self-respecting individual can just pick up and disown and disavow his father, mother, sister and brother. Yet when a person changes his faith, especially when he makes a transition of so radical a nature as the one from Judaism to Christianity or vice versa, he immediately sets up a barrier between himself and his flesh and blood. According to Jewish law conversion severs theoretically speaking all legal family ties between the convert and his kindred.[^26] Christian parents must sense this as keenly, when a child of theirs turns Jewish, as Jewish parents do when their offspring becomes Christian — that 18, if they take their Christianity seriously.

All these problems, conflicts, antagonisms, bruises and hurts can be avoided if, instead of allowing our impulses to lead us, as happened in the case of Samson,[^27] the strong man, whose doom was brought about by the Philistine maiden whom he had married, we follow the dictates of common sense, which advises us to marry one of our own. The most common cause of marital unhappiness is, after all, incompatibility. When Ecclesiastes said. “And I find woman to be more bitter than death,”[^28] he had in mind an unequal match. For of him who has found a suitable mate our sages say that “he is kissed by Elijah and beloved by God.”[^29] To be sure it is impossible to know in advance whether the person one marries will be compatible or not. Sometimes all the precautions taken turn out to have been in vain. Nevertheless experience has shown that he is safest in this respect who marries within his own class and station, an individual accustomed to a similar mode of life as his own; and this applies particularly to such matters as religion. When husband and wife have the same religious background, there are fewer gaps to bridge or adjustments to make than otherwise, and the potential of friction is to that extent reduced.

But what about love? How can one be so cold-blooded about a matter that involves a human being’s life-long happiness, which is a question of sentiment rather than a commercial transaction? How can a man and a woman feel for each other as deeply as husband and wife should, if, before they decide to become bound by the most intimate of human ties, they inspect and measure each other as a farmer examines an ox at a cattle exchange?

Well, first of all, it may well be questioned whether that which often parades under the guise of love — 1 mean the romantic attraction for each other of young persons of the opposite sex that is denominated by the French as “l’amour” — is really love, whether in most cases it is not rather mere infatuation. For if it were genuine love and not its counterfeit — pure erotic emotion, unfulfilled desire — it would not wear off as soon as the urge was satisfied. Real love should wax stronger the longer the lovers live together, like the fibres of two trees that have become intertwined with each other and grown together. Secondly since when is it a virtue, in so important a matter, so intimate a relationship as marriage, to stumble into it blindly? Where is it proven that unless a person falls in love, he can’t be happy, or that love must precede rather than follow marriage. The reason, in fact, according to Madame Scuderi, why many matches go on the rocks is because those who contract them, instead of having their eyes wide open before they enter into matrimony and half-shut after they are married, go into marriage with their eyes closed and first open them wide when it is already too late. “Love,” it has been said, “overlooks all faults.”[^30] This overlooking should be done when a man and a woman are already bound up for better or for worse. That is the time when they ought to learn to love each other for the good qualities they possess, for all that they have in common, aside from the opportunity for sharing life and fulfilling their destiny. Before they enter into their partnership let them be as critical as they can be, so that they may avoid possible pitfalls. And the number one question they should ask each other is: “Do we see eye to eye in the matter of our religious faith? Are we sufficiently close to each other in our outlook in this regard as to be able to establish a well-integrated household?” Marriage is not for a day, or a week or month or even a year. It is intended to last a life-time, to strike the deepest roots and produce the widest ramifications. To be sure, happiness is not a treasure that can be seized by force or snatched at will. Under the most ideal conditions luck will continue to play its part. It will always remain a toss-up between “maza” and “moze”, between having attained the heart’s desire and bitter disappointment.[^31] But one can’t expect to achieve happiness by plunging headlong into almost certain disaster.

Intermarriage with adherents of other faiths, especially of Jews with non-Jews, by the very nature of the circumstances, rarely ever leads to complete domestic bliss, because it lacks that all-important, indispensable requisite for such a relationship, namely harmony. True, no two human beings think or behave exactly alike. Each person is a distinct and separate entity, different from the other.[^32] Yet beneath all the normal differences between husband and wife, there must be sufficient similarity and agreement between them if they are to be held together. Among these those of religious background take probably the first place.

He who judges by these standards is led inevitably to the conclusion that intermarriage is not worth the price. It is not necessary for Jews to intermarry with their gentile neighbors in order to win the latter’s esteem and respect. On the other hand, those that do, are usually almost certain to forfeit their Jewish birthright, while it is extremely doubtful whether they will attain the married happiness for the sake of which they have sacrificed this great privilege of having been born as Jews.
