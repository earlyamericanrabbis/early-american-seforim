TIME AND ETERNITY

The section of the Torah called “Wa’era” continues the account of the trials and tribulations of our ancestors in Egypt. Their leader Moses had made his first appeal to Pharaoh to release his Hebrew slaves from bondage and he had been rebuffed by the tyrant. The set-back he had received in his efforts to help his brethren made Moses not only angry but despondent as well. Why had God sent him on so useless a mission? What purpose was there in his meddling when all that he accomplished thereby was to worsen the situation of those whose lot he had hoped to be able to improve?

The answer to all these complaints filed against the Almighty, who had made him His messenger, was to tell Moses that He, the God who was speaking to him, was Adonai, that He had manifested Himself to Israel’s ancestors, Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, as the God Shaddai, but that He had not been known to them by the name Adonai.[^1] Also in spite of all appearances to the contrary, He was going to deliver the children of Israel from their servitude and bring them to the land which He had promised to their forefathers as their everlasting possession.

That a certain amount of importance was attached to the correct designation of divine beings is known from the fact that, after Jacob had wrestled with the angel on the night before his reunion with Esau, he asked for his adversary’s name. A similar request was made by Manoah, the father of Samson, from the emissary of God, who had announced Samson’s birth. This is all very well, but it still does not clear up the mystery of why the mere divulgence to Moses of Israel’s God’s real appellative should have set the Israelite leader’s troubled mind at ease. Rashi endeavors to explain it by pointing out the fact that the formula “I am Adonai” was often used as a guarantee of retribution. But again one might ask ‘What was there in this statement to assure such action?”

The answer to this question might supply the clue to the meaning of the tetragrammaton, the four Hebrew letters constituting God’s proper name, that was forbidden by the third commandment of the Decalogue to be taken in vain. The true significance of the combination of the consonants yod-he-waw-he, the correct vocalization of which has never been determined, is no better known today than it was two-thousand years ago. Some light is thrown on the possible derivation of the word by a statement contained in last week’s pentateuchal lesson. When Moses asked God at the burning bush what to reply in the event that the Israelites were to press him for the name of the God who had sent him to them, the answer he received was “I shall be who I shall be. Thus shalt thou say unto the children of Israel: ‘I shall be (Ehyeh)’ has sent me to you.”[^2] As Moses Maimonides observes in the 63rd chapter of the first part of his Guide of the Perplexed the purpose of this designation was to indicate God’s existence. God is the being that exists, the sole true reality in the world And not only does He exist now and has He been in existence before the universe came into being, not only is He “the master of the world who reigned ere any creature was created,”[^3] but “after all else will have come to an end, He will still rule in dreaded loneliness.”[^4] For as Moses was told “This name of Mine is (equivalent to) ‘forever’.”[^5] In short the best rendering of yod-he-waw-he in English is probably a word like “the Eternal”, because it calls attention to the enduring, everlasting nature of God, to His being beyond time and tide.

That this was probably Moses’ understanding of the term is borne out by the sublime passage in that one chapter of the psalter which bears the title of “a prayer of Moses, the man of God,” namely “Lord, Thou hast been our habitation in all generations Before the mountains were born or ever Thou gavest form to the earth and the world, even from everlasting to everlasting Thou art God.”[^6] Dwelling on this same fact of God’s eternity, the psalm goes on to say how “a thousand years are in God’s sight but as yesterday when it is past and like a single night-watch,” and that all of man’s years “end like a sigh.”[^7]

It is extremely important to bear in mind this attribute of the master of the universe, whom we call God, in order to be sure not to pass over another one of His divine qualities, namely His justice. When we look at the world from the limited viewpoint of a single human life-time, which extends over two or three generations at the utmost, we are apt to arrive at the conclusion that “there is “neither justice nor judge.”[^8] Everything is left to chance and fortune. No one cares for the underdog. Victory is only unto the strong, the cunning, the clever. “Whoever is mighty prevails.”[^9] This is, however, an illusion. Those who think that way are like him who sees a seed buried in the ground and then rot and fall apart, and, stopping at that point, concludes that the work of the planter has been entirely in vain. It is necessary to be on hand, when the plant begins to break through the ground and puts forth its blossoms and produces its fruits, to divine the purpose behind the cultivator’s toil. So, too, must we view the course of events in the world from beginning to end, to survey the process of the affairs of man in its entirety, in order to realize that there is order and direction in the universe, that the good that men do does not remain unrequited and that wickedness and vice are eventually accounted for, that reward and punishment are meted out by the Supreme Judge, “who dealeth out unto the virtuous according to his deed and giveth unto the wicked according to his wickedness.”[^10]

The Lord was King.

> The world about us did not spring
> From forces uncontrolled and free,
> And man is not caprice or whim
> Created without thought or form...

The Lord is King.

> His spirit ever hovering
> Above His world, above mankind,
> To guide the destiny of man
> Against a blind and helpless fate.

The Lord forever will be King.

> His moral rule awakening
> Man’s conscience ’gainst debasing vice
> That breeds disease and blinds the eye,
> And keeps men bowed in servitude.

Moses seemed to have forgotten these facts for a moment. He was impatient. He wanted immediate results from his efforts. “Life’s so short,’ he said to himself. “Show me Thy glory (now)! he besought God. That was why God had to remind him of His — God?s attribute of eternity. “The reason why you find fault with My management of the universe, oh Moses, and why you doubt My word, is because you regard the momentary situation, the temporary phase, which is only passing. Remember then that I am Adonai, the Eternal, He that was and is and will be. My eternity is the guarantee that all current wrongs will be righted. If you will only have a little patience you will see that it is so.” “Now wilt thou see what I shall do unto Pharaoh. For with a strong hand will he send them. And with a strong hand will he drive them out from his land.”[^11]

“Time,” said a modern sage, “is an herb that cures all diseases.” “It makes more converts,” remarked another savant, “than reason.” “It is the greatest remedy for anger,” declared a Roman philosopher. “The cradle of hope but the grave of ambition, time is the stern corrector of fools and the salutary counsellor of the wise, bringing all they dread to the one and all they desire to the other.”

No people in the world can bear more eloquent testimony to the validity of these truths than we Jews. In the course of our unbroken history of nearly four-thousand years as a distinct entity we have watched empires rise and fall. Our ancestors saw the Pharaoh, who had oppressed them, drown in a watery grave together with all his horses and chariots, while they, his erstwhile slaves went free. They witnessed the hanging of Haman, who had plotted their extermination. They recorded the conquest of the empire of Nebuchadnezzar, who had burnt their temple and led their leaders into Babylonian exile. They observed the disappearance of mighty Rome, that thought Judea would never rise again after her children had been drowned in their blood by Rome’s invincible legions. We, in our day, have noted the fate of the Czars, who had engineered the pogroms of Bialystok and Kishinev and Odessa, that were responsible for the migration of 2,000,000 Russian Jews to America. We have also seen what became of the arch-fiend Adolf Hitler, who had once boasted in his overweening conceit that his Reich would last a thousand years.

That is why, though we may be disturbed, we are not intimidated by the threats, not only of the Nassers, but ever of the Khrushchevs and the Bulganins, when they promise to put an end to Israel’s reborn sovereignty. “In every generation,” we say in the words of the Passover Haggadah “there are those who arise against us to destroy us. But the Holy One Blessed Be He (always) saves us from their hand,”[^12]

> “O time the beautifier of the dead,
> Adorer of the ruin, comforter
> And only healer when the heart hath bled —
> Time! the corrector when our judgments err,
> The test of truth, love — sole philosopher.”

Time proved that God was right and that Moses had been wrong. Because of our link with God, who is eternal, we believe that we are an eternal people. Let us then recognize time as our ally. By dint of patience and perseverance we will undoubtedly live to see the realization of our hopes and dreams. “As men lebt derlebt men.” “Happy is he that waiteth and attaineth.”[^13]
