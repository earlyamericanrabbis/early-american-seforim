“JERUSALEM, THY CITY”

Among the major festivals of Jewish tradition Rosh Hashanah figures as a purely religious rather than a national holiday. It is not bound up with a specific event in Jewish history like Passover or Shabuot. It was not the occasion of a pilgrimage to the Temple of Jerusalem as was the feast of Tabernacles following it two weeks later. It came to be known in the post-Talmudic era as one of “the days of awe”, of that solemn season of the year that was dedicated to the soul-searching and self-examination, which the individual Jew undertook of his own accord as a preliminary to his spiritual regeneration, to Teshubah, that is repentance or return to God. Nevertheless although the thought content of Rosh Hashanah has basically little to do with the Jewish national past or Jewry’s aspirations for the future, Israel as a national entity and Jerusalem, the capital of the Land of Israel, appear prominently in the synagogue liturgy of the holiday. We pray on this day that God may grant, among other things “glory to His people and joy to His land”, and also that “He may reign in lonely splendor on Mount Zion, His dwelling place and Jerusalem, His holy city.” The combination is, from the traditional Jewish standpoint, a perfectly natural one. Religion has from the very inception been a dominant element in Israel’s national civilization, and the Land of Israel, and particularly the city of Jerusalem, have always occupied a central position in Jewish religious thinking. They were one and inseparable, two facets of one and the same coin.

The consecration of Jerusalem is traced by Scripture to the very beginnings of Jewish history. Long before the tribes of Israel, under the generalship of Joshua, had taken possession of the Promised Land, Abraham, the ancestor of the Jewish race, brought his beloved son Isaac to the top of Mount Moriah, where Solomon later on built the Temple, in order to offer him up as a sacrifice to God, only to be told that the sacrifice was not necessary. Ever since that time Jerusalem held the first place in Israel’s affections. Carried away far from home by the army of Nebuchadnezzar after the collapse of the kingdom of Judah, the Judean captives in Babylonian exile took a solemn oath never to forget Jerusalem.[^1] Then, when several centuries later the Romans did to the second Temple what the Babylonians had done to the first, the longing for the holy city was kept alive in such prayers of the synagogue as “May our eyes behold Thy return unto Zion in mercy.”[^2] When on November 29, 1947 the United Nations voted in favor of the creation of an independent Jewish state on the soil of Palestine, Jerusalem was supposed to have been excluded from its territory and turned into an area under international control. Arab hostility and the impotence of the United Nations Organization in enforcing its own decisions compelled the Jewish defenders of the Holy City to take matters into their own hands, with the result that the greater portion of the new Jerusalem remained Jewish, and that not only that part of the municipal district itself but the approaches to it, too, became Jewish domain.

More lives were sacrificed in the defense of Jerusalem, which is connected with the rest of Israel by a narrow corridor, that looks like a wedge thrust into the heart of Jordan’s territory, than for the remainder of the Jewish state; and neither the pressure of the Vatican nor the combined efforts of the Arab nations, backed by their Asian sympathizers, will wrest it from Jewish control. In defiance of the opposition of the United States, England and France, the embassies of which are still located in Tel Aviv, Jerusalem was made the capital of Israel and all the ministries of its government were gradually moved into iit from their temporary headquarters in Hakirya. Why?’ The enigma is not to be resolved logically. It can be explained only psychologically.

Economically, for example, Jerusalem is no asset to Israel. Industry is difficult to establish there, not only on account of the high cost of transportation up the steep mountains but because of the shortage of water, which has always been the city’s number one problem Strategically it is a detriment, because as it is now situated it would be expensive as well as difficult to defend. The nature of Israel’s attachment to Jerusalem is not rational but rather sentimental. It is motivated by a sentiment such as that expressed by the author of the Song of Songs when he exclaimed “Many waters cannot quench love, neither can the floods drown it.”[^3]

Why do Jews love Jerusalem so much? What is there about this ancient city that causes it to exercise the fascination it has on the soul of the Jew? One can readily see how a God-intoxicated poet like Jehudah Halevi would be smitten with it. For him Jerusalem was the spiritual capital[^4] of the world, and it was in keeping with his character and outlook to rhapsodize: “Thou art the house of royalty, thou art the throne of the Lord.” “Would that I might be wandering where God was revealed unto thy seers and messengers.”[^5] But why should secularist non-believers of the type of a Ben-Gurion be exercised over it? On account of its symbolic significance for Jewish nationhood as the hereditary capital of Israel? National capitals have been known to move. Did not Peter the Great of Russia, when it suited him, transfer the seat of his government from Moscow to St. Petersburg? Did not the modern Moroccans exchange the ancient center of Moorish culture, Fez, for Rabat, where their Sultan now has his palace? The only explanation that can be offered for the nostalgia that even Jewish radicals of the Holy Land entertain for Jerusalem is the pulsation, notwithstanding all the evidence to the contrary, of a deep religious sentiment in their hearts.

Those charged with harboring so old-fashioned and irrational a feeling would be the first to deny such an allegation, which is also, of course, contradicted by the general impression made upon the visitor from abroad by the behavior of the rank and file, the masses of modern Israel and especially the native-born or Sabras. True, Saturday is the official weekly day of rest in Israel and its rabbinate is vested with juridical powers it does not possess anywhere else in the world with the possible exception of Morocco. There are also to be found in Jerusalem, B’neh B’rak and other localities groups that adhere scrupulously to the ancient traditions, not deviating by one iota from the law in its strictest interpretation. But they are the exception, not the rule. For the most part, the Sabbath, as it is observed by the majority of the Israelis is but a Jewish version of the Christian Sunday; and as for the jurisdiction of the rabbinical courts in matters of personal status, such as marriage, divorce and inheritance, which was a concession made to the religious parties in order to secure their support of the labor government — that may be of only temporary duration. On the whole those who come to the land, hallowed by centuries of veneration, looking for a religious spirit, that is lacking in the Diaspora, are apt to be disappointed.

Nevertheless before giving way to complete pessimism regarding the future of the Jewish religion in the country of its birth, one should inquire into the possible causes of the prevailing apathy towards the traditional practices and beliefs. May it not be due to the harrowing experiences through which many of the present inhabitants of Israel passed in the concentration camps of Europe? Is it not possible that the brutal murder of their loved ones before their own eyes turned them against God? Perhaps. But the explanation is too simple to be true. Jews have lived through such tragedies before, and at the moment in which they took place and the wound was fresh, they complained as the psalmist did in his distress: “My God, My God, why hast thou forsaken me?”[^6] A witness of the horrors of the Crusades even went so far as to utter so blasphemous an accusation as “Who is like unto Thee among the silent, oh Lord?”[^7] Yet after the storm was over, they would always go back to Him, who was their sole refuge in times of trouble, as Solomon ibn Gabirol had expressed it- “And if with rebukes Thou dost chastise me for my sin, I flee from Thee to Thee, and seek shelter from Thy fury in Thy shade.”[^8]

Can it be that the shaking off of the yoke of the Torah is the result of the heavy demands made by Jewish traditional law? Is it against the disciplines imposed by Judaism that the citizens of modern Israel are rebelling, the privation of convenience and comfort exacted by its restrictions? Such an assumption is not acceptable either. Certainly the Chanah Seneshes and all the brave lads and girls, who shed their life’s blood to win freedom for their people, or the pioneers, who exposed themselves to such dread diseases as malaria in order to drain the swamps of the homeland so that the generations of the future might enjoy good health and have bread to eat, were not wanting in the capacity for sacrifice, of that self-discipline which is expected of its adherents by the religion of Israel.

The true cause of the lack of enthusiasm of the average Israeli for the faith of his fathers first becomes apparent when one leaves Israel for a country like Italy. When, for example, one notes the wealth and magnificence of the edifices dedicated to the glory of God that fill its cities, one realizes what it is that might be lacking in Jewish Jerusalem. “Worship the Lord in the beauty of holiness,” said the psalmist.[^9] It was on that account that Solomon made his Temple the most resplendent building in his realm ‘“Beautiful landscape, the whole world’s gladness, city of a great king! God is known as a fortress in its palaces,”[^10] — such was the impression the Holy City made upon a contemporary. Oh how the hearts of the historian Josephus and the philosopher Philo swelled with pride when they spoke to their non-Jewish neighbors of Jewry’s national shrine as “the most beautiful and renowned temple, which is honored by the whole East and West.”[^11] Nothing like this, alas, is to be found in the modern Jerusalem. The city certainly has its natural beauty, and the panorama it presents from each of its four hills is inspiring, breathtaking. However the most striking building in its Jewish sector is not a Jewish house of worship but the Protestant Y.M.C.A. To its synagogues there still applies the lament of the liturgist: “The Holy City and its districts have fallen into shame and disrepute, and all its costly treasures are sunken into the earth and hidden.”[^12] How can Judaism be expected to inspire respect and reverence in Jewry’s latter day children, who have been reared in the radiance and splendor of the non-Jewish world, in the “beauty of Jepheth”,[^13] when so little attempt is made to render its symbols appealing to the aesthetic senses?

Of the Temple of old it is reported that “in God’s shrine everything bespeaketh glory.”[^14] That is certainly not the feeling one carries away from a visit to most of the houses dedicated to the worship of God in the land which is the cradle of the Jewish faith. Decorum is simply non-existent. Even when the Scriptures are being read the commotion, disorder and movement do not cease, and incomprehensibility of the sacred text cannot be offered as an excuse in a country, in which Hebrew has again become the language of the home and the market-place. Reforms are sorely needed. But the brand of Reform that has become fashionable in America is not the answer to Israel’s religious problem The reaction of Israeli intellectuals to the naive attempts of Diaspora innovators to attract them to their eviscerated form of worship is essentially that of Chaim Lieberman of the Daily Forward. “As far as I am concerned, I can get along without praying altogether. But if it comes to not praying, I would rather do it out of a complete prayer-book than an abbreviated one.”

What will ultimately emerge out of the present religious ferment in the birthplace of the Jewish religion — and a hunger for the word of God is definitely noticeable — is difficult to predict. Like many other things religion in Israel, which is still in the throes of creation, is unfinished business. Like the parched soil of the Negev the field of theology, that has long lain fallow in it, is in need of irrigation and fertilization before it will yield desirable fruits. In this process, just as in the material upbuilding of the country, Diaspora Jewry has an important, aye a vital part to play. The magnificence of Saint Peter’s, let it be remembered, is not due to the Italians alone, notwithstanding their high artistic endowment, for Italy is, so far as natural resources are concerned, a poor country. It is the Catholics of the world, especially those of America, that are the chief source of the Vatican’s wealth and the mainstay of the religious institutions of the Eternal City. This was true equally of Jerusalem at the time of its ancient glory. It was gifts flowing to the Temple treasury from all over the world that were the source of the city’s wealth and the cause of its splendor. The Talmud still remembers some of the methods that were employed to assure the prosperity of Israel’s religious capital. One reason, it recalls, for the requirement that Nets reva's, the yield of four-year-old trees, he consumed within its walls was to make certain that Jerusalem’s markets might be adorned by their display.[^15]

If Jerusalem is again to become a center of inspiration to Jews everywhere, it is up to its would-be beneficiaries to take a lively interest in its affairs and contribute with all means at their disposal to its beautification, as the prophet says‘ “For Zion’s sake I will not hold my peace, and for Jerusalem’s sake I will not rest... till He establish and till He make Jerusalem a praise in the earth.”[^16]
