THE TRUTH

> The portion called “Toledot” (offspring) deals with

the birth and childhood of the offspring of Isaac and Rebecca.

Of the twin sons that were born to the second patriarchal couple Scripture’s favorite was, as would be expected, Israel’s ancestor Jacob. And lest there be any doubt about it, Malachi, the last of the prophets, sets the record straight when he puts into the mouth of Israel’s God the words: “ ‘Was not Esau

Jacob’s brother?’ says the Lord. ‘Yet loved I Jacob and Esau

I hated.”<sup>1</sup> A human being who has his Maker’s approval — and Isaiah, who lived several centuries before Malachi, already ready speaks of ‘Jacob as God’s chosen one (Isaiah 41.8) — such a human being ought, so it would seem, to be endowed with virtues that would recommend him to Him who is perfection itself. Now one of the chief characteristics of God + is truth. “The seal of the Holy One Blessed Be He,” say the sages of the Talmud, “is truth.”<sup>2</sup> “God is not man that + he should lie,” remarked the Midyanite prophet Balaam.<sup>3</sup>

“The Glory of Israel,” said Samuel, “does not utter false- hood.”<sup>4</sup> Yet if Israel’s forefather Jacob were to be measured by this yardstick, if what is related about the methods employed by him to secure from his father the blessing intended for his brother were to be subjected to such a test, . he would be found wanting.

> The arguments advanced by the rabbinic commentators

in exoneration of Jacob’s conduct in this instance may all be perfectly valid. It may very well be, as they suggest, that

Jacob and not Esau was, from the standpoint of the time of his conception, the real first-born of his parents, and that, even if Esau was the older of the two, he had sold to Jacob all the privileges appertaining to his primogeniture which included the right to the blessing. Nor can it be claimed that the sale of the birthright had been fraudulent, that Jacob had simply taken advantage of his brother’s weakness for food, at a time when he was worn out and tired, to cheat him of his patrimony. The Bible testifies that the transfer was performed in broad daylight<sup>5</sup> It was a bona fide bargain, completely above board. No pressure had to be brought to bear on Esau to obtain his consent, because “Esau despised the birthright.”<sup>6</sup> All he cared for was to satisfy his immediate appetite.

Nevertheless, after all has been said, it can still not be denied that Jacob resorted to a subterfuge in order to secure the blessing from his father. That he was fully aware of the irregularity of what he was doing is clearly stated in the biblical text. When his mother Rebecca, who had hatched the plan to outwit the blind Isaac, bade Jacob to go through with it, Jacob’s first response was “Behold, my brother Esau is a hairy man while I am smooth-skinned. Peradventure my father will feel me, and I will be in his sight as a mocker.”<sup>7</sup> This was perhaps the only means of breaking the spell exercised by Esau over his father, who, as the Midrash points out,<sup>8</sup> had allowed his love for his elder son to cover up the latter’s faults. But two wrongs do not make one might. The fact that Isaac had been hoodwinked by Esau all his life, that he had been taken in by the pious mien which, according to the rabbinic expositors, the older of his twins had put on in order to win his fatherly affection, did not warrant Jacob to practice the same kind of deception. Scripture states clearly and unequivocally. “Beware of a mendacious word.”<sup>9</sup> It makes no difference what the circumstances may be in which a person finds himself. He must not tell an untruth. Jacob had defied this rule. And it was not Esau alone who pointed out this fact when he asked, playing on the meaning of the name ‘Ya’akob”. “Was he called Jacob because he twice circumvented me?” The prophet Jeremiah a thousand years later made use of the same pun “Every brother,” he remarked, “dealeth crookedly as Jacob (once) did.”<sup>10</sup>

The Bible’s portrayal of its hero Jacob presents a most difficult and perplexing moral dilemma. If being truthful at all times and under all conditions is the highest dictate of conscience, if only he is deserving, to use the phraseology of the psalmist, “to sojourn in God’s tabernacle and dwell on His holy mountain, who speaks the truth,” not only in public but “in his heart,’<sup>11</sup> then there would be nothing left but condemnation and censure of Jacob’s action toward his father and brother. This is the problem. What solution is there for it?

Before venturing an answer we should clarify in our minds what it is that we really understand by the term “truth”, and why we consider the speaking of the truth to be so all important for the happiness and wellbeing of humanity as a whole as well as of the individual. The most common definition of the connotation of the word “truth” is that it refers to conformity with reality, with things as they are, with the facts of existence, with life in its multiform facets in this vast, unending universe of which man is a part. The “truth” is the correct appraisal and understanding of all that the brain can encompass as well as of the infinity of ideas, relations and conditions that are beyond its comprehension and grasp. In short in its broadest sense the truth embraces the sum-total of whatever is knowable. It is obvious that no mortal, limited as he is in his capacity for the acquisition of knowledge, can aspire to such a goal. Only God is omniscient. Only He, from whom nought is hidden, to whom “things shrouded in darkness are revealed and who is Himself the dwelling place of light,”<sup>12</sup> knows everything. The best that man can hope for is the glimpse of a ray of the sun, the acquisition of a tiny segment of the whole, and that, too, only by developing a passion for knowledge. “God, give me the striving for the truth,” said one of the great literary figures of our day. “The truth itself keep Thou with Thee.” The requisites, according to a thinker like Saadia, for the most modest success in man’s journey of discovery of the truth, are constant self-criticism, a careful testing of the sources of knowledge and of the reliability of the methods whereby he arrives at his conclusions, accuracy in collecting the facts whereon he bases his theories and guarding against the deception of the senses.

More than ever is it realized today how imperfect is the instrument with which man does his thinking and how mistaken even the wisest is apt to be in his views. It was because of its rejection of the belief in the infallibility of any human being that science was able to make the astounding progress it did during the past two centuries, and that more has been unravelled during that short period about the nature and the behavior of the physical universe than in all preceding ages. “Truth,” it has been remarked “is the ground of science.” “Exactitude is the first rule that must be observed by every technician.”” To make sure that an edifice built will stand up, the foundation upon which it rests must be solid, secure, or the entire structure will collapse. By checking and checking again it is possible not only to guard against error but also to correct the mistakes of the past and, by means of this revision, go forward.

Because man’s acquaintance with the truth is not static, standing still at one place, but dynamic, owing to the fact that scientific theories change from time to time as man grows older and wiser, the impression is created that the truth, too, is variable. But that is not so at all. Just as God, whose body, to use a Platonic figure of speech, is truth, remains forever the same — “I the Lord have not changed”<sup>13</sup> — so the truth is imperturbable.

> “Marble and recording brass decay
> And, like the ‘graver’s memory, pass away;...
> But truth divine forever stands secure,
> Its head is guarded as its base is sure.”

It is this quality of timelessness that makes truth the most reliable guide to follow not only in the pursuit of knowledge but in all human relations. “The lie has no feet” says a popular proverb. A government that is founded on a tissue of lies cannot long endure. Sooner or later the falsehoods it has fed its subjects are exposed and it founders like a house of cards.

> “While error, wounded, writhes with pain,
> Truth crushed to earth shall rise again.”

Why, then, do human beings insist on telling lies? The foremost reason, perhaps, is that the truth is often uncomplimentary, not at all flattering. When Johnny came home one day all bruised as a result of a fight he had had, in defiance of his mother’s warnings, and his mother said to him: “Where did you get this black eye? I want an explanation and I want the truth,” the bad little boy countered: “Must it be both?” Also like pure gold the truth is not easy to circulate. Men have learned that it is far more convenient to adulterate the truth than to refine themselves. Nevertheless in the long run the truth turns out to be a better excuse than the finest fabrication. To bolster one lie it is necessary to think up many others, while the truth stands on its own feet all the time. This is not to be taken as a justification for throwing the truth into men’s faces regardless of the circumstances. Just as the average eye cannot look at the naked light of the sun without being blinded, so the truth presented bluntly and suddenly may produce shock. That is why it is not only unkind and brutal but even dangerous for a physician to tell a patient the worst about his illness, when he knows that the ailing person cannot take it and that the only result of the revelation would be to aggravate his condition. Yes, even so wonder-working a medicine as the truth must be administered wisely, gradually, with circumspection and sufficient sugar-coating to render it palatable.

In the end, of course, there is no avoiding it. Man must face “the truth, the whole truth and nothing but the truth ” The implication of this commonplace of the courtroom is that the truth is never an isolated fact, standing by itself. It is rather a series of facts closely connected with each other, and to take any one of these facts out of its context and divorce it from the rest would result in a distortion of the truth. It was such a distortion committed by the German diplomat Von Moltke that precipitated the Franco-Prussian war of 1870. An incomplete quotation from a telegram created the impression of hostile intent on the part of France and served as a pretext for an invasion of French territory by the German army. Who knows how many other such crises may have arisen from similar causes?

That the truth is by no means clear-cut and simple is not difficult to demonstrate. Take a matter like bloodshed, for example. It is abhorred because it negates the purpose of the Creator in bringing forth life. That is why Scripture says: “Whoso sheddeth the blood of man by man shall his blood be shed.<sup>14</sup> Nevertheless not every person taking another’s life is branded as a murderer. Only he that does it maliciously and without cause is condemned for having committed a crime. He, however, who acts in self-defense must be exonerated. By the same token would it be wrong to view the individual border incidents in the Middle East in isolation and mete out censure or impose sanctions accordingly. Only if the acts of retaliation are considered in the light of what preceded them can the blame be fixed and the proper remedy or means of prevention be prescribed.
