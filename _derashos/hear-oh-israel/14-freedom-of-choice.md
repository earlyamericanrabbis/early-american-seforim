FREEDOM OF CHOICE

The opening sentence of chapter 10 of the book of Exodus poses a problem that has never been satisfactorily resolved. In the conception of the Bible — and that is the accepted view of Judaism — man is rewarded or punished in accordance with his deeds. The Almighty, according to the Mishnah,[^1] follows in His dealings with him the rule of measure for measure. The justification of the application of this treatment lies in the assumption that man’s choice of good or evil is entirely his own, that no one, not even his Creator, influences him in his decision regarding the road he is to travel. Man may not be “the master of his fate,” in spite of the poet’s declaration that he is, for heaven certainly has something to do with determining that. Yet “he is the captain of his soul.” “Whithersoever a human being wishes to go,” says the Talmud,[^2] “thither is he led.” Only under such conditions is it fair to make man pay for his mistakes, to decree that as he sows, so shall he reap.

The assumption, which is basic to the whole moral structure of what is believed to be God’s world, to the idea that the government by which the universe is ruled is founded on justice, that is on the principle that no being must be held accountable for what is not its own doing, this assumption seems to be completely negated by what Scripture relates about God’s behavior toward Pharaoh, the oppressor of the Israelites in the days of Moses. Unquestionably the Egyptian tyrant deserved the lesson he was taught. He was guilty not only of unspeakable cruelties toward his Hebrew slaves. By oppressing the Israelites he left himself wide open also to the charge of rebellion against God. For since God is the champion of the downtrodden and oppressed, whoever touches them sins against Him, who is their patron and protector. But that this God Himself should have hardened the heart of Pharaoh, that He should have spurred the despot on to still further increase the burdens of his despotic rule, so that he might be struck the harder when the time for an accounting would come — that was manifestly not “cricket” as the English would say. Either you let a man alone, so that, whatever the consequences of his deeds might be, he and he alone would have to shoulder them, or, if you choose to take a hand in the direction of his affairs, then accept a share of the blame if aught goes wrong. You cannot have it both ways. You can’t put him up to mischief and then make him suffer for it. That’s not playing the game fairly. Such would be our first reaction to the order given to Moses: “Come unto Pharaoh, for I have hardened his heart and the heart of his servants, in order that I might show these my signs in their midst.”[^3]

The difficulty could, of course, be parried by pointing out that God’s ways were different from those of man. “For your thoughts are not my thoughts nor are your ways my ways.”[^4] In executing judgment upon those deserving to be punished the procedure of the divine king would necessarily differ from that which is customary in human courts. When an individual who has committed a wrong is hailed before an earthly judge, he may, if it is his first offense, be released with a reprimand, reinforced by an admonition to mend his ways so that what has happened may not occur again. Then, if repeated warnings fail to check his anti-socal tendencies, an officer of the law is sent to arrest him and he is made to expiate for his crime by whatever penalty the statutes of the state may prescribe in the case, whether it be solitary confinement or some more drastic punishment. Divine justice does not operate exactly in this manner. Not that it does not give the offender a premonition or foretaste of the sort of chastisement he might expect if he persists in his insubordination. Pharaoh, the king of Egypt, received a number of such reminders before the full fury of the Lord was unleashed upon him. However when he, that has transgressed against God’s commands, refuses to heed His warnings, he is not simply shipped off to jail, nor is he seized by force to be decapitated by a guillotine or hung from the gallows. No, he is merely allowed to continue on the road he has chosen till he reaches its inevitable end, namely that of his total destruction. To use a colloquialism, “he is given enough rope to hang himself.”

That is what happened to Pharaoh. He overreached himself. So greedy was he for the retention of his Hebrew slaves, that in his unbridled rapacity he pursued them into the sea, where he found a watery grave. Overextension was also the chief cause of the downfall and undoing of the greatest oppressor of Jewry in our time, Adolf Hitler. So great was the lust for power of this megalomaniac of our day, that he did not content himself with the possession of Austria and Czechoslovakia, which he would cheerfully have been permitted to keep. With every conquest he had made, his appetite grew, till he tried to gobble up the entire world, and, as might have been foreseen, he choked in the attempt.

Thus does Providence avenge itself on those that oppose its rule. Such is the method employed by the divine judge to right the wrongs of human society. All this our preachers of old saw implied in the very wording of the biblical text.[^5] In connection with the first five plagues visited upon the Egyptians, the expression used by Scripture to characterize Pharaoh’s obstinacy is “and Pharaoh’s heart remained stubborn.”[^6] Only after these initial warnings is it stated that “God had hardened the heart of Pharaoh.”[^7] The reason, according to the Midrash, was “that when God noted that Pharaoh had failed to repent in spite of the first five plagues, He said ‘From now on, even if he should be inclined to repent, I will harden his heart so that I may exact punishment from him for all?”

This disposes of one question, the question of why God Himself had hardened the heart of Pharaoh, notwithstanding His eagerness to have the tyrant let Israel go free. But that still does not remove the major difficulty that the biblical narrative presents. Scripture, for example, makes no secret of the fact that God foresaw right from the very beginning every move that Pharaoh was destined to make. When He first sent Moses on his mission, He told the latter in no uncertain terms’ “Do thou speak whatever I shall command thee, and let thy brother Aaron speak to Pharaoh to release the children of Israel from his land. But I shall harden the heart of Pharaoh...so that Pharaoh will not listen to you.”[^8] But if this was the case, wasn’t poor Pharaoh a mere puppet in God’s hand? Why then punish him for something he could not prevent from coming to pass? To say that he could have acted otherwise would be putting a limitation on God’s omniscience. It would be equivalent to admitting that God was not aware of everything His creatures might decide to do, which is unacceptable from the standpoint of Jewish theology, according to which nothing that takes place or is destined to transpire in the world is hidden from the Lord’s ken. “He knoweth what is in the darkness, and the light dwelleth with him.”[^9]

In whichever direction one turns, then, one stumbles up against difficulty. If, as Rabbi Akiba would have it, “all is foreseen (by God),”[^10] then man is not completely free to choose, for he must perforce choose that which God knows he will choose. If, on the other hand “choice is granted (to man)”, then that choice must not be hamstrung by God’s advance information of what would be. This paradox is one that all Jewish thinkers, from Saadia Gaon to Spinoza and including Moses Maimonides, have grappled with without real success. All that the great Gaon of Sura, the author of the first systematic interpretation of Judaism, was able to contribute to the resolution of the contradiction was to suggest that only the final outcome was embraced in God’s foreknowledge, not the intermediate stages. This explanation is not at all satisfactory, because it presupposes a restriction on God’s omniscience. Maimonides’ attempt to cut the Gordian knot by asserting that God’s knowledge was different in its nature from that of man merely begs the question. It is equivalent to saying: “I really don’t know how to solve this riddle.” Spinoza, in order to overcome the difficulty, defines freedom as living in accordance with the laws of nature which isn’t what is usually understood by freedom in the literal meaning of the word. Perhaps the honest thing to do is to let the matter rest as it is, to say, as the Talmud frequently does when it comes to an impasse. “Teko!” “Let the question stand!” That was probably what Rabbi Akiba meant by stating the paradox in its contradictory form: “Though all is foreseen (by God), yet is choice granted (to man”. For unless man possess full freedom of choice, he could not be a moral agent subject to reward or punishment. One cannot sentence a beast of prey for murder, when killing is the purpose for which it has been created. Nor — supposing that there are such beings — do angels deserve to be commended for being chaste and not committing sins of the flesh, because they have no bodies the appetites of which have to be gratified. Only creatures having the power and ability to choose between two alternatives are fit to be judged.

Where does this discussion leave us? It leads us to the recognition that there are mysteries that life presents which may never be cleared up. No one will ever be able to demonstrate conclusively how animation first appeared on our planet, if, as it is believed, it started out by being composed of completely lifeless, inorganic matter. Nor is it possible to describe the birth of intelligence in the mind of an infant, nor to show by what process the ability to think and reason was acquired by the human species, which in all other respects resembles the rest of the animal kingdom. The claim made recently by archeologists that they had discovered somewhere in Africa the missing link between the speechless, unreasoning ape and articulate homo sapiens will still have to be reinforced by some tall explaining before the sceptics will be convinced of sts validity.

In fine, just like the question of how what man does can be charged against him or accounted to his credit, so long as all his thoughts and plans are known to God, there are many other enigmas that may never be solved by man. And yet life must go on. Instead, therefore, of racking our brains to find answers that may never be forthcoming, we adopt certain hypotheses upon which we base our program of living. Among these is the belief that the normal human being is completely responsible for his behavior, because the course he follows is of his own choosing At the same time, however, we affirm with equal certainty that there exists a God “who tests the reins and heart of men”[^11] and “having created them, gives heed to all their works.”[^12]
