“CLOUDS OF GLORY”

The solemn season of the high holidays with their inspiring message, that lifts the Jew heavenward, is followed by Sukkot, the festival of the fruit harvest, with its appeal to the senses, that bring him down to earth again. When the Torah tells its adherents on the feast of Tabernacles: “And thou shalt rejoice on thy festival,”[^1] it does not have in mind pure spiritual joy, such as a philosopher’s gladness or the delight of an artist in his handiwork, the type that was attributed to the Divine Architect, who, upon completion of His world, proclaimed all that He had made to be “very good.”[^2] No, the reference of the injunction “Thou shalt rejoice on thy festival” is to acts as physical and sensual as eating and drinking and relaxing. Nevertheless even on Sukkot, when the emphasis is on things as material as food and drink, when the very roof of the hut, that shields him that sits in it, from the glaring light and the burning rays of the sun, must be made of “the growth of the soil,”[^3] even when the Jew reminds himself to what extent all this is bound up with and dependent on forces that transcend nature. These forces have nothing to do with wind and weather, with sensations like hunger and thirst and such functions of the body as alimentation and digestion.

This fact is called to his attention by the Sukkah in which he is commanded to make his abode for an entire week. The purpose, according to the Torah, of his deliberate exile from his comfortable home at the height of the harvest, when he “gathers in his crop from the field,”[^4] is to recall to him the situation of his ancestors, who, in the course of their wanderings through the wilderness were made to take refuge by the God, who had delivered them from the bondage of Egypt, in just such shelters as these. “In order that your generations may know that I caused the children of Israel to dwell in booths when I took them out of the land of Egypt.”[^5] But where, outside of this one notice in the book of Leviticus, is it as much as hinted at in Scripture that the Israelites built huts for themselves during the time of their sojourn in the desert? The only type of dwelling made mention of is the tent. It was to their tents that Moses ordered the Israelites to return after the revelation at Mount Sinai.[^6] “How beautiful are thy tents, oh Jacob” was the expression used by the heathen prophet Balaam in praising Israel.[^7] The tabernacle, Israel’s first sanctuary was a portable tent.[^8] Besides where would they have found lumber for their booths in the waterless waste where nothing grew? Rashi, therefore, following the Aramaic translation of Onkelos, which coincides with the view of Rabbi Eliezer,[^9] or, if you will, Rabbi Akiba,[^10] explains that by the word Sukkot in our passage were meant the clouds of glory.[^11] These clouds accompanied the children of Israel on their journey and served as a protection against enemies, as indeed they are said to have done when the Egyptians pursued their erstwhile Hebrew slaves at the sea.[^12] It was also thanks to this protective covering that, according to our sages,[^13] most of the Israelites, that is all except a few stragglers, escaped the onslaught of the Amalekites, who attacked them on the way.[^14]

At all events Israel’s preservation was not due to the possession of those material resources upon which men generally place their reliance and pin their hopes. On the contrary, its survival defied all ordinary human calculations and recognized rules of existence. If the Israelites came through the harrowing experience of their forty years’ journey in the desolation it was because of God’s solicitude and interest to keep them alive. “And He afflicted thee,” so the book of Deuteronomy puts it, “and He made thee suffer hunger and He caused thee to eat the Manna, in order to let thee know that not by bread alone doth man live but on whatever issueth from the mouth of the Lord doth he subsist.”[^15] That is why just at the time, when he would be most tempted to revel in his material prosperity and make boast of his niches and of the security it gives him, the Jew is required to move from his sturdy, well-built home into the frail Sukkah, that is completely at the mercy of the elements and the safety of which is dependent solely on the will of the Almighty, concerning whom the psalmist has said. “His faithfulness is a shield and a buckler.”[^16]

The commandment of the Sukkah has been called by our sages a mizwah kallah"[^17] an inexpensive mite, easy to fulfill, and indeed, compared to other obligations imposed upon the Jew, it is that. Over against the cost of observing the prohibition of chamez on Passover by the elimination from the menu of all edibles that are not guaranteed to be kasher for Pesach, that of constructing a Sukkah is virtually negligible. However high the price of etrogim may be — and the harder they are to obtain, the steeper the mark-up — about the Sukkah there is never any worry. It does not have to be made of the best materials. Any piece of wood lying around will do. It does not require four complete walls.[^18] Two partitions and the outline of a doorway are sufficient to satisfy the law. For a roof anything that is not a receptacle but has sprung from the soil may be used.[^19] Furthermore, if it should rain even men are excused from eating in the Sukkah,[^20] while women and children are at all times exempted.[^21]

Notwithstanding all these lenencies, and even though the precept of the Sukkah is so seemingly easy to observe, demanding neither undue exertion, nor expense nor sacrifice, or perhaps for this very reason, did it become the criterion of trust in God, of dependence on His bounty and care. For just as the things that are most vital to man’s existence, such as the air he breathes and the water with which he quenches his thirst and the sunshine on which he feasts his eyes, are free or nearly so, so does faith cost very little. Yet it is precisely here that men so often fail to prove themselves.

The Talmud tells us[^22] that on judgment day, when the nations of the world will be called to task by God for having neglected His Torah, they will offer as an excuse for their failure herein the fact that this God’s law had not been urged upon them as it had been on Israel. “Did you hold Mount Sina: suspended over our heads like a tub threatening to bury us underneath, if we refused your gift, as you did in the case of the Israelites? Had you acted with us in that manner, we might have accepted your Torah as they did.” Since God is never unfair with His creatures, the story continues, He will say to them. “Very well. I shall give you another chance to redeem yourselves. I have a simple precept. Sukkah is its name. If you will fulfill it, I will account it unto you as though you had carried out all My commandments.” Thereupon each of them will get busy building his booth. However on the first hot day they will forget all their good intentions and pull down again what they had built up.

What did this parable intend to point out? The answer is not difficult to figure out. The Sukkah is a symbol of peace. Do we not beseech God on every Sabbath and holiday eve “to spread over us His tabernacle of peace”?[^23] Though peace is the container of all other blessings[^24] — for without peace neither health, nor wealth, nor knowledge are of much worth to man — it is relatively inexpensive. When the world is at peace, when amicable relations exist among the nations, there is no need for vast armaments and large armies. Mutual confidence, justice and fairness and regard for one another’s rights cost very little. Yet men find it hard to muster such qualities. At the first provocation, when the strength of their professions is put to the test, they pull down their Sukkah, the delicate edifice of goodwill they had erected. Rather than exercise self-control, which is not expensive, they go to the expense and trouble, in order to maintain their honor and prestige, of preparing for war. Placing their reliance solely on “their strength and the might of their hands”,[^25] they arm themselves to the teeth, forgetting that no armor is so impenetrable that it cannot be pierced and that there has never been created a force material or physical that has not found its match in another that overpowered and defeated it. They overlook what Job already knew, namely that even Leviathan’s scales are vulnerable,[^26] that war never solved any problem and that the greatest victories were only preparations for defeats later on.

If only the nations that have been blessed by the Almighty with the most abundant material resources would recognize this truth, they would realize how much their retention of these means depended on the thoughts that others had of them, on the sentiments towards them of their fellowmen, on the state of mind of their neighbors rather than on the perfection of their own war machines and the strength of their military defenses. Instead of squandering their wealth on the piling up of expensive armaments, that profit neither them nor others, they would use it — and it would require but a fraction of the cost of armaments — to build up international trust and goodwill.

That is the lesson of the Sukkah. “Better a handful of peace,” said Ecclesiastes[^27] “than two hands full of trouble and pursuit of the wind.” Our happiness is more dependent on such intangibles as peace, understanding and benevolence than on things as concrete as money and estates, over which we may exercise control for a while but which are never securely ours without the intangible forces that safeguard them for us. Instead of spending all our energies in acquiring the former we should do well in investing a fraction thereof to secure the latter.
