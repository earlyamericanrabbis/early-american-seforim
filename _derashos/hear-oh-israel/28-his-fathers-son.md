HIS FATHER’S SON

The pentateuchal lessons of the New Year’s Day are concerned in the main with an account of the birth and childhood of Isaac, the son of the first Hebrew patriarchal couple, Abraham and Sarah. We read how Isaac was born to his parents long after they had passed the age of child bearing, and how, as he began to grow up, his mother Sarah found his half-brother Ishmael, the son of the bond-woman Hagar, whom she had herself given to her husband for a wife, misbehaving, she insisted upon the older boy’s banishment in order to make certain that he would not share in the inheritance of his father’s estate, together with her Isaac. Finally, we are told about a divine command that came to Abraham, to offer up his beloved son, who now remained the only heir of his worldly and spiritual fortunes, as a sacrifice — an order that the patriarch was about to execute when an angel stayed his hand and a ram was substituted for the lad.

Now we know that the Bible is not primarily a text book of history the purpose of which is information. The chief aim of at least five books of Moses, to be true to their collective title of “Torah”, is instruction, religious and moral guidance. Scripture, therefore, does not present all that is known. It is selective in its material, choosing from among the experiences of man only those that bear out the points it wishes to make. What, therefore, were the reasons that prompted the listing of the details that we have mentioned? Why does Scripture disclose the particular facts it brings to our attention about the birth and childhood of Isaac?

In order to understand the motivation underlying this portion of the biblical narrative, it is necessary for us to make clear to ourselves what it is that makes the birth of children so desirable. Why does the average human being long for offspring? Really, there is no one answer to this question but rather a whole variety of reasons. There are some persons who are supplied by children with an interest in life, something to look forward to and work for. Others, like the miser in George Eliot’s famous novel “Silas Marner”, endowed with an abundance of fatherly or motherly affection, see in them beings to cherish and fondle. To still others they constitute a sort of insurance against the trials of old age, someone to lean upon in the evening of life when strength faileth. Yet it seems that the most important reason is that which is implied in a statement attributed by the Bible to father Abraham. When God commended him for his conduct and assured him “Thy reward is very great”, the patriarch replied, “Of what avail is all Thou givest me, Lord God, so long as I am childless and whatever I possess will, when I am gone, fall into the hands of a complete stranger, my major domo, Eliezer?”[^1]

In other words, it is the will to live on, to continue existence on earth through one’s own flesh and blood that is the chief incentive for the longing for offspring. Men see in their children an extension of themselves. If our sages asserted that our forefather Jacob had never died, it was because so long as his descendants were alive he too could be considered as living.[^2] It was the absence of a natural heir, of a successor sprung from himself, that induced father Abraham to accept Sarah’s offer of a union with her Egyptian maid, which resulted in the birth of Ishmael. But after Abraham had Ishmael, what need was there for Isaac? Did not the former satisfy the requirements of succession as well as the latter? Was he not flesh of his father’s flesh and bone of his bone? Why was it that Isaac alone was to be called Abraham’s descendant’? The answer, as the rabbinic commentators point out, was that though he had been begotten by his father Abraham, Ishmael was not qualified spiritually to be his successor and heir on account of the great difference in character that existed between him and his progenitor.[^3] Abraham was a person of the highest principles and the loftiest moral stature, generous, serious, possessing a deep sense of responsibility.[^4] Ishmael, on the other hand, was a wild man of the desert to whom nothing was sacred. “His hand was against everybody and everybody’s hand was against him.”[^5] Considering the way he conducted himself, he might have been a complete stranger, not a lineal descendant of the dignified patriarch, one capable of carrying on the noble traditions of the patriarchal household. It was necessary that Abraham wait for the birth of an Isaac, unlikely though it seemed that such a one would make his appearance, in order that the perpetuation of these traditions might be assured and Abraham’s ideals be securely established. Isaac was the answer to Abraham’s prayers, the fulfillment of his hopes and dreams. He bore so striking a resemblance to his father that whoever saw them together was forced to admit that “Abraham had begotten Isaac.”[^6]

But why, after he had finally granted Abraham the reward he had asked for — “the inheritance of the Lord is children, the reward, the fruit of the body”[^7] ” — did God show himself an Indian giver by asking Abraham to offer this same Isaac as a sacrifice on the altar? Was there no other way of testing Abraham’s loyalty to his heavenly master? Modern Bible students explain that the object of the story of the binding of Isaac was to lodge a protest against the cruel practice of human sacrifice that was prevalent among many civilized peoples of antiquity. Those who are acquainted with Greek literature will recall the sacrifice by the Mycenean king and Homeric hero, Agamemnon, of his daughter, Iphigenia, in order to appease the anger of the gods. The Hebrew Scriptures, too, warn Israel repeatedly against the abominations of the ancient Canaanites, who used to cast their children into the fiery bowels of the idol Moloch.[^8] The prophet Micha asks, “Shall I give my first born for my transgression, the fruit of my body for the sin of my soul?”[^9] By making father Abraham go through all the stages of the preparation for the sacrifice and then, as he was about to put the finishing touches, staying his hand, it was indicated that God took no pleasure in such offerings.

But could not the same lesson have been taught without making Abraham go to all this trouble? Perhaps it could, but then Abraham would never have known that it is possible to lose one’s children by other means than by directly delivering them up unto death, that a person may forfeit that which he hopes and expects from his offsprings, in whom he sees his future, the extension of his personality, by too much softness and leniency, by giving in to every whim and caprice. True, children have to live their own lives and there are no means that their parents have of controlling them after they have closed their eyes forever. But unless those, who are sprung from them, to some extent carry on what was so dear to their progenitors, what have the latter accomplished in begetting them? They may as well have been other people’s children. Abraham had made his mistake with Ishmael. He had lost him by being too easy-going with him. He was not going to repeat this error with Isaac. Together with his father, this favorite child of his was to go through all the rigors of the sacrifice. By Isaac’s doing that, the possibility of the loss of an heir and successor to Abraham was averted. Isaac had shown himself eligible for wearing the mantle of his father. He was worthy of the office and honor of succession and he had proven that he was his father’s son, that “Abraham had begotten Isaac.”

There is in this Bible story a most significant message for us in our day. We, who cling to the synagogue and its teachings, do so because we are anxious to preserve our religious heritage. We wish to preserve this heritage because we regard it as being supremely worthwhile. However, we live in an era of revolutionary changes, of radical transformations, of continual alterations that militate against our desire to perpetuate what we have inherited from the past. The ones chiefly affected by these forces of change are our children, the youth. We of the older generation still feel ourselves strong enough to withstand the onslaught. We are not so dazzled by the new things we see as to be induced to part with our cherished traditions. The younger generation, however, to a large extent, does not take this position. With them it is a case of either going along with the tide or nothing at all. Now we love our children and one thing we wish to avoid at all costs is to be seperated from them. What, then, must we do in order not to lose them?

The easiest way, of course, would be to follow the path of least resistance, for the fathers and the mothers who have been accustomed to the disciplines imposed by Jewish tradition to give up what to them was sacred just in order to humor the children. Instead of inculcating in them compliance with the commandment of “Honor thy father and thy mother”,[^10] the process would have to be reversed and the doctrines of “Honor thy children” be substituted for it. Of course, they, the children, know everything. They are the paragons of wisdom, of culture, of learning. Why should they concern themselves with the views of their elders, those incorrigible reactionaries who cling to the outmoded prejudices and the exploded misconceptions of another age? And so many of our older generation, the so-called ignorant fathers and mothers, meekly bow their heads to the superior wisdom of their children and slowly but surely they see their Jewish heritage slipping away, becoming more and more diluted until it is so completely changed from the orginal as no longer to be recognizable.

To be sure it is a smart thing to be modern, to keep up with the times, to make use of the most improved methods of doing things, not to trail behind. But then we should at least be discriminating and find out what is modern and what is not. Many of the supposed innovations for which the traditional theories and practices of Judaism must make way cannot even make a claim to modernity. The growing immorality among a considerable portion of our younger set, the throwing off of all restraints where formerly there prevailed the highest standards of fidelity, was as prevalent, if not more so, among the ancient Greeks and Romans as in modern society. It was a condition that antedated the Bible, which the biblical restrictions were intended to combat and overcome. “Do not act in accordance with the abominations of the Canaanites and the Egyptians”,[^11] cautions the Torah. As for the disregard of such exclusively Jewish institutions as the dietary laws, for example, that is nothing new either. It should be remembered that the non-Jewish world — and it always constituted the majority — never accepted these laws, no more 2000 years ago than today. Jews who refuse to abide by them are, therefore, not proving their modernity. They are, on the contrary, behaving in a very old-fashioned manner.

If anything is to be left of Judaism, if it is to resist the corrosions of time and circumstances, that are always operating and causing man’s inherited treasures to corrode, those who are bent on its preservation must employ differents tactics. They must follow a pattern that may be fraught with great resistance but which will eventually prove to have been the only one leading to success. If we wish to retain our children, then we must teach them to follow us, rather than permit them to induce us to follow them. “No religion,” said a leader of the Reform rabbinate in America, “is worth its salt which does not make great demands upon its adherents. The greatest enthusiasm was always engendered by faiths which called for the greatest sacrifices on the parts of their adherents... too many of our people want an easy-going religion, one which does not interfere with their leisure and sleep or their television, which calls for no study and no observance, which does not challenge or disturb them, a religion without spiritual travail, without any stab of thought or conscience, without any sacrifices; the religion of a self-pampering people. No religion has ever survived in that kind of an emotional and intellectual vacuum, Judaism least of all.”

To make our children realize these truths, we must convince them that the fundamental principle underlying the preservation of any tradition is “Honor thy father and thy mother.” If we shall have succeeded therein, they will remain ours. We will not be childless and something of us will survive when our time of parting comes. Through our offspring we will live, our work will go on.
