THE RIGHT TO BE JEALOUS

The potion of bitter water, that a wife suspected of infidelity to her husband, was supposed to have been made to drink by the ordinance of Mosaic law, is the only ordeal recorded in the Torah. Ordinarily Jewish jurisprudence did not admit into the courtroom as evidence anything else than the direct testimony of eyewitnesses. However in the case where a husband in a paroxysm of jealousy, even though it was based on nothing more substantial than incriminating appearances, charged his wife with having betrayed him, he was permitted, for the purpose of allaying or confirming his suspicions, to subject her to this ordeal or test. If she was guilty of the crime of which he accused her, it would be evidenced by certain physical deformities that would be produced in her, such as the falling of the hips and the swelling of the womb. If she was innocent, the water would, of course, have no effect on her.[^1]

Whether this ritual was ever carried out in practice we have no way of finding out. The Talmud, which devotes an entire tractate to the discussion of its details, does not report a single concrete instance in which a Jewish husband availed himself of the prerogative accorded to him by the law. The only light thrown on the subject is the observation made at the end of the Mishnah of Sotah[^2] to the effect that “since the number of adulterers increased in Israel the rite of the bitter waters was suspended and it was Rabbi Johanan son of Zakkai, (the savior of Judaism at the time of the destruction of the second temple of Jerusalem,) that ordered its abolition.” Now it is quite possible that, just like the precept concerning the “wayward and rebellious son”,[^3] whose penalty for gluttony and drunkenness was death, the law of the suspected wife was only of academic interest, to be studied but not to be enforced.[^4] But that still does not answer the question of whether a husband possessed the right to allow his feelings of jealousy to run away with him From the way in which the Torah indulges him in this matter, it would seem that he did. Did not this same Torah relax the prohibition of intermarriage with the heathens[^5] in the case of the non-Jewish beauty captured in war?[^6] Did it not also look aside when a relative took the law into his own hand to avenge the murder of a kinsman, even though that murder was purely accidental and unpremeditated?[^7]

The implication of the attitude evinced in Scripture in these and other such instances is that it was perfectly legit:-mate to give vent to the natural emotions and that it might be asking too much of men to insist upon their suppression. Our sages did not put this construction on the passages in question. One view expressed in the Talmud[^8] stated quite bluntly that “it was forbidden to be jealous.” A jealousy that thrives solely on imagination is reprehensible. It is a trait which, if it remains unchecked, would end by driving man out of this world.[^9] It is the greatest enemy of domestic peace, for it sows the seeds of dissension which causes separation between those who should be united like husband and wife. It is hard enough for two individuals, who are unlike, as most human beings are, to live together. Why add to their difficulties by dreaming up imaginary differences that have no basis in fact?

No, there is no divine night to be jealous. Unrestrained emotionalism is a sign of immaturity, of primitiveness, of arrested development. It is impossible for a person to think clearly when he is aroused. The moment passion seizes hold of a man, reason takes flight. Of children it may be expected that they will burst into tears whenever they are frustrated in the realization of their whims. Grown-ups must learn to check their impulses. “Only he is strong,” said ben Zoma, “who can conquer his inclinations.”[^10]

But aren’t there occasions when, far from being blameworthy, ardor is more commendable, when it becomes not only permissible but necessary to make an energetic stand and fight with passionate zeal on behalf of the right? Was it not for this very virtue that Phineas, the grandson of Aaron was rewarded with God’s everlasting covenant of peace? Was not this also the distinction of the prophet Elijah, who by firmly espousing the cause of the one and only God — “I have been exceedingly jealous for the Lord, the God of hosts”[^11] were the actual words by which he characterized his action — was able single-handed to counteract the harm done by the 450 prophets of Baal? How can evil ever be stamped out unless men are filled with righteous indignation when justice is violated’ Is not the chief fault to be found with the United Nations Organization of our time the fact that it does not react strongly enough against the flouting of its own decisions, and that it views with too much complacency the defeat of the very purpose for which it was formed, namely the protection of the weak against the powerful?

No, ardor, passion, rage is not always so abhorrent as to be berated. There are times when it is very much in order. The adjective “zealous” is not to be confused with the attribute “jealous”, even though the etymology of these two words is the same, both being derived from the Greek zelos, and although in Hebrew the term kin’ah serves for both. The first has a favorable connotation. To be full of zeal is to employ all the fervor one can muster for a constructive purpose. It is the legitimate use of the passions for accomplishing what is beneficent and laudable. The second has a sister coloring. Jealousy as such is a green-eyed monster, who becomes enraged, often without real justification, simply because he thinks that, what he believes to be his exclusive rights, have been infringed upon and his personal interests threatened.

An excellent illustration of the difference between these two traits is offered in the thinking behind Arab intransigency in regard to Israel as it was brought out in an interview with an Arab statesman quoted recently in the Baltimore Sun. “The trouble with you Occidentals,” said the spokesman for Israel’s hostile neighbors, “is that you fail completely to understand the Arab mind. You speak to us of common sense, of being practical, which would dictate that we bury the hatchet of enmity and accept the fact that Israel is there to stay, while we have our feelings. To us it is far more important to wipe away the shame and insult of two military defeats at the hands of Israel than to cooperate with our sworn enemy in any project, whatever its nature, even though it may redound to our benefit. You give priority to economic and social progress. You place on the top of your list of musts your material requirements, while to us nothing stands higher than our honor.”

There you have it — emotionalism for its own sake, indulgence in feelings even though the world might thereby be destroyed, on the one hand, and on the other hand wise restraint, the conquest of passion by prudence, the use of zeal only in the furtherance of justice, in the establishment of the kingdom of heaven by ushering in an era of mutual trust, understanding and peace, in which recrimination and backbiting would be forgotten and humanity can enjoy a prosperity and wellbeing unprecedented in the annals of race. What shall our choice be? There can be only answer.
