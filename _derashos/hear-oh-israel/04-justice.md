JUSTICE

The latter half of chapter 18 of the book of Genesis brings to the fore one of the perennial problems of human existence, a problem that will probably never be solved so long as our world is constituted as it is, namely that of justice. After the departure of his heavenly guests, the mission of one of whom was to bring to the patriarch Abraham the good tidings of the birth to him of a son by his wife Sarah, God, so Scripture tells us, decides to apprize His friend and confidant among the children of man of His intention to destroy the cities of Sodom and Gomorrah. The wickedness of the inhabitants of these two once flourishing centers of population in ancient Canaan had reached such proportions, the cries of the victims of the outrages perpetrated there had grown so loud, that the Master of the Universe, for all His patience, was no longer able to look on in silence. “Let me go down and see,” said He, “whether they have done altogether according to the outcry that has come to Me, and if not, I will know it.”[^1] What the Supreme Judge had in mind was the execution of stern justice upon the malefactors by bringing upon them one of those all-encompassing catastrophes that annihilate everyone who happens to be in their path regardless of who it may be.

The first reaction of Israel’s forefather to this startling news was one of stupefaction and horror. “Is it possible that the judge of all the earth should not do justice!”[^2] He could conceive of human governments, in their eagerness to stamp out rebellion the sources of which they are unable to fathom, resorting to the device of indiscriminate collective penalties. But that the Creator, from whose penetrating eyes nothing that transpires in the hearts of men is hidden, that the Almighty, for whom nought is impossible, should find it necessary to have recourse to punitive measures that would strike the innocent and the guilty alike, that somehow or other did not square with what, in his opinion, were the principles of divine justice, that ideal type of justice which he regarded as the foundation of God’s rule. Abraham did not doubt for a moment, after what his heavenly interlocutor had related to him of their depravity, that the Sodomites were deserving of the fate that was to befall them. They were not entitled to pity because they had never shown pity. Yet he could not help thinking that, just as in the Nineveh of the time of Jonah,[^3] there must have been among the multitudes living in Sodom children not yet old enough to know the difference between right and wrong. If these still unsullied young souls were to be permitted to perish because of the sins of their elders, then there would be no distinction between innocence and guilt. The righteous would be accorded the same treatment as the wicked. This is what emboldened Abraham, who was usually so meek, to challenge the justice of the wholesale destruction of the Sodomites, and it was only after God’s assurance that there were not ten persons among them, who were worthy of being spared, that his conscience was relieved.

Why does the Bible record in such great detail the conversation between the Supreme Judge and the father of our race about the impending doom of the Sodomites? Its purpose was obviously to point out the difference between ideal justice, which is practiced by God, and that which is current and passes for justice among men. The traditional portrayal of justice is the figure of a blindfolded woman holding in her hand the scales of a balance. The reason for the blindfolding is to assure the impartiality of the judgment, to reduce its chances of being swayed by personal considerations. Since human judges are subject to pressure or corruption, every possible precaution must be taken to eliminate those conditions that would prejudice them in favor of or against those who come before them to be judged. To make certain that they might not be tempted by the bribes of the rich nor intimidated by the threats of the powerful, Moses was advised to appoint as members of the judiciary men of prominence as well as of independent means, “men of wealth who fear God, men of truth, who hate unjust gain”.[^4] In short, jurors must close their eyes to the personal status of the litigants if their verdict is to be fair. In reality, however, blindness to the personal factors involved, far from being an asset, is a hindrance in reaching a just decision in any case involving the fang of responsibility or guilt, for responsibility and guilt are nothing but personal matters. One of the fundamental principles of rabbinic law, that is recognized by all other legal systems, is that whoever is unfortunate enough to have litigations with a person of unsound mind or a minor is at a disadvantage, for “he that injures them is culpable, while, if they injure others, they cannot be held accountable.” Juvenile delinquency is not dealt with in our courts of law as are crimes committed by mature adults, nor is it our practice to sentence the mentally deranged, who have committed murder, to the electric chair, but rather to put them away in institutions for the insane.

As for the balance in our picture, it is meant to indicate that justice must be even-handed, that in order that the sentence he considered fair and just the punishment must fit the crime. Just as the Divine Judge, in judging His creatures, follows the rule of measure for measure, “requiting the virtuous according to his deed and giving unto the wicked evil according to his wickedness,”[^5] so human justice should be based on the equation of “an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth.”[^6] “He that sheddeth the blood of man by man shall his blood shed”[^7] Theoretically speaking such a principle may seem entirely sound. Practically it is virtually impossible of application. First of all no two corresponding organs are of equal value. The eye of an artist, who can discern the subtlest distinctions of color, is more valuable than that of a person who is color-blind. The death of a physician, who brought healing to thousands of human beings, constitutes a far greater loss to the world than that of a tramp. Secondly the loss of an organ like an eye may lead to the death of the individual thus punished, so that instead of “an eye for an eye” the penalty paid would be “an eye and a life for an eye”, and in place of justice the result would be a gross miscarriage of justice. That is why the rabbinic commentators on this passage of the Bible insisted that all that the injured could claim was monetary compensation for the injury sustained, not revenge.[^8]

One of the axioms of justice is the principle of equality under the law. This principle was enunciated in the biblical ruling “There shall be a uniform judicial procedure for you as well as the stranger.”[^9] What could be more democratic? Isn’t this exactly what the constitution of the United States of America guarantees its citizens? Wasn't this among the most important stipulations made by the leaders of the French Revolution when they proclaimed the “rights of man”? Nevertheless it can very easily be demonstrated that the equal treatment of unequals is as much a travesty of justice as the unequal treatment of equals. For example, let us consider, what would happen if a law were passed forbidding the formation of labor unions and each individual employee would be compelled to negotiate separately with his employer. Since employers are usually persons in a much better financial situation than those looking for work to do and the latter are far more numerous than the former: labor would always get the short end of the bargain. Or suppose that it were to be definitely decided by the United Nations, as a solution of the present Middle East crisis, that each country involved in the dispute be awarded arms in proportion to its population, where would that leave little Israel?

It was these considerations that gave the Greek philosopher Plato the idea that justice is attained only when every citizen of a republic knows his place in the commonwealth and performs the task for which he is best fitted. Such an arrangement does not, to be sure, make for democracy. It is even doubtful whether the accurate determination of what an individual excels in is feasible. Some human beings are so versatile that they could make good in almost anything they would undertake, while others have no talent at all. The truth that Plato emphasized and that must be accepted is that “men have not been created equal.” They differ not only because of the variation of native endowment but by virtue of the circumstances in which they find themselves. It is a well-known fact that, when two candidates with the identical qualifications compete for a position, the chances of the one, who belongs to the majority of the population, are much better than those of him who is a member of a minority group.

What is just and fair is, therefore, not to be decided by a rule of thumb. The personal factors weigh very heavily in the calculations. Each case merits individual attention. The question that must constantly be asked is “Have we fulfilled the rule of ‘giving to each his due’ —- suum cuique tribuere”? In fine, if there is to be real justice in the world such conditions social, political and economic must be established that the opportunity for every individual as well as every nation, great or small, to realize his or its legitimate aspirations would be assured. In a society in which “the wolf and the lamb could sojourn peaceably side by side, and the leopard would he down with the kid and the calf beside the lion and a little child would lead them”,[^10] in the Security Council of such a society every member nation would obtain a fair hearing and could expect proper redress for its grievances.

Our homilists of old suggest that the chief argument of the patriarch Abraham in his intercession on behalf of the Sodomites was that, to be absolutely fair with His creatures, the Supreme Judge would have to temper His justice with mercy. “If you God insist on strict retaliation, on the exaction of measure for measure, you cannot have a world.”[^11] Man is by nature weak, and in judging him you must take account of the shortcomings of the flesh that are largely responsible for his misdeeds. “For He knoweth our inclination. He remembereth that we are but dust.”[^12] That may be the reason why Abraham prefaced his remarks by saying that he, Abraham, was, like all members of the human species, only “dust and ashes”. And God must have conceded his point, for He is described by the psalmist, not only as “just in all his ways”, but also “loving in all His deeds”.[^13]

“Justice, justice shalt thou pursue”[^14] is the preamble of the injunction in the book of Deuteronomy pertaining to the appointment and the qualifications of judges. But why “pursue”? Because, as it has been aptly observed, “justice is not without us as a fact, but like the kingdom of God a great yearning within us”. In fact, “our whole social life is in essence but a long, slow striving for the victory of justice over force.”
