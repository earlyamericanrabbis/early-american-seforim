“THE WAY OF FREEDOM”

In addition to the shankbone, which is commemorative of the paschal lamb and the cakes of unleavened bread and the bitter-herbs, the home-ritual which is performed on the first two nights of Passover includes the “four cups” of wine. The drinking of this quantity of the beverage that “causes the heart of man to rejoice”[^1] at the evening meal of Israel’s freedom festival is not just accidental or optional. It 18, according to the Mishnah so obligatory that “even the poorest of the poor” must comply with the precept. And if he does not have the means for procuring four cups of wine, they must be provided for him out of public funds.[^2] The reason for this insistence is that the drinking of the wine is the foremost outward indication of the inner feeling of freedom that a Jew should experience on this anniversary of his first liberation, more even than the reclining position at the festive board,[^3] from which women are, out of consideration of modesty, exempted.

But why four cups? Why would not one or two or three have done just as well? One answer to this question cited by the Jerusalem Talmud[^4] is that in the announcement to the Israelites of their deliverance from the bondage of Egypt four different expressions of redemption were employed, namely “And I shall bring forth... and I shall save... and I shall redeem... and I shall take.”[^5] Each one of these expressions was deserving of a drink on its own account. That was why four cups of wine were ordained at the recitation of the Hagadah of Passover. This explanation may appear to be at first blush rather artificial, one of those instances in which our doctors of the law confessed that they were unable to find the scriptural basis of an ancient usage and that the “source cited was just a prop to lean on.”[^6] For if the quantity of wine to be consumed depended entirely and solely on the number of words of a specific type occurring in a given text of the Bible, he who was looking for an excuse to drink more than the prescribed amount could find another text to support his view. If the devil can quote Scripture, why not a lover of the fruit of the vine?

A further explanatory comment by Rabbenu Sherira,[^7] the head of the academy of Pumbeditha in the 10th century, indicates that the elucidation offered by the Talmud of Jerusalem was neither haphazard nor arbitrary. According to this foremost historian among the geonim of Babylonia there was an historic reason for the number of the cups of wine prescribed by tradition for the Seder ritual. It corresponded to the number of exiles or subjections to foreign rule, that Israel endured in the course of its career as a people, namely those of Egypt, Babylonia, Persia and Greece. Each time God’s chosen people drained the cup of bitterness. But the draining of these cups was followed by the drinking out of a cup of salvation, symbolizing Israel’s deliverance from the yoke of aliens.[^8] Only one exile remained to be accounted for, the longest of them all, that of Rome, which in the days of the gaon was not yet over. That is probably the reason why a fifth cup, the one designated for Elijah, the forerunner of the Messiah, was placed on the table but left undrunk.

So much for the contribution made by the gaon Sherira towards clearing up the motivation behind the “four cups” that figure in the Seder ceremony. But what does all this add up to? What lesson do these facts that are brought to our attention teach us? What is their significance, their meaning? The enigmas of Israel’s past are often resolved by analogies from the experiences of other peoples. Thus, for example, America, too, had its struggle for liberty. As Abraham Lincoln, the emancipator of the negroes, put it in his famous Gettysburg Address, the United States of America were founded as “a nation conceived in liberty and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal.” Yet although four score and seven years had passed since the declaration of independence was issued, proclaiming these lofty principles of democracy, the black man was still a slave in “the land of the free”, and a bloody civil war had to be fought before the inalienable rights claimed by all other natives of America were extended to him. We are now in the year 1958. Nevertheless although he has long already won emancipation in theory, the negro’s struggle for equality with his white brethren in fact is far from ended. The decision of the highest court of the country declaring the segregation of colored from white citizens in public institutions unconstitutional is met by determined opposition and stout defiance in the deep South, not only on the part of the rank and file of the white population, but by political and civic leaders all the way down the line. “God himself,” so they attempt to justify their position by wrapping themselves in the cloak of religious truth, “has made the distinction between the Caucasian and the negro races by creating the one white and the other black. Let no man dare to bring together that which the Almighty has intended to remain separate.”

From the vehemence of their outbursts one would be led to believe that the sacred institution of the Jim Crow car and of separate waiting rooms and ticket offices and lavatories in railroad stations and other public places had been in existence since time immemorial. As a matter of fact, as it was recently discovered by consulting the archives on the subject, segregation is not nearly as old as it is pretended to be. In the 1870’s and 1880’s it did not yet exist altogether. In those days, when they were much less qualified than they are presently, negroes voted in large numbers in most Southern states and the politicians, far from putting stumbling blocks in their way, as many of them do now, offered them all sorts of inducements to obtain their backing. If since the beginning of the twentieth century a deterioration set in in the political status of the negroes south of the Mason-Dixon line, this was due to circumstances which those affected by them had done nothing to bring on. World war one, in which negroes and white men fought and died side by side and were often buried in common graves, helped considerably in breaking down the barriers between the races. This trend was still further accentuated by world war two, which resulted in the civil rights bills of presidents Truman and Eisenhower. The forces of liberalism were definitely gaining until the present setback, dealt by the Southern die-hards, took place.

The conclusion to be drawn from these facts is that the course of human progress never follows a straight line. Man’s spiritual evolution, his intellectual and moral growth from the humble beginnings, from which he started out, is not continual but intermittent. Every bit of evidence produced by history points to a zigzag. Man takes a few steps forward and then retreats. Each advance is followed by partial retrogression. The French Revolution of 1789 was succeeded by the Napoleonic reaction of 1796; the comparatively liberal regime of Alexander II of Russia by the tyrannical rules of Alexander III and Nicholas II.

Jewish history has pursued a similar course in modern times as well as in antiquity. The disruption of East European Jewry as a result of the dislocations produced by the first world war was partially offset by the Balfour Declaration. The whittling down of the intent of the mandate after the massacres and riots of 1929 in Palestine was checked when the persecution of the Jews of Germany by Hitler caused thousands of them to migrate to the Jewish national home. With the coming of world war two the gates of Palestine were closed again. However the extermination by the Nazis of 6,000,000 Jews in Continental Europe caused the United Nations to view favorably the establishment within the boundaries of the Holy Land of an independent Jewish commonwealth and the state of Israel was born. Jewish hopes were once more on the ascendant. The trouble started again, as the two major powers, that were the first to recognize Israel’s right to exist, America and Soviet Russia, had a falling out with each other. As each tried to woo the Arab States, Israel, which was in the middle, suffered. Egypt, defying the orders of the United Nations, tightened her boycott and, made cocky by the receipt of Russian arms, posed a threat to an isolated Israel. Israel’s lightning Sina: campaign exploded the bubble of Egypt’s military strength, but President Eisenhower’s demand of withdrawal, not only from the Sinai Peninsula but the Gaza Strip as well, virtually nullified all of Israel’s gains and confirmed the anti-Western Nasser in power.

Thus it goes up and down. The fifth cup, that of Elijah is poured but not drunk. One day it will For it is the way of freedom to be attained in stages, not all at once. Progress has been made. Israel’s population is close to the 2,000,000 mark and 90% of it is Jewish, and the flow of Zion’s exiled children returning from the lands of their dispersion has by no means come to standstill. Nothing like this has happened in two thousand years. Notwithstanding all set-backs there have been indubitable gains. There is every reason, therefore, to hope that, with God’s help, the complete redemption, the G’ulah sh‘lemah is not too far off.
