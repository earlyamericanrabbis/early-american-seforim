<span dir="rtl">ארבעים שנה היה משה משמר עצמו שלא להקפיד כנגדן ולא פרק משאן מעליו</span> “For forty years did Moses refrain from showing anger against the people, and from surrendering their burden” (Numbers Rabbah 19). From the day on which God’s mission to liberate the Jews from Egyptian bondage had been assigned to him on Mount Horeb, and through all the forty years of their wandering, both he and his brother Aaron had suffered greatly at their hands. They remained unimpressed by the magnificent miracles performed in their behalf by the Lord. His devoted labors and loyal application to their interests brought Moses few friends; definitely opposed to him were the princes and men of wealth, and those in their favor. No opportunity to deprecate and assail the leader was passed by. On every occasion which presented difficulties to the people’s protracted journey, they uttered their complaints, and reproached Moses with having taken them from the excellent land of Egypt into a wilderness; their deliverance, they wailed, had brought them only misfortune. When Moses first brought the news to the people that God had heard their cries and seen their agony, and was prepared to liberate them, he was greeted in this fashion: <span dir="rtl">ולא שמעו אל משה מקצר רוח ומעבדה קשה</span> “They hearkened not unto Moses, through anguish of spirit, and through hard service” (Exodus vi, 9). When he gained an audience with Pharaoh, accompanied by several prominent elders, <span dir="rtl">היו מגנבים את עצמם ונשמטים אחד אחד והלכו להם</span> “they drew away stealthily one by one,” leaving Moses and Aaron alone (Exodus Rabbah 5). Then, when Pharaoh, irate, issued still more stringent decrees against the Israelites, the elders were first to denounce the two emissaries:

<span dir="rtl">הבאשתם את ריחנו בעיני פרעה ובעיני עבדיו לתת חרב בידם להרגנו</span>

“Ye have made our odor to be abhorred in the eyes of Pharaoh, and in the eyes of his servants, to put a sword in their hand to slay us” (Exodus v, 21).

When the hour of deliverance struck, and the people were to gird themselves for their departure, again did Moses and Aaron find themselves deserted. The people had personal matters to hold their attention. <span dir="rtl">כל ישראל נתעסקו בביזה</span> “All Israel was occupied with despoiling (the Egyptians)” (Sotah 13). All through the wilderness Moses was continuously tortured by their waywardness. When they beheld the hosts of Pharaoh pursuing them, they cried out, <span dir="rtl">מה זאת עשית לנו להוציאנו ממצרים</span> “What is it that thou hast done to us, to bring us forth out of Egypt?” (Exodus xiv, 11). Repeatedly we read the phrases, “And the people cried out unto Moses,” “And the people complained against Moses.” They kept on recalling their good fortune in Egypt: <span dir="rtl">בשבתנו על סיר הבשר, באכלנו לחם לשבע</span> “when we sat by the flesh pots, and when we did eat bread to satisfaction” (xvi, 3). They refused to suffer for the boon of liberty. Always they demanded new miracles, new supernatural evidences — yet refused to remain content.

When he built them a tabernacle, they suspected him of misusing communal funds. <span dir="rtl">אמר משה יודע אני שכמה מישראל רוננים הם, הריני עשה להם חשבון מכל מלאכת המשכן</span> “Moses spoke, I know that many in Israel speak ill of me; hence shall I give them a reckoning of all the labor of the tabernacle” (Tanhuma, Pekude). Yet he bore them no resentment; and when God Himself grew angry at them, he defended the people with all his power. Even after the incident of the golden calf, when God cried, <span dir="rtl">הניחה לי ואכלם ואעשה אותך לגוי גדול</span> “Let me alone, that I may consume them; and I will make of thee a great nation” (Exodus xxxii, 10) — Moses exclaimed in anguish, <span dir="rtl">מחני-נא מספרך אשר כתבת</span> “Blot out my name from Thy book which Thou hast written!” Moses would not live without his people.

Later, when the wilderness journey was ended, and they stood on the boundaries of Edom, near the Promised Land, Moses felt assured that now the Israelites would be content, and would together expedite entrance into their new country; but a new conflict arose. <span dir="rtl">ולא היה מים לעדה</span>—<span dir="rtl">וירב העם עם משה</span> “And there was no water for the congregation. . . . And the people quarreled with Moses” (Numbers xx, 2, 3). The complaints were always of the same nature:

<span dir="rtl">למה הבאתם את-קהל ה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">אל-המדבר הזה למות שם אנחנו ובעירנו</span> “And why have ye brought up the assembly of the Eternal into this desert, that we and our beasts should die there?” (4).

<span dir="rtl">ולמה העליתנו ממצרים להביא אתנו אל המקום הרע הזה לא מקום זרע ותאנה וגפן ורמון ומים אין לשתות</span> “And therefore have ye made us to come up out of Egypt, to bring us into this evil place? it is no place of seed, or of figs, or of vines, or of pomegranates; neither is there any water to drink” (5). There was no thought of obtaining their own home in their own land — only of the today, of the immediate life of exile in which they were living. Thus did they arouse the wrath of Moses.

## Moses Strikes the Rock

The current quarrel with Moses was far more severe than any that had preceded it. Not alone did they express their resentment against him and Aaron; even the honor of the Eternal was not untouched. Recall the passage:

<span dir="rtl">המה מי מריבה אשר רבו בני-ישראל את-ה</span>’ “This is the water of Meribah (quarreling); because the children of Israel quarreled with the Eternal.” They seemed to be affrighted by the realization that very soon they would have to wage war against the inhabitants of the land. They still remembered the report of the spies: <span dir="rtl">כי עז העם היושב בארץ והערים בצורות גדולות מאד</span> “The people are powerful that abide in the land, and the cities are fortified and very great” (Numbers xiii, 28).

<span dir="rtl">נוטלים היו ישראל בניהם ובנותיהם קטנים וזורקים לתוך חיקו של משה ואמרו לו</span>: <span dir="rtl">בן עמרם</span>! <span dir="rtl">מה הנאה התקנת להם לאלו</span>? <span dir="rtl">מה פרנסה התקנת להם לאלו</span>? “The Israelites would set their little sons and daughters on Moses’ lap, and say: Son of Amram! What coming joys have you arranged for these children? What economic future have you provided for them?” (Yalkut Deuteronomy). Their intent was to proclaim that neither the Torah nor the land given by God could assure the future of their offspring. All their words and deeds were overlaid with the attitudes of Korach and the spies. Although they had witnessed the cruel end of these treacherous men and had seen how dearly Israel had paid for their derelictions, still did they remain under the influence of the evildoers — thus deeply distressing their leader.

God spoke unto Moses: <span dir="rtl">קח את המטה... ודברתם אל הסלע לעיניהם ונתן מימיו</span> “Take the staff. . . . and speak ye unto the rock before their eyes; and it shall give forth its water” (Numbers xx, 8). From this passage many sages have adduced that Moses did not in fact sin against God, for if he were only to speak to the rock, why should he be commanded to take along his staff? His real sin was in the expression of his wrath against all Israel, and his abuse of them. When he struck the rock, he imagined himself beating the people.

<span dir="rtl">וירם משה את-ידו ויך את-הסלע במטהו פעמים</span> “And Moses lifted up his hand, and with his staff he smote the rock twice” (11). <span dir="rtl">הכה פעם אחת התחיל הסלע נוטף מים מועטין, טפין-טפין. אמרו לו, המים הללו ליונקי שדים או לגמולי חלב</span>? “With his first blow the water began flowing slowly, drop by drop. Thereupon they asked him, Is this water for nurselings or babes just weaned?” They had asked for drink for the entire people, and for their sheep and cattle. <span dir="rtl">מיד הקפיד כנגדם והכהו פעמים ויצאו מים רבים</span> “Immediately he grew wroth against them, struck the rock a second time, and brought forth a great stream of water” (Tanhuma).

The second blow was really intended for the Israelites — this was his transgression. A popular leader must display no anger; he must reason and not strike. His patience must be of iron —else he may become — contrary to the will of God — a dictator and tyrant. The consequent doom was heard by Moses and Aaron:

<span dir="rtl">לכן לא תביאו את הקהל הזה אל הארץ אשר נתתי להם</span>

“Therefore ye shall not bring this assembly into the land which I have given them” (xx, 12).

Moses, ever ready to assume all guilt upon himself, pleaded with God, <span dir="rtl">רבש</span>”<span dir="rtl">ע אני הקפדתי, אהרן מה חטא</span>? “Master of the universe, it was I who showed anger; how then has Aaron sinned?” (Tanhuma). But God, unrelenting, replied:

<span dir="rtl">יאסף אהרן אל עמיו כי לא יבוא אל הארץ אשר נתתי לבני ישראל, על אשר מריתם את פי למי מריבה</span> “Let Aaron be gathered unto his people; for he will not enter the land which I promised the Israelites, insomuch as he defied my command at the waters of Meribah (conflict).” Aaron was equally guilty, for assenting to Moses’ act and not restraining his anger. Faith and confidence can never be created through force; it must arise through enlightenment and conviction.
