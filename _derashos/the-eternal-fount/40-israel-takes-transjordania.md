<span dir="rtl">בשלשה דברים נתחלקה ארץ ישראל, בגורלות, באורים ותומים ובכספים</span> “In three ways was Palestine apportioned — through lot, through divination, and through money” (Jer. Yoma 4, 1). By lot is meant pure chance, the fortunes of war. Now, in order to conquer the land, each tribe was expected to choose its proportion of military scouts, prepared to fight and die for their brethren. This must be done as prescribed by the *Urim v’Tumim*, in the sense of the true Law and spirit. The aim was not to establish a mere political and economic national center. Palestine was to become a spiritual focus, influencing all branches of the people throughout the diaspora.

This would unite them into a homogeneous people, capable of withstanding every hardship, and of combating the injurious influences striking at their harmony. Those tribes unable to supply the full quota of fighters must compound their assessment with money. They must provide the funds required for the various enterprises of land regeneration — and thus be considered part of the entire process of settlement.

The fate of Israel is in its own hand. When all divisions of the people display singleness of purpose, with unswerving devotion, their victory is assured. We read in the *Hegyonot el Ami* of Chief Rabbi Amiel of Tel Aviv an apt quotation from a midrash in Tosefot Menahot. This relates that Ashmodai once brought Solomon a man with two heads, whose father had died and who was now seeking his share of the inheritance. Ashmodai inquired whether the double-pated man was to be considered as two persons and be granted two portions or be considered but one. Solomon thereupon advised that a kettle of boiling water be poured over one head; if the other felt no pain, that would prove it a different person; but if the second began howling, that would indicate their singleness.

This counsel, so adds Amiel, is highly applicable to Jewry. The organism of Israel has many heads, which ordinarily impress the beholder as bearing no interrelationship. All talk, think, and plan differently. Often the query arises whether the heads belong to one body. Are the Jews a unified nation or not?

But all doubts vanish when boiling water is poured over one head. When persecutions and direct assaults overcome any part of Jewry, there is an instinctive feeling of anguish in all Jewish heads everywhere. Here is the proof of the oneness of these many heads on one body.

<span dir="rtl">ומקנה רב היה לבני ראובן ולבני גד</span> “Now the children of Reuben and the children of Gad had much cattle” (xxxii, 1). Hence they thus approached Moses:

<span dir="rtl">אם מצאנו חן בעיניך יתן את-הארץ הזאת לעבדיך לאחזה, אל תעברנו את הירדן</span> “If we have found favor in thine eyes, let this land be given unto thy servants for a possession, and make us not pass over the Jordan” (5).

<span dir="rtl">גדרות צאן נבנה למקננו פה וערים לטפנו</span> “We will build sheepfolds here for our cattle, and cities for our little ones” (15). The Midrash comments on this —

<span dir="rtl">חיבבו את ממנם יותר מן הנפשות, לפיכך גלו תחלה מכל השבטים</span> “They held their possessions more dearly than their children (in that they spoke first of providing for their cattle and then for their little ones); therefore they were first of the tribes to be driven into exile” (Numbers Rabbah 22).

And so is it always. As soon as Jews begin to care more for their physical property than for their offspring, for amassing wealth rather than for imparting spiritual values to the next generation, they undermine the existence of their people — and they are the first to disappear.

When Moses acceded to the request of these tribes, he replied, <span dir="rtl">בנו לכם ערים לטפכם וגדרות לצאנכם</span> “Build ye cities for your little ones, and folds for your flock” (24). The children were first to be taken care of. They are the true riches of a nation. When the young are filled with patriotism and imbued with fidelity to tradition, the future of their nation is secure.

## The Security of Israel

When Moses first heard the request of the tribes that wished to remain east of the Jordan for the sake of their cattle and other possessions, he cried angrily, <span dir="rtl">האחיכם יבאו למלחמה ואתם תשבו פה</span>, “Shall your brethren go to war, and shall ye abide here?” (6). Moses was also fearful lest this conduct halt the other tribes on their task of entering and subjugating the land. He exclaimed: <span dir="rtl">ולמה תניאון את-לב בני ישראל מעבר אל הארץ אשר נתן להם ה</span>’ “And wherefore discourage ye the heart of the children of Israel from passing into the land which the Eternal hath given them?” (7). He feared as well that the original plaint of the spies, that the people of the land were too strong for Israel, still lived in their hearts and was weakening their faith in the Lord. <span dir="rtl">כה עשו אבותיכם</span> “Thus did your fathers,” he added. They too spoke ill of the Holy Land, and were consequently doomed to remain in the wilderness <span dir="rtl">עד תם כל הדור העושה הרע</span> “until the end of all the generation of evildoers,” “until all the generation that had done evil. . . . was consumed” (13). Is it your desire to anger God once more, that He <span dir="rtl">ויסף עוד להניחו במדבר</span> “yet again place them in the desert” (15)?

Rabbi Jonathan Eibschutz (Yaarat Devash) has a slightly different rendering of the phrase already translated, “Shall your brethren go to war, and shall ye abide here?” “Do you believe that when enemies attack your brethren in Palestine or elsewhere, you will abide in peace and suffer naught? Then you are in error. You who sit with folded arms while your brethren are suffering will not either be spared.”

B. Joasohn has related the following historic fact: In the winter of 1093, at the beginning of the Crusades, the Jews of France sent a letter to their brothers in Germany, in which they urged the German Jews to offer prayers over the dire misfortune that had befallen Israel, declaring that a similar danger was imminent in Germany as well. The answer advised that the German community was indeed profoundly shocked by the terrible news they had received, and would assuredly pray for their agonized kin in France; but, they added, as to their own community, <span dir="rtl">להם לבני מגנצא לא ישימו לב ולא ידאגו להם, כי אין שום סכנה נשקפת להקהלות בגרמניה</span> “They need not be concerned or worry about the Jews of Mainz, for no possibility of danger lurks over the German communities.”

What actually happened to these assured persons within a few months is fully recounted in history — which forever repeats itself.

The tribes of Reuben and Gad did promise Moses that they would not isolate themselves in Transjordania and permit the others to fight alone. To the contrary, <span dir="rtl">ואנחנו נחלץ חשים לפני בני ישראל עד אשר אם-הביאנם אל-מקומם</span> “But we ourselves will equip and speed before the children of Israel, until we have brought them unto their place” (xxxii, 17). <span dir="rtl">לא נשוב אל בתינו עד התנחל בני ישראל איש נחלתו</span> “We will not return unto our houses, until the children of Israel have inherited every one his inheritance.” These words pacified Moses, who declared,

<span dir="rtl">אם-תעשון את-הדבר הזה... והייתם נקים מה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">ומישראל</span>

“If ye will do this thing . . . ye shall be guiltless before the Eternal, and before Israel” (20, 22). If God had destined this beautiful land for the transjordanian tribes, it was surely with the knowledge that they would aid their brethren to win and build up the major territory across the river — to form a single community.

<span dir="rtl">ויכתב משה את-מוצאיהם למסעיהם על-פי ה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">ואלה מסעיהם למוצאיהם</span> “And Moses wrote their goings out according to their journeys by the commandment of the Eternal; and these are their journeys according to their goings out” (xxxiii, 2). The Maggid of Dubnow has thus explained the inversion of “goings out” and “journeys” in the second clause:

“The children of Israel considered of highest importance the going out from Egypt, the exodus from the land of bondage; hence they regularly reckoned the distance already traversed, leaving Egypt behind. But the prime aim of Moses was to reach the promised land — therefore he wrote down ‘their goings out according to their journeys,’ since this form of recording told how much distance they had yet to cover before reaching Palestine.”
