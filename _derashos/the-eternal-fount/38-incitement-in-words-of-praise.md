<span dir="rtl">וירא בלק בן-צפור את כל-אשר-עשה ישראל לאמרי</span> “And Balak, the son of Zippor, saw all that Israel had done to the Amorites.” However, he was not interested in seeing what Amor had done to the Israelites. This attitude is the beginning and end of the fearful <span dir="rtl">שנאת עולם לעם עולם</span> “eternal hatred of the eternal people.” The peccadilloes charged to the Jew by others are never forgotten; but the great barbarities perpetrated by others against the Jew — these are not known nor remembered.

Moses made a peaceful request — <span dir="rtl">אעברה בארצך</span> “Let me but cross over thy land,” with the assurance that he would in no wise injure the inhabitants. But Sihon king of the Amorites assembled his entire army; <span dir="rtl">ויצא לקראת ישראל המדברה... וילחם בישראל</span> “and went out against Israel in the desert. . . . and fought against Israel” (xxi, 23). This is the incident which Balak refused to recall and thus concealed from his people. He knew only that <span dir="rtl">ויכהו ישראל</span> “Israel smote him (the Amorite)” and was therefore to be abjured.

<span dir="rtl">אכל בכסף תשברני... ומים בכסף תתן לי</span> “Thou shalt sell me food for money. . . . and give me water for money” (Deuteronomy ii, 28). Thus Moses had reassured the king. None of Israel would overreach himself in the land; everyone was prepared to be friendly and helpful to the populace. Jewish newcomers have always given to other lands, and taken naught from the native population; but Balak would not think of this. Only one thing remained in his mind — <span dir="rtl">ויירש את ארצו</span> that Israel had taken possession of the land from Sihon king of Amor and Og king of Bashan. To him Israel was only the assailant, the oppressor, not a pacific coworker, intent only upon his normal human rights.

Balak proceeded to incite his people against Israel. <span dir="rtl">הנה עם יצא ממצרים</span> “Behold, there is a people come out of Egypt.” He described these wanderers as having lived for long in a country where they lacked for nothing, yet suddenly arising and departing therefrom. This was represented as Jewish gratitude toward the land that had befriended them.

<span dir="rtl">הנה כסה את-עין הארץ</span> “Behold, they cover the face of the earth” — meaning, these newcomers occupy the best lands, and seize the most influential places in industry and commerce. <span dir="rtl">והוא ישב ממלי</span> “And they abide over against me” (xxii, 5) — they are my opponents politically. Israel, it is intimated, is never content with any land’s government, is always found in the opposition, and is the bulwark of liberal and radical parties.

<span dir="rtl">ויגר מואב מפני העם</span> “And Moab was affrighted of the people.” The poisonous propaganda of Balak was doing its work.

<span dir="rtl">כשהיו ישראל נראין לעמונים, נראין עטופין לשלום, ולמואבים נראין מזויינים למלחמה</span> “To the Ammonites, seeking peace, the Israelites had the semblance of peaceful men; to the Moabites, however, they appeared armed for warfare” (Numbers Rabbah 20).

<span dir="rtl">ובלק בן-צפור מלך למואב בעת ההיא</span> — “And Balak, the son of Zippor, was (became) king of the Moabites at that time” (xxii, 4). It was said that his envenomed diatribes against Israel had brought him to the throne.

<span dir="rtl">כל המצר לישראל נעשה ראש</span> “All who oppress Israel are elevated to power.” The darkest forces of a nation unite to make such their leaders. Thus spoke Rabbi Chaim Soloveitchik: “Why is the fact that Balak was king not mentioned at the beginning of the portion? This indicates that he rose to prominence only because of his active campaign against the people of Israel, whom he had presented to his followers as an object to be feared. When they were thoroughly frightened, then alone did he become king of Moab.”

Thus had the Lord commanded Moses: <span dir="rtl">אל-תצר את מואב ואל-תתגר בם</span> “Distress not the Moabites, neither contend with them in war” (Deut. ii, 9). This order the people obeyed. <span dir="rtl">ויחנו בערבות מואב</span> “And they encamped on the plains of Moab,” not approaching the inhabited land itself. Recalling the tragic fate of his combative neighbors, Balak feared to enter into warfare with Israel. He determined on a novel means of fighting the newcomers — by the tongue. Just as Israel’s strength lay in their prayers and erudition, so would he too employ the power of the tongue against them, through imprecation and defamation (Numbers Rabbah 20). He called for the notorious Jew-baiter Balaam (*she-bila’ am* — “who destroyeth the people”—Sanhedrin 105) to become minister of propaganda. <span dir="rtl">לכה-נא ארה-לי את-העם הזה</span> “Come now, therefore, I pray thee, curse me this people!” — <span dir="rtl">ואגרשנו מן-הארץ</span> — “that I may drive them out of the land” (6). The spreading of slanderous tales about them would bring about their expulsion and the expropriation of their goods by the king. The pen is mightier than the sword; if Balak could succeed in making Israel everywhere hated, he might do as he wished with them, and none would stay his hand.

## Blessings Intended as Curses

<span dir="rtl">מברכותיו של אותו רשע אתה יודע מה היה בלבו</span> “From the very blessings of this wicked man one could determine that which was truly in his heart” (Sanhedrin 105b). Whensoever the anti-Semite, the Jew-baiter, begins to praise the Jews and portray their achievements, his purpose is only to evoke greater hatred and envy toward the people. He would open the eye of the gentile to Jewish wealth and power, and thereby incite other men against Israel. Similar tactics have been employed against us from time immemorial; they are far more dangerous than ordinary slander and misrepresentation.

Often a gentile of distinction offers words of flattery to his Jewish brethren, but the effect of his statement is directly opposite to his apparent intent. The sycophantic weaklings among us are often misled by such words. Imagining that these overtures of friendship are well meant, they depart ever further from their own.

<span dir="rtl">טוב לשמוע תוכחות שהוכיח משה רבנו את ישראל</span>—<span dir="rtl">משמוע ברכות של בלעם שבהם גבה לבם ונפלו בשיטים</span> “It is better to hearken to the rebukes which Moses administered to Israel, than to the benedictions of Balaam, which elevated the pride of the people but brought about their discomfiture at Shittim, capital of Moab” (Midrash Kohelet 7).

The Talmud also indicates that all Jewish suffering arises primarily through the Balaams, who manage to create envious hatred of Israel among the nations. For the talmudic statement has it that Balaam was one of the three advisors with whom Pharaoh took counsel concerning the Israelites. He was chief speaker at the conference that declared <span dir="rtl">הבה נתחכמה לו פן ירבה</span> “Come, let us deal wisely with them, lest they multiply.” He proclaimed the policy of hurling newborn sons of Israel into the river. The other two demurred at this barbarity — <span dir="rtl">איוב שתק, יתרו ערק</span> “Job was silent, and Jethro forsook the land” (Sanhedrin 106). Balaam was triumphant; and the Israelites were doomed to savage suffering. In connection with the war against Amalek, we read the later phrase, <span dir="rtl">ויבא עמלק</span> “And Amalek came.” <span dir="rtl">מהיכן בא</span> “Whence came they?” asks the Midrash. Why should they suddenly arise to attack the host of Israel? <span dir="rtl">מאצל בלעם הרשע</span>? “From the side of Balaam the wicked,” comes the answer. They had been counseling with Balaam, who instigated the quarrel (Midrash Rabbah). Thus through history clanks the long chain of Balaam’s anti-Semitism; all Jew-baiters in our story, in every time and clime, have been of the same mind and the same school.

Balak erected seven altars, before which he convoked great assemblages of Moabites, while Balaam uttered his tirades against Israel. Thus spake he: <span dir="rtl">מן ארם ינחני בלק, לכה לי יעקב</span> “Balak has brought me from Aram, saying, Come and curse me Jacob” — come, elucidate the Jewish problem! However, <span dir="rtl">מה אקב לא קבה אל</span> “How can I do injury to those blessed of the Lord?” “How shall I imprecate, whom God hath not imprecated?” (xxiii, 8).

<span dir="rtl">כי-מראש צרים אראנו ומגבעות אשורנו</span> “For from the top of the rocks I see him, and from the hills I view him” (9). <span dir="rtl">מאן יכול להוא לישראל</span> “Who can overcome this Israel” on his high impreganble place? <span dir="rtl">הן עם לבדד ישכון ובגויים לא יתחשב</span> “Lo, the people shall dwell solitary, and shall not reckon itself among the nations” (9). <span dir="rtl">מי מנה עפר יעקב</span> “Who hath counted the dust of Jacob?” (10). <span dir="rtl">כמה חיילין וכמה אשריין נפקו ממנו</span> “Who can recount the hosts and armies laid in the dust by Israel, in his long history?” <span dir="rtl">ותהי אחריתי כמהו</span> “And let my last end be like his!” (10) (Zohar).

And so did Balaam without cease seek incitation against the Israelites. The Moabites were to be affrighted by a recital of Jewish power and wealth. It appears, however, that Balak did not fully understand this academic form of anti-Semitism. He demanded excesses, pogroms, bloodshed. He protested to Balaam: <span dir="rtl">לקב אויבי לקחתיך והנה ברכת ברך</span> “I took thee to imprecate mine enemies, and behold, thou hast blessed them altogether.”

Balaam replied that Balak did not understand the prophet’s true meaning. But the king angrily demanded at length, <span dir="rtl">ועתה ברח-לך אל מקומך</span> “Therefore now flee thou to thy place!” (xxiv, 11).

So is it ever — conflict breaks out among the ranks of anti-Semites, and thereby is Israel succored.

Hassidic lore thus speaks of Balaam’s contumacious conduct and his final downfall:

Once there came to Rabbi Baruch Mezhibuzer a certain anti-hassidic rabbi, who prided himself greatly on his learning. Though Baruch was known to be most hospitable, he received the guest coldly and without honor. When those standing by marveled at this conduct, Baruch explained:

“You know that the Sabbath and its synagogue service are marked by a vast hospitality. When the Sabbath falls on the New Moon, it gives up to that festival its right to the prophetic reading (*maftir*). When it falls on a standard festival, it transfers thereto the full reading of the Pentateuchal portion. When on Yom Kippur, the Sabbath even yields up its right to dining. Only when the Sabbath coincides with Rosh Hashanah, does it not yield up its right to forbid the sounding of the ram’s horn. Why is the horn (*shofar*) not sounded in that event? Because one despises a guest that always blows about himself.”
