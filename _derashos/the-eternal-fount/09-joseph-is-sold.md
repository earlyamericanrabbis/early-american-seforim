<span dir="rtl">וישב יעקב בארץ מגורי אביו, בארץ כנען</span> “And Jacob abode in the land of his father’s sojourning, in the land of Canaan.” After many years of wandering in strange lands, and suffering from the baseless persecution of many enemies, he again became a free man. Having built a home in the country of his parents, he experienced a new feeling of security.

Life no longer held out its meed of bitterness; nor needed he fear Esau and his children, who were never content to leave him happy and who actually sought to take his riches from him. He was finally saved from the possible vengeance of his brother Esau, though not without terror and hardship. No more did he fear Esau’s deceitful embrace, nor his companions who wished to do him ill.

The incident of the seduction of his daughter Dinah by Shechem, which brought on armed conflict between his own followers and those of Hamor and Shechem, was finally settled, and peace reigned.

<span dir="rtl">בקש יעקב לשבת בשלוה</span> “Now Jacob desired but to abide in peace,” spending his latter years in his own land, and devoting them to propagating his parental faith. It was his purpose to continue the teachings of his grandfather Abraham and father Isaac, who had built many altars, and had brought to their neighbors the lofty concept of monotheism. But no such opportunity was vouchsafed Jacob. His life was to continue harsh and tumultuous. <span dir="rtl">קפץ עליו רוגזו של יוסף</span> “He was stricken by the tragic happening in the life of Joseph,” the conflict between the youth and his brothers which was to end in overwhelming grief (Genesis Rabbah 84).

The narrative of Joseph and his brethren, says Rabbi Isaac Nissenbaum, mirrors the perennial sadness of Father Jacob’s fate in the thousands of years of his existence. Always he suffered from his own offspring more than from strangers. Alien enemies he was able to elude in time. He managed to coexist with the descendants of Esau; the struggle with these might be bloody and protracted, but he never lost his courage or his hope for victory. His own young however provided the greatest grief; and he could not fight his way out of the conflict. The mighty, unyielding fighter became weak and helpless before the envy and hatred existing among his own children. <span dir="rtl">מחריביך ומהרסיך ממך יצאו</span> “Thy destroyers and they that lay thee waste shall go forth from thee” (Isaiah xlix, 17). This curse forever pursued Jacob, and fastened on him through all the generations of exile.

## Joseph Endures Jacob’s Trials

<span dir="rtl">אלה תולדות יעקב</span>—<span dir="rtl">יוסף</span> “These are the progeny of Jacob—Joseph.” Jacob’s story is repeated in the life of his son. The utterly unique and vari-colored life of the father was further unfolded in the tale of Joseph. Not to Reuben, the firstborn, or the other sons, was given the need of experiencing again the extraordinary mishaps of Jacob’s career. The mighty billows of the ocean are not reflected in the quieter torrent of the Jordan. In the placider existence of the older brothers there was no inkling of inner and outer conflict. Joseph alone remained his prototype; only this son passed through the extraordinary turbulence of the patriarch’s vicissitudes.

<span dir="rtl">יוסף היה דומה לאביו בכל דבר, וכל מה שעבר על יעקב עבר על יוסף</span> “Joseph was in all matters like unto his father; and all that befell Jacob also befell Joseph.” <span dir="rtl">יעקב קנא בו עשו, ויוסף קנאו בו אחיו</span> “Jacob endured the malice of Esau (because of Isaac’s blessings), and Joseph suffered his brothers’ displeasure (because of the favor shown him by Jacob).”

<span dir="rtl">יעקב גלה לחרן בשביל אחיו, יוסף גלה למצרים בשביל אחיו</span> “Because of his brother did Jacob seek exile in Haran; because of his brothers was Joseph exiled to Egypt” (Tanhuma Vayeshev).

And what is more — <span dir="rtl">זה הוליד בנים בחוץ לארץ, וזה הוליד בנים בחוץ לארץ</span> “Jacob’s children were born in alien lands; and so were Joseph’s children” (Genesis Rabbah 84). Offspring born in exile are not so attached to their parents as those born in the ancestral land. The new and powerful influence exercised by novel surroundings tends to estrange the youth from fathers and mothers. As the years advance there are lessening understanding and association between the generations. Their outlooks are altogether dissimilar; their attitudes toward life are at utter variance.

## Brothers’ Quarrel

Joseph was very young when his father settled in Canaan, barely seventeen years old. As a <span dir="rtl">בן זקונים</span>, son of Jacob’s latter years, born to his favorite wife Rachel — he was especially beloved by his father. The father dressed him in silk and bestowed upon him a greater measure of tenderness. The other brothers hated the youth for it. Like all pampered children, Joseph dreamed of future greatness and rulership. He envisioned his supremacy, economic and spiritual, over his brothers; and he had no compunction about recounting these dreams to them and to his father.

He beheld them all <span dir="rtl">מאלמים אלמים... והנה קמה אלמתי וגם נצבה</span> “binding sheaves . . . and lo, my sheaf arose, and also placed itself upright” (xxxvii, 7). The other sheaves bowed down to his. This was to indicate that ultimately the others would come to him for economic succor. In the second dream the sun and moon and eleven stars bowed down to him—which symbolized his coming spiritual superiority. The recital of these night visions enhanced the hatred of the brothers, <span dir="rtl">ויגער בו אביו</span> Even “his father rebuked him,” demanding <span dir="rtl">מה החלום הזה אשר חלמת</span> “What is this (silly) dream that thou hast dreamed?” (10).

In the Yeshivah of Woloshin the following anecdote was related: A hassid once approached Rabbi Joseph Ber of Brisk to say that in a dream he had seen the other hassidim of the town elect him as their rabbi. To which the rabbi smiled: “You imbecile! If the townsmen had so dreamed about you, one might expect something ultimately to come of it; but when you dream about yourself, it has not the value of a sucked egg!”

The hatred of the brethren for Joseph increased steadily, and culminated in the tragic disposal of the youth to the Midianite traders, who later sold him into slavery in Egypt. Brothers selling brother into bondage, must culminate in calamity. In this case it conduced to the final enslavement of all in the Egyptian exile.

Chief Rabbi A. M. Amiel offers the following comment in his sermonic collection “To My People”: — “The tragic tale of the selling of Joseph has continued to the present day. It has appeared in altered forms in every generation. It is responsible not alone for the <span dir="rtl">עשרה הרוגי מלכות</span> the ten slaughtered saints described in the liturgy and Midrash, but for the direct or indirect slaughter of many additional thousands — the countless martyrs of Jewish history.

“To this day Jewry is wracked by internecine hatred and strife in all the lands of the exile. The greater tragedy lies in the fact that most of these divisions are predicated upon abstract ideas, inconsequential visions, purposeless dreams. <span dir="rtl">ויוסיפו עוד שנא אתו על חלמותיו ועל דבריו</span> ‘And they continued to hate him still more for his dreams, and for his words’ (8). They quarreled over vain words, over matters without living reality. Hatred exists first; then come the explanation and justification therefor.

“It is first related: <span dir="rtl">וישנאו אותו ולא יכלו דברו לשלום</span> ‘They hated him, and could not speak peaceably unto him’ (xxxvii, 4). There was no effort made toward mutual understanding. After this it is said: <span dir="rtl">ויקנאו בו אחיו</span> ‘And his brethren envied him’ (11). The distance between them grew steadily greater. <span dir="rtl">בטרם יקרב אליהם ויתנכלו אותו להמיתו</span> ‘Even before he approached unto them, they conspired against him to put him to death’ (18). No desire existed for rapprochement. There remained a baseless enmity, which ever leads but to the general destruction and discomfiture.”

## Fraternal Conflict and the Messiah

Our mystical literature, lost in the realms between heaven and earth, and finding moral teachings in every passage of the Torah, presents fantastic picturizations of the tragedy of Joseph. It admonishes us that in every misfortune assailing our people, the Jews themselves must accept the blame. Yet simultaneously it declares that the people need not be disquieted or despair, since God, ever mindful of his own, is preparing their salvation. <span dir="rtl">קודם שירדו השבטים למצרים, היו מבזין את בני השפחות ולא נהגו בהם מנהג אחוה</span> “Before the tribes went down into Egypt, they looked with disdain upon the descendants of the handmaids (Bilhah and Zilpah) and refused to deal with them in brotherly fashion.” This aroused the wrath of God, who thus proclaimed:

<span dir="rtl">אורידם למצרים ויהיו כלם עבדים ובשעה שאגלם יאמרו כלם עבדים היינו לפרעה, נמצא כלם שוים</span>

“I shall bring them down to Egypt, where they will all become slaves. And when I redeem them, they will say, ‘Slaves were we to Pharaoh’; and they will be everyone equal” (Pesikta Hadata).

Our faith cannot tolerate contempt of brother for brother. It is especially averse to those who look down upon those poorer than themselves. Such inimical attitudes undermine the fundaments of a nation, and bring to its enslavement by other peoples. Any nation whose members are in continual conflict, and where one section exalts itself above another, is so completely weakened as to find rehabilitation impossible. A group so constituted must seek for miracles, for a Messiah, to rid them of their inner and extraneous exilism, of the enemies within and without.

We are told that the light of the coming Messiah was kindled immediately after the selling of Joseph:

<span dir="rtl">השבטים עסוקים במכירת יוסף</span>; <span dir="rtl">ראובן עסוק בשקו ותעניתו</span>; <span dir="rtl">יהודה עסוק לישא אשה</span>; <span dir="rtl">והקב</span>”<span dir="rtl">ה בורא אורו של משיח</span> “The tribes of Israel are occupied in disposing of Joseph; Reuben (the elder) envelops himself in sackcloth, and fasts; Judah (the householder) seeks a new wife (a new home); but the Holy One, blessed be He, in the meanwhile occupies himself with kindling the light of the Messiah” (Genesis Rabbah, Vayeshev).

How perspicaciously has the Midrash interpreted the very time in which we are living!
