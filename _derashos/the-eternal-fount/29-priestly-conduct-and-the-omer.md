<span dir="rtl">ויאמר ה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">אל משה</span>: <span dir="rtl">אמור אל הכהנים בני אהרן</span>—<span dir="rtl">לנפש לא יטמא בעמיו</span> “And the Lord said unto Moses: Say unto the priests, the sons of Aaron. . .. There shall none be defiled by the dead among his peoples” (Leviticus xxi, 1). The priest conducting his holy offices in Tabernacle or Temple is the religious teacher and spiritual leader of his generation. <span dir="rtl">נזר אלהיו</span> “He bears the crown of his God.” Hence: <span dir="rtl">מה אני בקדושתי אף הוא בקדושתו</span> “As I remain in my holiness, so shall he remain in his holiness” and never be tainted with impurity (Sanhedrin Jer. ii, 1).

Holiness and purity are concepts intimately related in Jewish tradition. To be holy implies being clean of every blemish that may stay the process of spiritual progress. <span dir="rtl">מעלין בקודש ולא מורידין</span> “One must advance upward and not downward in observance of holiness.” Life itself implies upward progress — for only the living are enabled to climb to the heights destined them by God. Death is a quick descent, breaking the chain of human development. Death therefore impurifies all that it touches. And the sanctified priest must not come near the dead.

The chief requisite for any priest, rabbi, or other leader of Jewry, is to attain an understanding of the lofty ideal and principles of their Law, their faith. Each generation is a link in the long chain of Jewish achievement. It is therefore incumbent upon every Jewish leader to perform his proper share in developing the Jewish spirit. Priestly duties must apply more to the living than to the dead. <span dir="rtl">לא המתים יהללו יה</span> “The dead praise not the Lord.” They are part of yesterday, of the past. But Jewish ideals are concerned with tomorrow, with the future — they are the motivation of the living.

The repositories of the dead were never the prime interest of Judaism. The “kaddish” and memorial prayers were never reckoned the chief expressions of Jewish worship. Our synagogues and temples have always <span dir="rtl">בתי אלקים חי</span> “homes of the living God,” houses of study for the Law of Life (<span dir="rtl">תורת חיים</span>). The common people may permit themselves to be made impure by the dead, but not the priest or rabbi. Maharal of Prague was the first to name those engaged in service to the dead <span dir="rtl">חברא קדישא</span> “holy company” — because it was so difficult to find persons willing to participate in that labor. Hence did he ascribe holiness to them by this name, and also decreed that the community provide the group with an annual banquet, in gratitude for services rendered.

The priest must also be prepossessing in appearance and habit. In the Guide, Maimonides thus expounds the phrase <span dir="rtl">אשר יהיה בו מום לא יקרב</span> “For any man that hath a blemish shall not approach” (Leviticus xxi, 17). He who approaches the altar must be respected of the people, who will not give their esteem to one physically imperfect. Also, the priest must don <span dir="rtl">בגדים שהם מתוקנים לעבודה</span> “garments that are suitable for his holy services” (Yoma 43). <span dir="rtl">כהן גדול שלבש בגדי כהן הדיוט ועבד, עבודתו פסולה</span> “When a high priest dons the garments of an ordinary priest and performs his holy service, such service is invalid” (Zebahim 18). The chief spiritual leader must create a favorable impression upon his people. He must be most circumspect in his conduct <span dir="rtl">שלא לכנוס למקדש שתוי יין</span> “not enter the sanctuary befuddled by wine” (Keritot 13b). His family life must also be irreproachable, lest he become subject to gossip and consequent loss of respect.

<span dir="rtl">לא תחללו את שם קדשי, ונקדשתי בתוך בני ישראל</span> “Neither shall ye profane my holy name; in order that I be hallowed among the children of Israel” (xxii, 32). The chief rabbi of New York, Jacob Joseph of blessed memory, thus replied to a Reform rabbi who boasted that he was sanctifying the name of God by being invited to address Christian churches: “Against that sort of self-given permission, to desecrate the Sabbath and to weaken all our sanctities, for the purpose of possible ‘sanctification of the Name’ thereby — a most doubtful probability — the Torah has expressly warned us. It declares, ‘Neither shall ye profane My holy Name’; and then adds the phrase, ‘in order that I be hallowed among the children of Israel.’ This implies that if one desecrates God’s laws, ultimately sanctification of His Name will be sought not in Israel, but among alien peoples.”

## Counting the Omer; Festival of R. Simeon b. Yohai

Moses commanded the Jews, when they reached their own land, to bring an *omer*, a measure of the first products of the land, to the priest.

<span dir="rtl">כשהייתי נותן לכם את המן, הייתי נותן עומר לכל אחד ואחד מכם בכל יום, עכשיו הביאו לי עומר אחד משלכם</span>

“When I was giving you the manna (said God), I would give each one of you an omer every day; now bring Me at least one omer from all of you” (Vayikra Rabbah 28). The omer offering is designed as thanks to the Lord for the abundance bestowed on Israel in their own land, and as a remembrance of the food they ate in the wilderness. God does not demand tremendous sacrifices; but He desires men to remember that their possessions are not altogether their own, but given of the Lord — hence is it their duty to give of their own to those who have not.

<span dir="rtl">וספרתם לכם ממחרת השבת שבע שבתות תמימת תהיינה</span> “And ye shall count unto you from the morrow after the day of rest. . . . seven weeks shall be complete” (xxiii, 15). About this counting, which begins after the first day of Passover, there are varied explanations. Many maintain that it was the result of the eagerness of the people to receive the Torah, so that they counted the days with impatience between Passover and Shabuoth.

<span dir="rtl">בשעה שאמר להם משה, תעבדון את האלהים על ההר הזה</span>,

<span dir="rtl">אמרו לו ישראל, משה רבינו אימתי עבודה זו</span>? <span dir="rtl">אמר להם לסוף חמשים יום, והיו מונין כל אחד ואחד לעצמו, מכאן קבעו חכמים לספירת העמר</span>.

“When Moses commanded the Jews, Ye shall serve God on this mountain, they asked of him, When will our service begin? He answered, At the end of fifty days. Thereupon every Jew began counting the time day by day. And because of this the sages decreed that the counting be resumed every year” (Ran, comm. to Pesahim).

The Midrash comments on the phrase “seven weeks shall be complete.” <span dir="rtl">אימתי הן תמימות</span>? <span dir="rtl">בזמן שישראל עושין רצונו של מקום</span> “When will there be completeness? When Israel performs the will of God” (Vayikra Rabbah 28). If the people is eager to receive the Torah, they must remain throughout free from transgression. A day during which any sin is committed can not be reckoned in the number of the week; and to be fit for the Torah the weeks of the people must be complete, seven meritorious days.

In the work *Matteh Moses* we read the thought of Rabbenu Judah the Pious, that the Torah prescribed this counting of the Omer because in those days, between Passover and Shabuoth, the people were occupied in the fields; thus far from home and city, they might forget the passage of time and make an error in their reckoning of the day of Shabuoth. To avoid that error, they were to count each day as it passed.

The days of counting — Sefirah — are also in part days of sadness. Observant Jews arrange no occasions of joy for those days, for then thousands of the disciples of Rabbi Akiba were slain during the Bar Cochba insurrection against Rome (Yebamot 62b). Only on the thirty-third day — Lag BaOmer—is open festivity permitted. Then, says Maharil, the plague was lifted. Others offer the explanation that on that day the manna began to fall (Hatam Sefer, Yoreh Deah, 233).

To the kabbalists Lag BaOmer is the festal day of Rabbi Simeon ben Yohai, upon which he disclosed his divine secrets to his disciples, before his death (Zohar 291). To this day oriental Jews celebrate the festival at Meron near Safed, where Rabbi Simeon is buried. Thousands of men and women gather here, kindle a huge conflagration, and circle around it with torches, chanting <span dir="rtl">כלא הדא משום צדיק</span> “All this is in honor of the righteous man!”
