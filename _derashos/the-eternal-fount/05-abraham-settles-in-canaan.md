The time came when the patriarch was forced to cease his nomadic life, his frequent and aimless journeys, and to find a permanent habitation in Canaan. When Abraham and Isaac returned to Beer Sheba from the scene of the son’s binding for sacrifice, Sarah was not to be found there. She had departed for Hebron in search of them; and in that city she had died.

<span dir="rtl">ראה מלאך המות מתרים כנגדו</span> “Abraham beheld Death blocking his path” (Genesis Rabbah 8).

How could he leave the body of his beloved wife in that place, and proceed on his way? His life companion, she had gone with him everywhere and shared his every trial. She had more than once risked her life to save his — as in Egypt before Pharaoh, and in Gerar before Abimelech. More than this, she had been his partner in his most compelling purpose — that of spreading the divine ideal. While Abraham labored with the men to bring them to God, Sarah was devoting herself to the women (<span dir="rtl">אברהם מגייר אנשים ושרה מגיירת נשים</span> “Abraham was converting the men, while Sarah converted the women” — Rashi to Lech Lecha). It was unthinkable that he should forever separate from her, even in death. He must settle in that spot forever, and provide a last resting place for her and for himself.

From the moment that God had bade him forsake his city Haran, where he and his father had established themselves, and journey <span dir="rtl">אל הארץ אשר אראך</span> “to the land which I shall show thee,” Abraham had traveled without cease. He had visited many lands and many cities. Haran to Shechem to Elon Mamreh to Bethel; then, when famine broke out, to Egypt, and back to Bethel, Hebron, Gerar, and Beer Sheba.

He found no pleasure in most of the lands he visited, and uttered the frequent prayer: <span dir="rtl">הלואי זה יהיה חלקי בארץ הזאת</span> “Would that I be not destined to dwell in this land.” For those that pleased him he spoke the opposing formula: “Would that I be destined to dwell in this land.” But he remained uncertain as to the spot most suitable for his permanent home. God had not vouchsafed the proper symbol; therefore he persisted in his travels from place to place (Genesis Rabbah 39).

When friends and followers accompanying him reiterated <span dir="rtl">הארץ היכן היא</span> “Where is this land promised by God? Why does He not indicate the site whereon you may make an end of your wanderings?” — Abraham would reply, “When you reach a place where the dead are buried, you will know that there are the confines of the land we seek” (Yalkut Shimeoni).

“In this wise,” commented Dr. Shmaryahu Levin, “Abraham instructed his children that, in order to take over a land and make of it their national home, a nation must be prepared to offer the greatest of sacrifices, even that of life itself.” The death of the matriarch Sarah provided the first grave to be filled in the new land, and then began the real settlement of the country. Abraham established himself there, and built his home, to become the permanent inheritance of his descendants.

## History Repeats

In the narrative told in the present portion, of Abraham’s acquisition of the land, of his dealings with the “Arabs” of that day and their “Mufti,” Ephron the Hittite, one sees mirrored the same difficulties confronted by our people in the current strivings after Palestine. The huge problem of Palestinian settlement, undertaken by the Zionist Organization, the task of converting an obstinate terrain into a national Jewish home, meets with hardships similar to those which troubled Abraham thousands of years ago.

Abraham did not come to Canaan empty-handed. <span dir="rtl">כבד מאד במקנה בכסף ובזהב</span> “He was very rich in cattle, in silver, and in gold” (Genesis xiii, 2). He bore with him all the wealth he had accumulated in exile. He was determined that when at length he reached the land indicated by God, he should have with him the goods and capital whereby he could develop it to the benefit of himself and his neighbors.

Although Abraham had a divine historic right to the site, since God had promised <span dir="rtl">לזרעך אתן את הארץ הזאת</span> “Unto thy seed will I give this land,” the patriarch made no effort to exercise this right against anyone. He asked of the native inhabitants: <span dir="rtl">ופגעו לי בעפרון בן צחר</span> “Intreat for me to Ephron the son of Zohar” (xxiii, 8). <span dir="rtl">סרסוני לי</span> “Become my intermediaries” (Genesis Rabbah 48). His purpose was to bring all to a round table where they might amicably arrange a satisfactory method of developing the countryside.

## The Holy Places

In these land dealings between Abraham and the sons of Heth, the matter of national shrines at once presented itself. The natives were troubled lest, when Abraham’s descendants took fuller possession of the soil, they might profane their holy places. Hence they demanded:

<span dir="rtl">כרות עמנו שבועה שאין בני ישראל יורשים את עיר יבום</span> “Give us your oath and covenant that the children of Israel will not take possession of the city Jabus.” This city contained their sacred copper idols. Abraham swore to respect their desires in this matter. He promised that his descendants would make no claim to Jabus so long as its inhabitants would not willingly become part of the commonwealth (Pirke D’Rabbi Eliezer, ch. 36).

A similar difficulty exists today. Because of declared fears lest Mohammedan and Christian shrines be profaned, Great Britain has found it requisite to occupy all the cities which for long generations have been sanctified by Jewish history. Even the holiest of cities, Jerusalem, where the Temple once stood, and from whose precincts our great prophets and teachers spread the word of God throughout the world, is in peril today of being completely excluded from the Jewish state.

To justify this proposal, unwarranted by history and the Mandate — which simply and unequivocally proclaims its purpose as the establishment of a Jewish national home in our holy land —Britain has covered its purpose by the deceptive title, “corridor.” The Empire wishes to create the impression that in this dissociation of the capital city from Jewish rule the intent is to help the Jews, to protect them from possible raids by the Arabs. But thereby there are excluded from the Jewish homeland many important historic spots; and the Jewish state is shrunk to five million dunams, far too little for the uses of our people.

## Abraham Pays Well for Cemetery Ground

For the ground in which Abraham buried his wife Sarah, he was content to pay <span dir="rtl">בכסף מלא</span> “its full value in silver.” Waste land, plots neglected for centuries were purchased at truly exorbitant values.

This was the answer Abraham received to his offers: <span dir="rtl">במבחר קברינו קבר את מתיך</span>—<span dir="rtl">מתים הרבה</span> “You may bury your many dead in our choicest graves” (Genesis Rabbah 58). The natives were highly pleased to dispose of their useless ground to the Jews at excessive rates. Compare the modern efforts to make the land healthy, to dry up swamps and plague spots, to build highways for easier transport, during which countless loyal Palestinian pioneers have dug their own graves in the waste territories — for which they too had expended inordinate sums.

The flourishing colonies created by the Jews, their many new industries, have provided gainful occupations for the native Arabs. Through Jewish sacrifices these people have attained health and riches. The sanitary innovations of our own Hadassah organization are responsible for the rapid growth of new Arab population. Yet they continue to rant against us and place obstacles in the way of our further progress.

## Prince of God — Not Permanent Resident

<span dir="rtl">אומרים הרבה ואפילו מעט אינם עושים</span> “They promise much, but perform not even a little” (Baba Mezia 87). For every added plot Abraham was required to pay <span dir="rtl">כסף עובר לסוחר</span> “price beyond the value of the purchase.” This became a hardship to him. When he presented himself as a permanent resident, in the words <span dir="rtl">תושב, אנכי עמכם</span> they replied: <span dir="rtl">נשיא אלקים אתה בתוכנו</span> “Nay, thou art a prince of God in the midst of us.” This may be paraphrased, if you wish to remain a religious minority in our land, devoted simply to service of God and study of His Law, you will be welcome among us. Thus do the Arab leaders today protest that they have no objection to Jews settling in Palestine as a religious group; but to permit their permanent settlement, their ingress in large numbers to build up the country as their national home — this is no more to be permitted than it was by the sons of Heth in olden days.

Abraham did not lose heart, however. He employed every righteous effort in dealing with the sons of Heth. The patriarch expended large sums to acquire new soil, slowly consolidating his position and coming nearer to his goal.

<span dir="rtl">ויקם שדה עפרון אשר במכפלה אשר לפני ממרא השדה והמערה אשר בו וכל-העץ אשר בשדה אשר בכל-גבלו סביב</span>; <span dir="rtl">לאברהם למקנה</span>

“And the field of Ephron, which was in Machpelah, which was before Mamre, the field, and the cave which was therein, and all the trees that were in the field, that were in all the boundaries round were made sure unto Abraham for a purchase. ...” (xxii, 17-18).

So too in our own day, if the Jews remain unified and determined in their efforts toward recreating the homeland, ignoring obstacles and fighting unceasingly for the right, we shall ultimately overcome every hardship; and the land promised by God to Abraham for his descendants will again be ours, from Dan to Beer Sheba.
