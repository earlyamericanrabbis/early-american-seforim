“Get thee out of thy land, and out of thy birthplace” thus begins the story of Abraham; and the same destiny has followed his descendants during the long generations of their existence. <span dir="rtl">מה שאירע לאברהם אירע לבניו</span> “That which happened unto Abraham also happened to his descendants.”

Abraham was called “the Hebrew” — <span dir="rtl">אברהם העברי</span> — because <span dir="rtl">כל העולם מעבר אחד והוא מעבר אחד</span> “The whole world is on one side (ever, from which ‘Hebrew’ is derived), and he is on the other” (Genesis Rabbah 44). His descendants are also called Hebrews — they are opposed to the entire world, while the world in its entirety opposes them.

As a child Abraham repudiated any recognition of the mighty Nimrod as a god. For this rebellion he was hurled into a furnace, whence he was rescued by a miracle. His offspring also steadfastly refused to accept despotic man-gods or other forms of idolatry; for this they were burnt in pyres and auto-da-fés, to be saved by newer miracles.

And their salvation has always come from God alone. Michael, Gabriel, and their fellow angels are pictured as entreating God to permit their descent to earth, to rescue Abraham from the furnace. But the Divine answer is: <span dir="rtl">אני יחיד בעולמי והוא יחיד בעולמו, נאה ליחיד להציל את היחיד</span> “I am unique in my world, and he is unique in his world; it is proper that He who is One shall succor him who is also one” (Yalkut Shimeoni). Abraham’s progeny received many offers in later generations to be aided in their need by strangers; but they never consented to live by <span dir="rtl">חסד לאומים</span> “the favor of alien peoples,” preferring to seek protection under the wings of their one God.

Because of Nimrod’s persecutions in Ur-Kasdim, their birthplace, Terah, Abraham’s father, decided to betake himself with his children to the land of Canaan. But <span dir="rtl">ויבאו עד חרן וישבו שם</span> “they came unto Haran, and abode there.” Similarly do Abraham’s progeny, following a great national calamity, determine to migrate to “Canaan” or Eretz Israel to rebuild their national home; but for economic reasons they halt on the way in strange lands, which again become their Haran, land of ire and persecution (Rashi thus plays on the words Haran and baron, “anger” (<span dir="rtl">חרון אף של עולם</span>).

Evil indeed are the effects of the exile on the entire existence of Israel: <span dir="rtl">הדרך ממעטת פו</span>”<span dir="rtl">ר ממעטת את הממון, וממעטת את השם</span> “Wandering diminishes the number of children, the amount of one’s riches, and the Divine Name” (Genesis Rabbah 44).

A most appropriate comment to this midrash is provided by Rabbi Isaac Nissenbaum:

“The exile lessens our numbers. At the time of the second destruction, there were about six million Jewish souls. According to natural growth our people in this day should have numbered a hundred million at the minimum. But the horrible persecutions of the exile, the murderous pogroms which also created epidemics of apostacy, retarded our growth, so that we today count less than eighteen millions in all.

“In addition, exile has done harm to our worldly possessions. The energy and industry of the Jewish people led to the amassing of many great fortunes in their thousands of years on earth. Yet we remain the most poverty stricken group existent, for conditions of exile have made it impossible for us to retain any of our capital.

“Added to all this, our wanderings are responsible for injury to our glorious name. Out of the ‘chosen people’ of early days, which gave the world distinguished prophets, faith, and ideals, has in the passing of time arisen <span dir="rtl">שה פזורה ישראל</span> ‘lost sheep,’ always among savage wolves, <span dir="rtl">ללעג וקלס בגויים</span> derision and sport among the nations,” deprived of all human rights.”

Jews have a law: <span dir="rtl">מי שהחשיך לו בדרך מוסר כיסו לנכרי</span> “A man who finds himself on the road late Friday as it is becoming dark, should turn his purse over to a gentile (who may carry it on the Sabbath).” This is a rule Jews have always observed. So soon as their way in exile has grown dark, they have transferred their possessions to the gentiles — and barely escaped with their lives.

## Covenant of the Portions

Abraham, heeding the voice of the Lord, began the journey to the land of which God had spoken. But he looked upon a people torn into various economic and political parties <span dir="rtl">עגלה משולשת, עז משולשת, ואיל משולש</span> “calf, goat, and ram cut into pieces,” each part disparate and existing as a distinct entity. <span dir="rtl">ויתן איש בתרו לקראת רעהו</span> “And he sought to bring the various pieces together.” He was troubled too about <span dir="rtl">צפור לא בתר</span> the birds that were not split. These were the young birds, the little children, whose spirits had not yet been riven, but who would some day be torn in spirit as were their elders. And <span dir="rtl">אימה חשכה</span> “a dark fear” — fell upon him.

He heard the enemies of Israel say to them:

<span dir="rtl">כתבו על קרן השור שאין לכם חלק באלקי ישראל</span> “Inscribe upon the horn of the ox that ye have no share in the God of Israel” (Genesis Rabbah 44). If you wish to have any use of the fat ox, of all the economic privileges of our lands, then repudiate your faith, your Torah, and adopt our own religion. <span dir="rtl">ותרדמה נפלה על אברהם</span> “And a deep sleep fell upon Abraham.”

The maggid of Dubnow says: One of the greatest misfortunes of Jewry has always been that in a difficult or opportune period, instead of remaining alert and laboring with all energy for liberation, the Jews are enveloped in a <span dir="rtl">תרדמת מרמיטה</span> lethargic sleep — hands folded, doing nothing for their people.

The result has been: <span dir="rtl">ועבדום וענו אותם בארץ לא להם</span> “And they enslaved and afflicted them in a land that was not theirs.” Jews are not alone sufferers at the hands of natives of the lands they inhabit, but they are enslaved and tormented even by the immigrant groups, who do not own the land, newcomers, who nevertheless take a proprietary attitude toward the country and assail the Jews (Midrash Samuel).

In his despair Abraham cried to the Lord, <span dir="rtl">מה תתן לי ואנכי הולך ערירי</span> “What canst Thou give me, seeking I go childless!” How shall I be able to fullfill your commands, the vast undertaking of settling and building up the land, when the people is torn into pieces, and no one hears me? (The Hebrew anri may be translated “alone” as well as “childless.”)

As he stood there, addressing the Lord, Abraham observed <span dir="rtl">לפיד אש עבר בין הגזרים</span> “a torch of fire pass between the fragments.” The hellish flames of hatred and persecution pass between the portions of the people and unite them. Then did God complete a covenant with the people. <span dir="rtl">לזרעך נתתי את הארץ הזאת</span> “To thy seed do I give this land”: <span dir="rtl">מנהר מצרים עד-הנהר הגדול נהר פרת</span> “from the river of Egypt unto the great river, the river Euphrates.” That is to say, I give them as their own, for their national home, a complete territory, not one divided into small parts.

We may well conclude with a witticism of Rabbi Berdichever, in commenting on a passage from this portion:

In the week during which Lech Lecha is read, the rabbi was going about to collect money for an indigent bride. He approached a miserly magnate who, instead of offering a handsome donation, contented himself with blessing Berdichever and felicitating the young pair. Upon which the rabbi exclaimed: “In this week’s portion we find the phrase <span dir="rtl">ומלכי צדק הוציא לחם ויין ויברכהו</span> ‘And Melchizedek. . . .brought forth bread and wine. And he blessed him’ (Genesis xiv, 18, 19). You see that Melchizedek first provided food and drink for Abraham, and then blessed him. Begging your pardon, first give me something to defray the cost of the wedding, and then you may offer all the felicitations your heart desires.”
