<span dir="rtl">ויחי יעקב בארץ מצרים שבע עשרה שנה</span> “And Jacob lived in the land of Egypt seventeen years” (xlvii, 28). When Jacob returned to Canaan, it is written: <span dir="rtl">וישב יעקב בארץ מגורי אביו</span> “And Jacob abode in the land of his father’s sojourning” (xxxvii, 1). In exile Jacob was able to exist for a limited period, only temporarily, but not to establish himself as a permanent householder. Life in alien parts was without fundament, lacking the freedom and independence created by a settled home. No one can enjoy a life spent at the tables of others. Only his physical hunger can thus be sated, but there still remains the suffering of the spirit. Perpetual subjection to strange influences is a narrowing and deadening experience. Such an existence is well called <span dir="rtl">ויחי</span> “and he lived,” and not <span dir="rtl">וישב</span> “and he abode.”

The Midrash inquires: <span dir="rtl">למה פרשה זו סתומה מכל הפרשיות של תורה</span>? “Why is this portion conjoined with the other Pentateuchal portions (and not distinct, like them)?” It answers, profoundly: <span dir="rtl">משום שהתחילה שעבוד מצרים על ישראל</span> “Because herewith began Israel’s subjection to Egypt” and there was an end of Jacob’s distinctiveness and independence (Genesis Rabbah 96). The portion cannot stand by itself; it is bound to the others. Jacob’s life is bound up with the life of Egypt; exile destroys his every unique quality.

<span dir="rtl">מצרת השעבוד נסתמו עיניהם ולבם של ישראל</span> “Through the pressure of exile the eyes and hearts of Israel were trammeled” (Rashi). Far worse than the fact of enslavement or subjection is the closing of eye and heart to their own degradation. Israel became habituated to their lowly position as aliens, strangers, men of the bowed head. The Zohar aptly remarks:

<span dir="rtl">צלמא דבר נש אשתחשך, ודיוקנא דאתכזי בארעא אתמענת</span> “The visage of an alien dweller is darkened, and the bright countenance seen in his own land becomes cheerless and depressed.”

Seventeen years did Jacob reside in Egypt with his entire family, accomplishing nothing. There is no record of achievement on their part, at a time when momentous events were taking place in the country, when great masses of the population were being transported to new settlements, and the entire economic structure was being altered. During all these years Jacob lived quietly in Goshen with his family, on Joseph’s bounty. Almost a generation of time spent in nothingness! If they made any contribution at all to the land’s progress, it was undoubtedly as Egyptians, as indigenous citizens and not as Israelites. There is no credit given them for accomplishment in any of our archives; even their names are not mentioned.

Jacob became ill; his days were numbered. Thus he called for his son Joseph: <span dir="rtl">בשבילך ירדתי למצרים</span> “On thy account did I go down unto Egypt.” <span dir="rtl">אל נא תקברני במצרים</span> “Do not, I pray thee, bury me in Egypt.” <span dir="rtl">אפילו אדם מת בספינה הנפש הולכת אל אבותיו</span> “Even though a man die on shipboard, his soul turns back to his parents” (Midrash Tanhuma). In this manner Jacob explained to Joseph that since Joseph had expatriated his father, it was as though the older man dwelt on a ship, without solid foundation, and susceptible to every billowy eruption. A nation thus insecurely placed cannot progress. The man who perforce resides on the sea is practically without living or achieving; but his spiritual part ever reverts to his forbears.

Jacob would not be interred in Egypt — <span dir="rtl">שלא יעשו אותו עבודה-זרה</span> “lest they employ his memory for idol-worship” (Genesis Rabbah 96). Sufficient was it that all his life’s labors were somehow transformed into activity for aliens and were not accounted to his people’s credit. Hence he desired that in the end he should be buried in his own land and thus be credited to his people. <span dir="rtl">מי שמת בחוץ-לארץ ונקבר שם</span>—<span dir="rtl">שתי מיתות יש בידו</span> “He who dies in exile and is there buried, has died a double death” (Midrash Tanhuma). He has perished once for the present, which is no longer his, and once for the future, which is also not his own. Neither his life nor his death are accounted to his people.

## The Divine Presence Goes and Returns

Jacob summoned all his children, and spoke:

<span dir="rtl">האספו ואגידה לכם את אשר-יקרא אתכם באחרית הימים</span>

“Gather yourselves together, that I may tell you that which shall befall you in the remoteness of days” (xlix, 1). Comments the Midrash: <span dir="rtl">היו אגודה אחת, התקינו עצמכם לגאולה</span> “Be as one company; prepare yourselves for the redemption” (Genesis Rabbah 98). “The remoteness of days” is for the Jew completely dependent upon the “gather yourselves together.” If the Jews will unite, and in all lands strive to be one company, with a single aim, then their future, their latter days, will be secured. Men must be prepared for redemption; this entails a conscious effort.

Shmaryahu Levin once offered this comment on the talmudic injunction, <span dir="rtl">אל תגעו במשיחי אלו תינוקות של בית רבן</span>—“Do not molest my Messiahs — these are the pupils in the school of their master.” “The Jewish Messiah,” said he, “will not come from one man or a group of men. All the mothers of Israel must travail for our redeemer. The entire people must provide their strongest aid toward the freeing and redemption of our nation.”

The Talmud (Pesahim 56) has presented an extraordinary picture of the last hours of the patriarch Jacob: <span dir="rtl">בקש יעקב לגלות לבניו את קץ הימין, ונסתלקה ממנו שכינה</span> “Jacob wished to uncover to his children the end of the exile, but the Divine Presence had departed from him.” The divine vision he had experienced before his sons were gathered around him, vanished with their presence. Jacob, terrified, <span dir="rtl">אמר חס ושלום יש במטתי פסול</span> “said, ‘God forbid, there may be unworthiness in my family!’ ” There might be, among children and grandchildren, such as no longer had faith in the God of their fathers; and because of this, God’s Presence might have departed — It would not remain in the association of heretics, unbelievers, deniers of the Jewish concept of Divine Unity.

The sons, noting his distress, understood its origin.

<span dir="rtl">אמרו שמע ישראל ה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">אלהינו ה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">אחד</span>—<span dir="rtl">כשם שאין בלבך אלא אחד, כך אין בלבנו אלא אחד</span>

“They recited, ‘Hear, O Israel, the Eternal is our God, the Eternal is one — just as in your heart there is but one God, so in our own there exists but the one God.’ ” They quieted his qualms, so that he would suspect none of them of infidelity. All possessed the same faith, held the same aims. When Jacob heard these words, <span dir="rtl">פתח ואמר, ברוך שם כבוד מלכותו לעולם ועד</span> “He began and spoke, ‘Blessed be the name of His glorious kingdom for ever and ever!’”

The Talmud here speaks most sagely. Jacob, on his deathbed, his hours numbered, was passing away in a strange land, leaving his offspring in exile, among worshipers of idols. He could not know whether his pure faith, for which he and his forbears had suffered and striven so greatly, would not die out with himself. It was terrifying to contemplate the Divine Presence, which had accompanied him in all his wanderings, forsaking his children and exposing them to every peril of apostacy and assimilation among their Egyptian neighbors. <span dir="rtl">חמא למרחוק ואזדעזע</span> “He gazed into the future and was appalled” (Genesis Rabbah 98).

But he heard the glorious prayer that came from the lips of his children, the prayer that in brief resonance recounted the great ideal of the lives of the patriarchs — “Hear, O Israel!” Once more he breathed freely. His prophetic eye saw his descendants to the farthest generations leaping into flame and water with these words on their lips, to uphold the sanctities of their faith. His life had not been in vain. His divine ideal was to live in his descendants “forever and ever!” Now the Divine Presence returned and stood at the head of his bed.
