For thirty-four years Jacob did not see his home in Canaan. Fourteen years were spent on the tortuous road between Beer-sheba and Aram-Naharaim; fourteen in service of Laban, for his daughters Rachel and Leah; and six in labor on his own account, during which Jacob amassed great riches.

Laban had been quite poor when the young man first came to him. His daughter Rachel, despite her higher social place, was constrained to serve as his shepherd. Her brothers were too young, and there were no funds for engaging a pastor (Genesis Rabbah 63). Jacob’s advent completely altered his circumstances; he soon achieved great wealth. Jacob himself remarked:

<span dir="rtl">כי מעט אשר-היה לך לפני ויפרץ לרב</span> “For a little which thou hadst before me, is now increased unto a multitude” (xxx, 30). This had been admitted by Laban: <span dir="rtl">נחשתי ויברכני ה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">בגללך</span> “I have divined that the Eternal hath blessed me for thy sake” (27).

While Jacob labored unremittingly for Laban:

<span dir="rtl">הייתי ביום אכלני חרב וקרח בלילה ותדד שנתי מעיני</span>

“Where I was by day the drought consumed me, and the frost by night; and my sleep departed from mine eyes” (xxxi, 40) —Laban and his sons were altogether satisfied with him. His honesty gratified them. <span dir="rtl">טרפה לא-הבאתי אליך אנכי אחטנה</span> “That which was torn I brought not unto thee: I bare the loss of it” (39).

But when Jacob began working for himself — <span dir="rtl">ויפרץ האיש מאד</span> “and the man increased exceedingly” (xxx, 43) — they grew envious of him. They chose to forget that he had also brought riches to them. Contrariwise they complained: <span dir="rtl">לקח יעקב את כל אשר לאבינו, ומאשר לאבינו עשה את הכבד הזה</span> “Jacob hath taken all that was our father’s; and of that which was our father’s hath he made all this glory” (xxxi, 1). Laban later spoke similarly: <span dir="rtl">הבנות בנותי, והבנים בני, והצאן צאני, וכל אשר אתה ראה לי הוא</span> “These daughters are my daughters, and these children are my children, and these flocks are my flocks, and all that thou seest is mine” (xxxi, 43).

Such is the sad fate of the stranger, the alien. Though he bring joy and wealth to the land of his settlement, no sooner has he become openly affluent than he is accused of sharp dealing and is the object of envy.

Yet his wealth does bring about an alteration. It creates a revolution in his thought. There is less idealism and more materialism in his soul. Jacob, poor, and finding little rest in the stones of the wilderness, dreamed of heaven, of angels, of God. But in his new affluence his dreams were of another sort: <span dir="rtl">וארא בחלום והנה העתודים העולים על הצאן עקודים נקודים וברדים</span> “And I saw in a dream, and, behold, the rams which went up on the flocks were ringstraked, speckled, and grisled” (xxxi, 10). Heaven was forgotten; his visions now embraced more riches, a greater income.

Suddenly there came to him the voice of God:

<span dir="rtl">חמיך אין מסביר לך פנים, שוב אל ארץ אבותיך ואהיה עמך</span> “Your father-in-law will no longer tolerate you; return to your fatherland, and I shall be with you” (Genesis Rabbah 64). He was to know that the wealth he had attained in that place would not remain in his possession; it would be taken from him. <span dir="rtl">נכסי חוץ לארץ אין בהם ברכה</span> “Possessions gathered in exile have no blessing or permanence.” In his own land his earnings would remain with him; there would be an assured future for himself and his offspring.

## Jacob Departs for Canaan

Jacob came to an understanding with Laban, arranged a pact of friendship, and with wives, children, slaves, and flocks, set out for his parents’ home in Canaan. But Laban remained his enemy. He could not forgive Jacob’s departure, nor the goods he had amassed. Immediately after Jacob’s going, he despatched a secret to Esau by his son Beur. This read: “Have you heard of the trick Jacob played me? He came to me poor and tattered. I welcomed him most heartily, giving him my two daughters and two maid-servants as wives. Because of my intervention, God brought him good fortune. But now that he has become rich, he has left me and is on the way to Canaan. You can find him in the hills near the river Jabok; all his goods are with him. Do with him as you see fit.”

But his mother Rebeccah learned of this message, and herself despatched messengers to Jacob to warn him to be cautious. She counseled him to speak frankly to his brother, and not adopt a tone of haughtiness. “It is your duty,” were her words, “to respect your older brother. Perhaps God will soften his heart, and his wrath against you will abate” (Yalkut Sippurim).

Jacob sent out men to uncover Esau’s whereabouts, and to ascertain what plans his brother might be making for a meeting. They reported, sorrowfully: <span dir="rtl">באנו אל אחיך אל עשו, היית אומר</span>

<span dir="rtl">אחיך הוא, אבל הוא נוהג עמך כעשו הרשע, עודנו בשנאתו</span> “We came to your brother Esau. You imagined that he bethought himself as a brother, but he still remains the wicked Esau, still utterly hating you” (Genesis Rabbah 71). They added the alarming news that Esau was leading an army of four hundred men to attack him.

But Jacob feared not. He knew that when God commanded him to return to his home, He had promised <span dir="rtl">והייתי עמך</span> “And I shall be with thee.” Hence Esau could do no harm to Jacob. But the promise of God did not cover the children: <span dir="rtl">כי ירא אנכי אתו פן יבא והכני אם על בנים</span> “For I fear him, lest he will come and smite me, and the mother with the children”; <span dir="rtl">הצילני נא מיד אחי מיד עשו</span> “Deliver me, I pray Thee, from the hand of my brother, from the hand of Esau” (xxxii, 11). On this passage Rabbi Joseph Ber of Brisk commented: “Jacob sought protection from Esau’s hand, in that he sought to avoid physical assault; and also in the sense that extending the hand of fellowship too cordially might cause intermingling of the families and a consequent deterioration of the Hebraic idealism of Jacob’s family.”

## Gifts to Esau

To achieve Esau’s favor, Jacob knew, required great expenditure. He should have to provide <span dir="rtl">לדורון לתפלה ולמלחמה</span> the several contigencies of bribery, entreaty, or conflict. The Esaus must always be well paid for their generosity in not molesting Jacob. The very fact that Esau organizes no pogrom or economic boycott against him should make Jacob most happy and grateful.

Jacob chose a present for his brother —goats, sheep, camels, kine, she-asses, and foals, <span dir="rtl">עדר עדר לבדו</span> “every drove by itself” (xxxii, 16). <span dir="rtl">למה לא הכניסן כלן כאחת</span> “Why did he not deliver them in one group?” <span dir="rtl">כדי להשביע עיניו של רשע</span> “In order to sate the eyes of the wicked man” (Genesis Rabbah 76). Esau’s eyes were large — he would not be satisfied by a single bribe or present, but must have a succession of them.

<span dir="rtl">ויצו את הראשון לאמר כי יפגשך עשו אחי ושאלך לאמר למי אתה ואנה תלך ולמי אלה לפניך. ואמרת לעבדך ליעקב מנחה היא שלוחה לאדני לעשו</span>

“And he commanded the first, saying, When Esau, my brother, meeteth thee, and asketh thee, saying, Whose art thou? and whither goest thou? and whose are these before thee? Then thou shalt say, They are thy servant Jacob’s; it is a present sent unto my lord Esau” (xxxii, 17-18). This was to indicate that Jacob was not the equal of Esau, born of the same parents; but that Esau was the lord, and Jacob even as his subject.

But Jacob also made preparations for combat, if needed. He did in truth prefer to avoid conflict, and settle the matter by money payment, but despite his pacific character, he was prepared to fight should nothing else avail. He divided his caravan strategically into two processions, <span dir="rtl">והוא עבר לפניהם</span> “And he himself passed before them” (xxxiii, 3). On this the Midrash comments: <span dir="rtl">כהדא תרנגולתא דמנערה אגפה מן קיטמא</span> “Like the hen shaking sand from its wings” (Genesis Rabbah 75). The midrashic means that just as the hen cannot cleanse herself of the sand in which she has been sitting without rising and shaking herself, so could not Jacob obtain true salvation without rising and shaking off the dust of exile, without fighting for freedom.

Esau’s conduct was in character. He showed himself at first to be most generous and friendly. He embraced Jacob and spoke: <span dir="rtl">יש לי רב אחי, יהי לך אשר לך</span> “I have enough, my brother; be thine that which is thine” (xxxiii, 9). But Jacob understood that there was deceit in Esau’s kiss, that his thought was still of enmity. <span dir="rtl">ויפצר בו ויקח</span> “And he pressed him (Esau) and he took it.” Esau favored his brother by accepting his gifts.

## Jacob Remains Alone

Jacob gathered together his goods, sent his wives and children across the Jabok, and remained in that spot. <span dir="rtl">נשתייר על פכין קטנים</span> “He remained because of certain minor goods he had not put together.” The Talmud reproaches him for becoming like those who <span dir="rtl">חביב עליהם ממונם יותר מגופם</span> “favor their money over their physical well-being” (Hullin 91). It is averred that when Jacob became primarily concerned with his wealth, his material possessions, <span dir="rtl">ויותר יעקב לבדו</span>, he remained alone. His family departed distantly from him, and he was doomed to a lonely old age.

<span dir="rtl">תלמיד חכם אל ישא יחידי בלילה</span> “A man of learning should not travel alone by night” (Hullin 91). “This is an indication,” my father used to say, “of the tragedy of modern orthodox Jewry. The gloom of exile has brought great harm to our people. Alien influences have taken the children from the parents. Had the pious parents taken a strong enough interest in the upbringing of their young, had they sought to inculcate an abiding affection for their people’s faith and tradition, our life and our future might now be faced with greater assurance.

“But when they are interested solely in their businesses, and money becomes more necessary than health — even when they enter a synagogue or perform a pious deed, they remain alone —without their children. Our synagogues become homes for the aged; our institutions remain alive only through some miracle. It is no wonder, then, that when the night of old age comes upon us the fathers and mothers remain alone in the gloom, deserted by the youth that live in another world entirely.”

<span dir="rtl">ויאבק איש עמו</span> “And there wrestled a man with him.” This implies the struggle in Jacob’s mind. After long thought he concluded <span dir="rtl">ותקע כף ירך יעקב</span> “and the hollow of Jacob’s thigh was sprained” — meaning that the <span dir="rtl">יוצאי ירכו</span> “issue of his loins” had departed from him. The struggles for economic security in his younger years <span dir="rtl">העלו אבק ברגליהם עד כסא הכבוד</span> “had created a dust which reached unto heaven” (Hullin 91). Reform, assimilation, and other alien movements had insinuated themselves between children and parents and brought destruction upon their faith.

When Jacob sought to ascertain the reasons for this denouement, he asked the being wrestling with him, <span dir="rtl">הגידה-נא שמך</span> “Tell me, I pray thee, thy name.” But the only reply was: <span dir="rtl">למה זה תשאל לשמי</span> “Wherefore is it that thou dost ask after my name?”

<span dir="rtl">אין לנו שם קבוע</span> “We have no established name;” <span dir="rtl">משתנין שמותינו</span> “our names interchange” (Genesis Rabbah 71). In every land assimilation bears a different name, a different character. The various reform movements, so-called, have merely shattered the unity of Israel. Instead of existing as <span dir="rtl">עם ישראל</span>, “the people of Israel,” we are become “every drove by itself” varied sects and associations, without unity.

The patriarch Jacob struggled with the stranger <span dir="rtl">עד עלות השחר</span> “until the breaking of the day.” He would not surrender. “Jacob” became “Israel.” His private Jacob interests were no longer more important; as Israel he was now concerned with the entire community. <span dir="rtl">שרית עם-אלהים ועם אנשים ותוכל</span> “Thou hast contended with God and with men, and hast prevailed.”

Although <span dir="rtl">צלע על ירכו</span> “he halted upon his thigh;” he limped in the matter of his children’s training, <span dir="rtl">ויזרח לו השמש כאשר עבר את פנואל</span> “The sun shone upon him as he passed over Penuel” on the way to his own land. <span dir="rtl">שמש שבאה בעבורו, זרחה בעבורו</span> “The sun which sank for him (when he went into exile), shone for him once more (when he returned to his ancestral national home)” (Hullin 91).
