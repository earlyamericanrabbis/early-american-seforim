Two hundred and ten years had passed since Jacob and his descendants, on invitation of Joseph, had descended into Egypt. Only seventy souls had they been — and these included Joseph and his children. Now they had grown into a nation of six hundred thousand.

At first all had gone well with them. They were given the land of Goshen by Joseph. <span dir="rtl">וישב ישראל בארץ מצרים בארץ גשן ויאחזו בה ויפרו וירבו מאד</span> “And Israel abode in the land of Egypt, in the country of Goshen; and they had possessions therein, and were fruitful, and multiplied exceedingly” (Genesis xlvii, 27). Pharaoh’s dynasty, saved from disintegration by Joseph, whose abilities also built it into a position of wealth and power, at the outset displayed marked favor toward the family of the viceroy. They lived after their own fashion; and many were made overseers of the king’s flocks.

<span dir="rtl">ויקם מלך חדש על מצרים, אשר לא ידע את יוסף</span> “Now there arose a new king over Egypt, who knew not Joseph” (i, 8). <span dir="rtl">דהוי דמי כמאן דלא ידע לו כלל</span> “He acted as though utterly ignorant of Joseph and his achievements” (Sotah 11). Gradually there insinuated itself the matter of <span dir="rtl">הבה נתחכמה לו</span> “Come on, let us deal wisely with them.” How shall we deal with these Israelites, how dispose of them? <span dir="rtl">ויקוצו מפני בני ישראל</span> “And they were harassed because of the children of Israel” (12). The Talmud plays on the Hebrew words here, and comments, <span dir="rtl">היו בעיניהם כקוצים</span> “They became as thorns in their eyes” (Sotah 11).

Such has ever been the lot of Israel. So long as there is a negligible number of Jews in a land, they are tolerated. But so soon is the “earth filled with them,” than there is talk of “dealing wisely,” and Jewry becomes a difficult problem in the eyes of the citizenry. There arises the familiar plaint, <span dir="rtl">הנה עם בני ישראל רב ועצום ממנו</span> “Behold, the people of Israel are more numerous and powerful than we” (9). There is an intimation here that the strength of Israel has been usurped from the rest of the population. (The same word, mimenu, means “than we” and “from us.”) Abimelech king of Gerar directed the same complaint against Isaac: <span dir="rtl">לך מעמנו כי עצמת ממנו מאד</span> “Go from us; for thou art become much mightier than we (through us)” (Genesis xxvi, 16). And thus spake the children of Laban: <span dir="rtl">לקח יעקב את כל אשר לאבינו</span> “Jacob hath taken all that was our father’s” (Genesis xxxi, 1). To that very decision concerning Israel’s undue power and riches came Pharaoh and his counsellors. This people was occupying too great a place in trade and industry; they were taking too much to themselves, hence must be robbing everyone else in the process.

There began a series of persecutions against Israel. First came new laws to abridge the people’s rights as citizens and freemen. <span dir="rtl">וישימו עליו שרי מסים למען ענותו בסבליתם</span> “Therefore did they set over them officers of tribute to afflict them with their burdens” (Exodus i, 11). Forced labor and special taxes — all the previous industry of the people went for naught. Only the slightest return was granted them for their labor. New imposts consumed them. Taxes to the government, bribes to the taskmasters — never a moment of release or respite (Exodus Rabbah 2).

<span dir="rtl">וכאשר יענו אתו כן ירבה וכן יפרץ</span> “And as they afflicted them, so they multiplied and grew” (12). The Jew is ever a stiff-necked people. With liberty and kindliness it is possible he may be misled from his ordinary course; but under oppression his Judaic spirit grows mighty, and holds him to his people and his God. <span dir="rtl">כן רבו וכן פרצו מבעי</span>’ <span dir="rtl">לי</span>’ “The Bible should have employed the ordinary past tense and not the future in telling that Israel multiplied and grew.” <span dir="rtl">רוח הקדש מבשרתן</span> — <span dir="rtl">כן ירבה וכן יפרץ</span> “But the Holy Spirit indicated by this usage that Israel would continue to prosper in spite of all enemies.” The more the oppression, the more powerful the expression of the unconquerable spirit of the people (Sotah 11).

## Rays of Redemption

<span dir="rtl">וישמע אלהים את נאקתם ויזכר את בריתו</span> “And God heard their groaning, and God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Joseph” (ii, 24). The years decreed for Israel’s suffering, in the covenant made between the pieces of the sacrificial animals, had been attained. Reckoning all the time spent in wandering by the patriarchs themselves and the period of exile in Egypt, there was fulfillment of <span dir="rtl">גר יהיה זרעך בארץ לא להם ועבדום וענו אתם ארבע מאות שנה</span> “Thy seed shall be a stranger in a land that is not theirs, and shall serve them; and they shall afflict them four hundred years” (Genesis xv, 13). The era of hardship having been completed, there now began to gleam the rays of redemption.

The Midrash declares that the Torah recapitulates the names of the tribes at the beginning of this portion, because in each name there is possible a play of words indicating the redemptive process. Thus:

<span dir="rtl">ראובן</span>—<span dir="rtl">ראה ראיתי את עני עמי</span> “Reuben — God hath seen (Hebrew, raah) the affliction of my people.”

<span dir="rtl">שמעון</span>—<span dir="rtl">וישמע אלהים את נאקתם</span> “Simeon — And God heard (Hebrew, shama, sime. . . .) their groan.”

<span dir="rtl">לוי</span>—<span dir="rtl">עמו אנכי בצרה, שנתחבר הקב</span>”<span dir="rtl">ה לצרתם</span> “Levi — ‘I am with him in his trouble’ — for God adhered (levi has that meaning) to them in their difficulties.”

<span dir="rtl">יהודה</span>—<span dir="rtl">שהודו הקב</span>”<span dir="rtl">ה</span> “Judah — in that they thanked (Hebrew, hodu, as in root of Judah) the Lord” for His liberation.

<span dir="rtl">יששכר</span>—<span dir="rtl">שנתן להם הקב</span>”<span dir="rtl">ה שכר שעבודם</span> “Issachar — in that the Holy One blessed be He gave them reward (Hebrew, sechar) for their bondage.”

<span dir="rtl">זבולון</span>—<span dir="rtl">שהשכין הקב</span>”<span dir="rtl">ה שכינתו בקרבם</span> “Zebulon—in that God made his Divine Presence to dwell (Hebrew, zebul, also means dwell) in their midst.”

<span dir="rtl">בנימין</span>—<span dir="rtl">ימינך ה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">נאדרי בכח</span> “Benjamin — the right hand (Hebrew, jemin) of the Lord is glorious with power.”

<span dir="rtl">דן</span>—<span dir="rtl">וגם את הגוי אשר יעבודו דן אנכי</span> “Dan — ‘And also the nation that shall enslave you shall I judge (Hebrew, dan).’ ”

<span dir="rtl">נפתלי</span>—<span dir="rtl">תורה ומצוות שנתן להם הקב</span>”<span dir="rtl">ה שמתוקים מנפת צופים</span> “Napthali — the Holy One blessed be He, gave them Torah and mitzvot, which are sweeter than fine honey” (Hebrew, nofet, honey — first letters of Naphtali).

<span dir="rtl">גד</span>—<span dir="rtl">על שם המן שהיה כזרע גד</span> “Gad — because of the manna, which had the appearance of oleander (Hebrew, gad) seed.”

<span dir="rtl">אשר</span>—<span dir="rtl">ואשרו אתכם כל הגוים</span> “Asher — and all the nations will praise (Hebrew, asher) you.”

<span dir="rtl">יוסף</span>—<span dir="rtl">שעתיד הקב</span>”<span dir="rtl">ה להוסיף ולגאול את ישראל</span> “Joseph — for the Holy One blessed be He is destined to continue (Hebrew, hosif, as in Joseph) the redemption of Israel.”

Thus the names of the tribes symbolized redemption for all the Jews in Egypt; hence they would not alter their designations. The Midrash adds:

<span dir="rtl">משום שלא שנו את שמם, זכו להגאל</span> “Inasmuch as they would not change their names, they were deemed worthy of being delivered from exile” (Exodus Rabbah 2).

## The Liberator is Born

Pharaoh’s soothsayers deduced from the stars that soon would be born a Jewish child, who would ultimately free his people from bondage and bring catastrophe upon Pharaoh and Egypt (Exodus Rabbah 2). In terror, the king promulgated the order to the Jewish midwives <span dir="rtl">בילדכן את העבריות</span> — <span dir="rtl">אם בן הוא והמתן אתו</span> “When ye do the office of a midwife to the Hebrew women . . . if it be a son, then ye shall put him to death” (i, 16). Finding his decree not obeyed, he sent out another command, <span dir="rtl">הבן הילוד, תיארה תשליכהו</span> “Every son that is born, ye shall cast into the river” (22).

But this horrible decree proved also of no avail — the redeemer of Israel appeared in despite. <span dir="rtl">בשעה שנולד משה, נתמלא כל הבית כולו אורה</span> “At the time Moses was born, the entire house became filled with light” (Exodus Rabbah 2). His mother Jochebed recognized at once that this was a child utterly unique. She concealed him for three months. When she could do so no longer, she placed him in a pitched container, and floated the receptacle on the river. By chance Pharaoh’s daughter had gone down to bathe that day; she remarked the babe, and had a servitor remove it from the water. <span dir="rtl">והנה נער בכה ותחמול עליו</span> “And behold, it was a weeping boy; and she pitied him,” saying, <span dir="rtl">מילדי העברים זה</span> “This is one of the Hebrews’ children” (ii, 6).

Says B. Joasohn: “When Pharaoh’s daughter beheld a yeled, a babe, and heard him weep like a naar, a grown child, she understood that this must be a Hebrew.” She determined to keep the little one alive. His sister, Miriam, who had witnessed the incident, asked the princess if she desired a Hebrew nurse for the infant, and obtained consent to convey him to his own mother. <span dir="rtl">ויגדל הילד ותבאהו לבת-פרעה ויהי-לה לבן</span> “And the child grew, and she brought him unto Pharaoh’s daughter, and he became as her son” (10).

Under similar circumstances were born Abraham, founder of the faith, and Moses, liberator and lawgiver. Nimrod the cruel also decreed, before the birth of Abraham, that every new-born son should be put to death; Pharaoh emulated Nimrod’s example before the birth of Moses. Amtelai, mother of Abraham, was compelled to conceal her child in the forest, whereas Jochebed floated her babe on the water.

Such is the origin of those destined to deliver a people whose entire existence has been predicated upon miraculous events.
