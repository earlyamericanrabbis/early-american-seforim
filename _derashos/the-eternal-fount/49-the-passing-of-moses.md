<span dir="rtl">וילך משה</span> “And Moses went” among the people to bid them farewell. He had heard the ultimate divine judgment — <span dir="rtl">הן קרבו ימיך למות</span> “Behold, thy days approach that thou must die” (14). <span dir="rtl">קרא את יהושע ואצונו</span> “Call Joshua. . . . that I may give him a charge.” Even though Moses was termed <span dir="rtl">בכל ביתי נאמן הוא</span> “He is faithful in all Mine house” (Numbers xii, 7), and had loyally transmitted his divine lore and precepts to the Israelites, and through them to all humanity; now he must abdicate, and pass his task on to another. The divine mission was no longer to be his. The spirit of prophecy, which had brought him so close to God — “face to face” — was departing from him. Now Moses could not understand the speech of the Almighty; and he turned to Joshua for explanation of the words addressed to the new leader. In agony he cried out, <span dir="rtl">מאה מיתות ולא קנאה אחת</span> “Let me have a hundred deaths but not one feeling of envy, to embitter my life and bring constant thought of death!” (Midrash Rabbah 9).

For forty years had Moses served his people. He had sacrificed his very soul for them, fought for them against Pharaoh in Egypt, against the hostile forces of the wilderness, even against God Himself when He wished to castigate them. And now all must be given over to a successor.

<span dir="rtl">לא אוכל עוד לצאת ולבא, שניטלה ממני הרשות ונתנה לאחר</span> “I am no longer able to go out and to come in unto them, for the power has been taken from me and given to another” (Rashi). Now the lawgiver passes his days among the various classes of his people; some he covers with praise, to others he gives admonitions to cleave to their people and fulfill the promises of the Torah. Like a devoted father he rebukes them, speaks to all, worries over them, and yet promises them a brilliant destiny.

It is related of Rabbi Israel Salanter that before his death he summoned all his householders to his bedside. To some he bade a warm farewell. Among these was a well-known “Epicurean,” with whom Salanter spent more time than with any of the others, and from whom he took his departure with unusual fervor. Bystanders remarked on his greater favor toward this backslider than to the pious gentry with him. “Let me explain,” returned the rabbi. “I am hopeful of seeing you men once more, after long years, in Paradise. But this fellow I doubt whether I shall ever meet again — hence my fervent goodby to him” (B. Joasohn).

## The Soul That Soared Heavenward Cannot Cross the Jordan

The Midrash, depicting the last days of Moses, speaks in rue: <span dir="rtl">אתמול היה עולה לרקיע כנשר</span> — <span dir="rtl">עכשיו היה מבקש לעבור את הירדן ואינו יכול</span> “Yesterday he soared in the heavens like an eagle — now he would but cross over the Jordan, and cannot” (Deuteronomy Rabbah 9). The hero of heroes, who rose like a pillar of light on the threshold of Jewish history, who had forced heaven and earth to do his bidding, was now too enfeebled to pass the tiny Jordan and gaze upon the Promised Land, for which he had labored so greatly as his ultimate goal. The master of prophecy, before whom angels had trembled and even Deity had altered His judgments, could not prevail upon God to permit his ingress into Palestine with his own people, as the culmination of all his life’s purpose. divides in three the 120 years of Moses’ existence — forty years in the palace of Pharaoh as adopted son of the princess, who had found him in the ark among the bullrushes — thus was his early life spent. There he was taught all the requirements of a popular leader. But even at this time he was interesting himself in the problems of his brethren. <span dir="rtl">וירא בסבלותם</span> “He saw their burdens”; <span dir="rtl">וירא כי אין איש</span> “and he saw there was no man” (Exodus ii, 11-12). No proper leader was there to organize them and prepare them for liberation. He himself determined to take up their cause, but an Israelite informer compelled his flight from the land of Israel’s bondage.

Moses’ next forty years were spent in Midian. There he married the daughter of Jethro, priest of Midian, and served as shepherd for his father-in-law. There was born his older son Gershom, so-called because <span dir="rtl">גר הייתי בארץ נכריה</span> “I have been a stranger in a strange land” (Exodus ii, 22). Through all his years of shepherding Moses was constantly occupied with thought of human destiny. <span dir="rtl">ויבוא אל הר האלהים</span> “And he came to the mountain of God” (iii, 1) — to the highest pinnacle of human thought, of all spiritual development. At Mount Horeb he beheld the divine vision, which impelled him to return to Egypt and free his brethren of their exiled bondage.

More fruitful than any experience by other men were the last forty years of his life. In that period Moses was able to convert a slave people into a free nation — a chosen people and a kingdom of priests. Human beings sunk in impurity, coarse and unknowing, were under his guidance transformed into beings little less than the angels, carriers of the Law which, whether they willed it or no, worked its power on all men — and which in the latter days will reign supreme in the thoughts of humanity’s soaring intelligence.

Moses was most eager to conduct the people into the Promised Land, for there, in their own territory, he was assured that they would be able to establish the spiritual center he had for so long striven to create. But he was not fated to attain this consummation; he was to die in the wilderness, and leave the completion of his task to Joshua, his disciple.

<span dir="rtl">כל הבריות כשהן מתין מטפלין בהם בני אדם, אבל אתה, אני ופמליא שלי מטפלין בך</span> “When all other human creatures die, the sons of men alone occupy themselves with the departed; but when you die, Myself and My angelic household will care for you” (Deuteronomy Rabbah 9). <span dir="rtl">ולא ידע איש את קבורתו עד היום הזה</span> “But no man knoweth of his sepulchre unto this day” (Deuteronomy xxxiv, 6). For in truth Moses has not died. He lives and will continue to live as long as there is a people of Israel. So writes Ahad Haam: “Moses, the illustrious, remains Israel’s leader, marching in their van not alone for the forty years of the wilderness, but for the thousands of years of wandering in various wildernesses, from Egypt to modern days.”

## Final Words of Comfort

<span dir="rtl">ועתה כתבו לכם את-השירה הזאת</span> “Now therefore write ye this song for you” (xxxi, 19) — this Law which is God’s spiritual poem. Take it with you on all your wanderings; and teach it to your children and children’s children, whom it will also protect from dissolution. And if perchance <span dir="rtl">וקם העם הזה וזנה אחרי אלהי נכר הארץ</span> “this people will rise up and go astray after the gods of the aliens of the land” (16) — then <span dir="rtl">ומצאהו רעות רבות וצרות</span> “many evils and distresses shall befall them” <span dir="rtl">ועזבתים והסתרתי פני מהם</span> “and I will forsake them, and I will hide My face from them” (17). <span dir="rtl">ואמר ביום ההוא הלא על כי-אין אלהי בקרבי מצאוני הרעות האלה</span> “So that they will say in that day, Are not these evils come upon us, because our God is not amongst us?” All Israel’s misfortunes arise through loss of faith. <span dir="rtl">וענתה השירה הזאת לפניו</span> “This song shall testify against them as a witness” (21). However, these words can mean that the song will provide an answer to despair and give the people courage and hope to combat all hardships and achieve an ultimate victory.

These are the comments of the Maggid of Dubnow: “When night is approaching, and some itinerant peddler of fruit looks upon his greatly dwindled stock as he prepares to hasten home —he gathers together the remainders, the few pears or apples or plums from each bin, and puts them all together for quicker disposal. Hence I interpret the passage, ‘Many evils and distresses shall befall them,’ to mean that when Israel finds itself beset by a variety of hardships, this is a symbol that the culmination is near; the Messiah is coming to take his people home.”
