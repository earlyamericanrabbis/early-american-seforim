“And the Lord called unto Moses” — God summoned Moses to the Tabernacle, to hold private converse with him. The “aleph” in “*vayikra*” (and he called) is much smaller than the other letters of the word, signifying that the call was for Moses alone. <span dir="rtl">הקול הולך ומגיע לאזניו וכל ישראל לא שומעין</span> “The voice goes forth and reaches unto his ears, but none other of Israel hears it” (Rashi). The reason for such secrecy is declared to be God’s desire to expound to Moses the purpose of the sacrifices. Had the Israelites also hearkened to God’s words, they might have assumed that God desired the sacrifices for Himself, for His own pleasure, whereas the contrary was His true intent.

The prophets, who were later to expand the ideals of the Torah, speak with acerbity against the earthy, materialist aspect of the sacrifices. They abhor those who imagine that God really desires their offerings, which they consider atonement for all their sins. The prophet Samuel uttered that notable exclamation: <span dir="rtl">החפץ לה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">בעלות וזבחים כשמע בקול ה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">הנה שמע מזבח טוב להקשיב מחלב אילים</span> “Hath the Lord as much delight in burnt-offerings and in sacrifices, as in obeying the voice of the Lord? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to attend more than the fat of the rams” (Samuel I, xv, 22).

Isaiah castigates Israel’s rulers for oppressing the people. He calls them <span dir="rtl">קציני סדם</span> “princes of Sodom,” — who are characterized as evil and sinful men, displaying piety only in matters relating to God. They were of the belief that so long as they offered sufficient sacrifices to the Lord they might do as they pleased, and be assured of forgiveness. Says Isaiah:

<span dir="rtl">למה לי רוב זבחיכם יאמר ה</span>’... <span dir="rtl">קטרת תועבת היא לי... רחצו הזכו הסירו רע מעלליכם מנגד עיני</span>

“For what serveth me the multitude of your sacrifices, saith the Lord. ... Incense of abomination is it to me. ... Wash yourselves, make yourselves clean; put away the evil of your deeds from before my eyes....” (Isaiah i, 11, 13, 16).

At a later time Jeremiah speaks in the same strain:

<span dir="rtl">עלותיכם לא לרצון וזבחיכם לא ערבו לי</span>

“Your burnt-offerings are not acceptable, and your sacrifices are not agreeable unto me” (Jeremiah vi, 20). The Psalms speak the word of God: <span dir="rtl">האוכל בשר אבירים ודם עתודים אשתה</span> “Do I eat the flesh of fatted bulls, or drink the blood of he-goats?” (Ps. l, 13).

It becomes obvious, from these declarations, that the primary idea behind the sacrifices was not the offering itself, but the intent of the person bringing it. The very word — *karban* — means “coming near.” Its purpose is to lead man closer in his thoughts to God. Hence have the sages proclaimed:

<span dir="rtl">קרבן דליכא תשובה הוא זבח רשעים תועבה</span>

“A sacrifice brought without thought of repentance is accounted by the Lord as an offering of the wicked, an abomination” (Zebachim 7b).

## Free Will Must Accompany Offerings

The biblical phrase has it <span dir="rtl">אדם כי יקריב מכם קרבן לה</span>’ “If any of you wish to bring an offering unto the Lord” (Leviticus i, 2). To this Torat Cohanim comments: <span dir="rtl">יכל גזרה, ת</span>”<span dir="rtl">ל כי יקריב, הא אינו אלא רשות</span> “Since one might understand this to be an absolute command, the word *yakriv* (wish to bring) is used, to indicate that it is a matter of choice, of free will.” Men are not ordered to offer sacrifices if they do not comprehend their true meaning, if they are without penitence, and if their purpose is not to come nearer to the Godhead thereby. Their offerings, in such a case, are not acceptable to the Lord. There must be a voluntary intent and understanding that through the medium of sacrifice the person seeks closer proximity to God and improvement in his own conduct.

There are two main divisions of the sacrifices. One is the <span dir="rtl">קרבן תודה</span> or <span dir="rtl">שלמים</span>, thank- or peace-offerings, brought to the Lord out of gratitude for His bounties. These imply a recognition that a man’s goods, gained through his efforts, are not truly his own, but freely given by the Lord. Then there is the <span dir="rtl">קרבן אשם</span> or <span dir="rtl">חטאת</span> — the guilt offering brought in expiation of sin incurred against God or man.

<span dir="rtl">נפש כי תחטא ומעלה מעל בה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">וכחש בעמיתו... והשיב את הגזלה אשר גזל... ואת אשמו יביא לה</span>’

“If any person sin, and commit a trespass against the Lord; if he, namely, lie unto his neighbor. . . he shall restore what he hath taken violently away. . . and his trespass-offering shall he bring unto the Lord” (Leviticus v, 21, 23, 25). The sages are agreed that a sin <span dir="rtl">בין אדם לחברו</span> against one’s neighbor — is far more serious than a transgression <span dir="rtl">בין אדם למקום</span> against God. <span dir="rtl">קשה גזל הדיוט יותר מגזל גבוה</span> “The punishment for robbing a man is much severer than that for robbing Him on high (of a divine precept).”

Hence does the Torah declare that if any person feels double guilt, both against man and against God, “he shall restore what he hath taken violently away” to the injured man — and thereafter “bring his trespass-offering” to propitiate the Lord. God has no delight in an offering which is not the possession of the person who brings it — which has come to him through robbery (Sukkah 30). To despoil a neighbor in order to bring a sacrifice to God is an unforgivable sin.

<span dir="rtl">כל שאור וכל דבש לא תקטירו ממנו</span> “Of whatever is leaven, or of any honey, ye shall not sacrifice an offering made by fire unto the Lord” (Leviticus ii, 11). Rabbi Joseph Saul Nathanson thus comments on this injunction: “Leaven and honey are two extremes, both as to their intrinsic qualities and their taste. One is sour and the other sweet. This fact indicates that God Himself dislikes extremes. He does not favor one who is overly soured, who is completely devoted to thoughts of the world to come, with no interest in his own world. Likewise does He abhor the sycophant, him who approaches the Lord with words of hypocritical flattery. All extremes are hurtful; one must hold aloof from excess in either direction. It is best for the Jew to tread the way of gold, that which is called ‘the golden mean.’ ”

## Prayer in Lieu of Sacrifice

According to Maimonides (Guide, ch. 32) and other famed Jewish philosophers, the command of sacrifice was given to the Jew in order to preserve him from the sin of idol-worship. In early days the nations had a widespread custom of offering sacrifices to their gods. Many went so far in their superstitious practices as to bring human offerings. Thus the first-born male child was often slaughtered as a godly sacrifice.

To obviate such idolatrous and superstitious excesses among the Jews, it was biblically ordained to build a proper tabernacle and altar, to which only such offerings might be borne as would elevate humanity to closer kinship with God. This thought is directly expressed, adds Maimonides, in the verses: <span dir="rtl">למען אשר יביאו בני ישראל את זבחיהם לה</span>’... <span dir="rtl">ולא יזבחו עוד לשעירים</span> “In order that the children of Israel may bring their sacrifices. . . .unto the Lord. ... So that they shall offer no more their sacrifices unto evil spirits” (Leviticus xvii, 5, 7).

Many scholars would have it that in the story of the binding of Isaac on the altar, God sought to teach the world through Abraham that He has no desire in human sacrifice (Akedat Isaac). Instead of seeking the sacrifice of the firstborn, God established the method of their redemption by the Israelites through the payment of five shekalim to the priest. This thought is derived from the biblical query: <span dir="rtl">האתן בכורי פשעי, פרי בטני חטאת נפשי</span>? “Shall I give my firstborn for my transgression, the fruit of my body for the sin of my soul?” (Micah vi, 7).

In the latter years of the second Temple, when the priesthood was corrupted, and abused the true intent of the sacrifices, the men of the Great Assembly began gradually to replace sacrifice with spoken prayer. There are many talmudic passages clarifying this measure, several of the more significant of which are herewith cited. <span dir="rtl">בזמן שקוראים לפני אני מוחל להם על כל עונותיהם</span> “Whensoever they call upon me in prayer, I grant them forgiveness of all their sins” (Taanit 27).

<span dir="rtl">אמר רבא כל העוסק בתורה אינו צריך לא עולה ולא חטאת ולא מנחה ולא אשם</span> “Said Raba, All who busy themselves with study of the Law are exempted from the requirement of offering the various sacrifices” (Menahot 110).

<span dir="rtl">מי שדעתו שפלה מעלה עליו הכתוב כאילו הקריב כל הקרבנות כולן</span> “He who humbles himself in prayer before the Lord is considered as though he had offered up all the sacrifices” (Sanhedrin 43).

<span dir="rtl">אמר ר</span>’ <span dir="rtl">אלעזר גדולה תפלה יותר מן הקרבנות</span>

“Said Rabbi Eliezer, Prayer stands much higher than sacrifices” (Berakot 32).
