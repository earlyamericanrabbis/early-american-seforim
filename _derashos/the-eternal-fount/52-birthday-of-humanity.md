<span dir="rtl">אדם הראשון נברא בראש השנה</span> “Adam, first man, was created on Rosh Hashanah, the New Year” (Leviticus Rabbah 29). Jews do not reckon their years by the birth of a god. That which is born will also die. The Jewish people is eternal, for it believes in an eternal God. The Creator of life will not succumb to death.

Rosh Hashanah is a birthday for all humanity, and must so be seriously considered. It is not alone the beginning of a new year, but also the end of a year past. On such a day the balance must be drawn of all human accomplishments and failures, successes and defeats. The past year must serve as guide and preceptor for the year to come.

<span dir="rtl">מתכנסים מלאכי השרת לפני הקב</span>”<span dir="rtl">ה ושואלים אימתי ראש השנה</span> “The ministering angels gather about the Holy One blessed be He, and inquire, When does Rosh Hashanah fall?” <span dir="rtl">והקב</span>”<span dir="rtl">ה משיב, ולי אתם שואלים</span>? <span dir="rtl">אני ואתם נלך לב</span>”<span dir="rtl">ד של מטה</span> “And God replies, Why ask Me? Let us go down to the court of judgment below” (Yalkut Shimeoni, Tehillim 1).

To the Creator of eternity there are no beginnings and ends of years. To Him <span dir="rtl">אלף שנים... כיום אתמול כי יעבר</span> “A thousand years are as a yesterday when it is past” (Psalms xc, 4). The concept of past and future cannot be employed as we understand it, when we discuss the Creator of time.

When Moses requests the Lord, <span dir="rtl">הראני-נא את-כבדך</span> “I beseech Thee, shew me Thy glory” (Thy great future), he is thus answered, <span dir="rtl">וראית את-אחרי ופני לא יראו</span> “Thou shalt see what is behind Me: but My face shall not be seen” (Exodus xxxiii, 18, 23). My face, that which is directly before Me, may not be seen or understood. But man can study the past and learn its lessons.

Rosh Hashanah, beginning and end of years, is to be reckoned by men below, who live by divisions of time. They alone may establish the temporal guideposts called New Years.

The commonly employed name Rosh Hashanah, for the first of the month Tishri, appears among the Jews only in the later generations of the Talmud and after. The Torah uses the phrase <span dir="rtl">יום הזכרון</span> “Day of Memorial,” of self-searching, accounting of past and future events — and this is the fundamental Jewish idea of the season.

## Praying for All Humanity

When Jews gather in their synagogues to ask God for a good year, they at the same time pray for all mankind. When we plead with the Lord to bring <span dir="rtl">שמחה לארצך וששון לעירך</span> “happiness to Thy land and joy to Thy city,” we also pray for all peoples — <span dir="rtl">ויעשו כלם אגודה אחת</span> “May they all become one confraternity.” And though our aim is <span dir="rtl">משכן ותמלוך בהר ציון משכן כבודך</span> “Mayest Thou reign on Mount Zion, the tabernacle of Thy glory,” we crave the redemption of all humanity: <span dir="rtl">עולתה תקפוץ פיה וכל הרשעה כולה כעשן תכלה</span> “Iniquity shall be swallowed up, and evil shall pass away like smoke.”

We Jews have always understood that the fortune of one nation is mightily bound up with the status of all peoples. On Rosh Hashanah we read the Pentateuchal portion of the sacrifice of Isaac — which the Midrash gives a special significance:

“God showed Abraham the ram, provided as a substitute offering for Isaac, entangled among the trees of the forest, and then spoke thus:

<span dir="rtl">כך עתידין בניך להיות נאחזין באומות ונסבכים בצרות ונמשכים ממלכות למלכות</span> ‘So are thy sons destined to be entangled among the nations, involved in hardships, and dragged from one government to another’ ” (Leviticus Rabbah 19).

At a Zionist Congress, Dr. Chaim Weitzmann presented a true example of how the fate of Israel is governed by the conduct of other peoples. In his address concerning the status of Palestine upbuilding, wherein he recounted the maladministration of the British governors there, he remarked: “The Italian-Abyssinian war had great repercussions on the status of the Jews. Had Italy lost, the honor and prestige of Europe would have declined in the Orient, and chauvinistic forces there would have drawn new sources of energy and incitation from the fact. Now that Italy has won, the results have shaken the prestige of England, which took a specific stand in the action. This is reminiscent of the ‘fear for the pot,’ which is equally susceptible to injury if it falls on a stone, or if a stone falls upon it — <span dir="rtl">ווי לה לקדרה</span>. Such was our own situation. The air was electrified, and therefore certain soon to break forth in thunders and lightnings.”

All through the Jewish exile a similar fate has confronted our people. “Involved in hardships, and dragged from one government to another” — every international crisis has made its impress upon Israel. Hence do we pray on Rosh Hashanah, <span dir="rtl">תן פחדך על כל מעשיך</span> “Set the fear of Thee over all that Thou hast made” — not alone over Israel but over humanity everywhere.

## The Shofar Teaches Unity

Chief Rabbi Avigdor Amiel of Tel Aviv asks, in his *Derashot el Ami*, “Why must the sounder of the shofar recite the blessing, <span dir="rtl">לשמוע קול שופר</span> ‘to hear the sound of the shofar’? Would it not be more proper to say, <span dir="rtl">לתקוע קול שופר</span> ‘to blast the sound of the shofar’? The answer is that we have plenty of men eager to sound their own horns. Most people would like to become popular spokesmen. We need more who will hearken and follow, and who will unite themselves in one aim and ideal.”

<span dir="rtl">שופר אחד אמר רחמנא ולא שנים ושלשה שופרות</span> “God prescribed that we hear the blast of a single shofar, and not of several together.” The Talmud implies that the shofar itself has no specific sound, but emits the tones blown through it. The right sort of leader sends forth the proper blasts, calling for unity — the single notes called *tekiah*. But when he has personal and private purposes to express, the tones come forth brokenly, in the technical form called *shebarim*. And the resulting disunity brings on the *teruah*, the much shattered blast which represents pain and woe.

Rabbi Abuha asked, Why do we use a ram’s horn? Because the Lord prescribed this instrument, in remembrance of the near sacrifice of Isaac — <span dir="rtl">כאלו עקדתם את עצמכם לפני</span> “As though you yourselves were offering your persons as a sacrifice unto Me” (Rosh Hashanah 16). The individual must not seek control of others, but control of self; he must look upon himself as part of the nation, with which he must work in unison.

The Zaddik of Apt, also known as <span dir="rtl">אוהב ישראל</span> “Lover of Israel” — once addressed his congregation in this fashion, before the sounding of the shofar:

“Once I developed the desire to become the leader of all Israel. I worked energetically to attain this goal, but without success. Thereupon I determined to become leader in my own city — again unsuccessfully. Then I decided to become head in my household, only to be astounded by failing here also. But to carry out my ambition for power, I at length made an effort to control my own personality. But this aim too was not granted fulfillment. Now you will understand why no person should try to obtain power over others, insomuch as he cannot first develop control over his own faculties.”
