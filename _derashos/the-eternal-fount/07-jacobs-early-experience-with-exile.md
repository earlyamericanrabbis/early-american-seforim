<span dir="rtl">ויצא יעקב</span> “And Jacob went out from Beer-sheba.” Jacob was compelled to forsake his father’s house and experience exile in an alien land. When Abraham and Isaac sought to travel to other climes, the Bible reads <span dir="rtl">ויסע</span> or <span dir="rtl">וילך</span> “he journeyed” or “he went,” without compulsion by anyone. The word employed for Jacob’s departure implies compulsory going, actual fleeing in peril of his life. His devoted mother Rebeccah had learned that Esau bore his brother a bitter enmity, because of the blessings vouchsafed the younger son by Isaac. In truth, Esau had been making plans to murder Jacob. Rebeccah bade Jacob flee to her brother Laban, and live with him until Esau’s anger would be partially appeased.

She did not divulge the full extent of Esau’s machinations, but encouraged his speedy departure by telling Isaac that she was not pleased with <span dir="rtl">בנות הארץ</span> “the daughters of the land” in which they dwelt, and could not endure the thought of Jacob’s espousing any one of them. Isaac called for Jacob, blessed him, and ordered <span dir="rtl">לא תקח אשה מבנות כנען</span> “Thou shalt not take a wife of the daughters of Canaan” (xxviii, 6). The youth was told that in Padan-aram, at the home of Bethuel, his grandfather, he might espouse one of his uncle Laban’s daughters.

The journey from Beer-sheba to Haran was not a lengthy one. The distance could be covered in several days. Eliezer, Abraham’s steward, who had originally traveled to arrange the marriage of Rebecca and Isaac, made the same journey in but a few days. But this ordinarily quick and expeditious trip was not so readily covered by the hapless Jacob. He was always beset by hardships in his wanderings. Difficulties of all kinds plagued him and impeded his progress. <span dir="rtl">ויפגע במקום וילן שם כי בא השמש</span> “And he lighted upon the place, and tarried there all night, because the sun was set” (xxviii, 11). Dark indeed were the nights of his tarrying. While he dwelt in his own home in his native land, the sun shone for him as for all men. But no sooner had he forsaken that abode, than the sun darkened for him, and he was unable to reach his goal. The presumedly brief journey took all of fourteen years (Genesis Rabbah 69).

Before his going, Jacob received from his mother many precious gifts for his bride, and for all the family of Laban. But he was waylaid by Eliphaz, Esau’s first born, and robbed of everything, even his garments, so that he remained penniless and naked. He was forced to swim the Jordan, and wander for long in the great wilderness of Aram (Yalkut Shimeoni). Wherever he turned, he was confronted by huge stones; when he lay down between them legends relate that they quarreled among themselves and destroyed his rest (Rashi).

The “stones of the place,” the hardhearted rulers of the land, were sometimes bent on joining Jacob to themselves (Hullin 91), but entirely for their own advantage. They were well aware that, as soon as Jacob had rested and come to himself, he would reward them excessively for the slightest show of hospitality in his period of hardship. For out of these very stones he would erect great monuments and <span dir="rtl">ויצק שמן על ראשה</span> he would pour oil lavishly upon the heads of these structures, and figuratively upon the heads of his benefactors.

<span dir="rtl">מה שאירע ליעקב, אירע לבניו</span> “All that happened to Jacob, happened to his descendants,” speaks the midrashic phrase. Jewish history demonstrates that in medieval times a local ruler frequently purchased rights over another ruler’s Jews; transferred them to his own territory, where for a period he provided them with every freedom and privilege of settlement; but so soon as they began to prosper and develop the industry of their country, they would be torn by oppressive measures and the harshest taxation. These were the “Hof-Juden,” court Jews, with whom each monarch could do as he pleased (Graetz).

## The Angels Depart

After extended wandering in the arid desert, Jacob halted at a resting place — <span dir="rtl">וישכב במקום ההוא</span> “and lay down in that place.” He was weary and forlorn. During the time he dwelt with his parents, <span dir="rtl">היו מלאכי ארץ-ישראל משמרין אותו ומלוין אותו</span> “the angels of Palestine would guard and accompany him.” But,

<span dir="rtl">כיון שהגיע לחוץ לארץ נסתלקו</span> “as soon as he left the boundaries of the land, they forsook him” (Tanhuma Vayishlach 3). At home he was blessed with many friends and protectors, living quietly in his own tent and studying the Law. Away from home he was insecure and unshielded. He was open to every danger.

Nor could he observe the precepts of the Law in his accustomed manner. <span dir="rtl">ישראל שבחוץ לארץ עובדי עבודה זרה בטהרה הם</span> “Jews dwelling outside the Land become servers of strange gods even though without intention” (Abodah Zarah 8). The process of adapting oneself to every spot one reaches is both difficult and dangerous. It imperils the spiritual and cultural achievements of many past generations. Without purposeful intent one must often imitate the ways of one’s neighbors and neglect part of one’s own observances. The original form of life obtaining in the old land must be largely modified.

Chaim Weitzmann relates the following anecdote: “The town I came from was near the German border. Our Jews carried on trade with the Germans; and in order to be able to cross the border at will, they had to purchase passes from the police, each costing three roubles and good for three months.

“Jacob, the rabbi’s son, was not a businessman, hence had no necessity for regular visits to Germany. But once it was most necessary for him to go there at once. Not desiring to pay three roubles for a single crossing, he borrowed a pass from a friend. ‘Remember,’ warned the friend, ‘my name is Peretz, so if they ask you, don’t say you are Jacob, but Peretz.’ ”

“As Jacob neared the boundary line, the sentry asked him, ‘Who are you?’ The agitated young man replied, nervously, ‘Well, I’m certainly not Jacob!’ ”

“And this indicates,” concluded Dr. Weitzmann, “that always, when a Jew crosses the boundaries of his own land to dwell on alien soil, he is no longer the same Jacob — no longer the <span dir="rtl">איש תם</span> ‘complete man’ he was at home.”

## Divine Dream

Jacob remained weak and despondent. He saw a long, hard way before him, ere he might achieve his purposes. He was troubled lest he find it not possible to maintain the teachings of his forbears. There is a comment of Rabbi Berdichever on this passage from the portion — <span dir="rtl">ונתן לי לחם לאכל... והיה ה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">לי לאלקים</span> “and if He give me bread to eat, and a garment to put on. . . .then shall the Eternal be my God” (xxviii, 20-21). “When the Jew has food and clothing enough,” says the rabbi, “he is then able to serve God. But when he lacks these needs, then it may unfortunately happen that his anguish of spirit will lead to forgetfulness of his divine duties.”

The wanderer fell asleep on his stone pillow. God, merciful, could not look upon the sufferings of His loyal servant. <span dir="rtl">קיפל הקב</span>”<span dir="rtl">ה ארץ ישראל תחתיו</span> “The Holy One, blessed be He, rolled up all of Palestine under Jacob,” so that he might have a soft and comfortable bed (Hullin 91). The instant Jacob sensed the Land beneath him, he became animated; his melancholy vanished; his back straightened; and his thoughts turned to Heaven.

In his dream he beheld <span dir="rtl">סלם מצב ארצה</span> “a ladder set up on the earth” — <span dir="rtl">וראשו מגיע השמימה</span> “and the top of it reached to heaven.” Wheresoever Jacob might go, so long as he had with him the land of Israel, its Torah and its ideals, he remained conjoined with heaven. Also he beheld <span dir="rtl">מלאכי אלקים עולים ויורדים בו</span> “angels of God going up and down on it” (xxviii, 12). The angels he had been accustomed to see in Palestine were those going up, the more exalted and spiritual; those from without the land were going down, the low, earthly, material beings. But he also sensed: <span dir="rtl">והנה ה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">נצב עליו</span> “And, behold, the Eternal stood above him.” Even in the wilderness the Lord was watching over him and shielding him from misfortune.

He heard the voice of God: <span dir="rtl">הארץ אשר אתה שכב עליה לך אתננה ולזרעך</span> “The land whereon thou liest, to thee will I give it, and to thy seed” (13). He assured the fulfillment of the divine promises to Abraham and Isaac. Be not without hope — <span dir="rtl">והנה אנכי עמך ושמרתיך בכל אשר תלך</span> “And behold, I am with thee, and will keep thee whithersoever thou goest.” The writer’s father chose to interpret this passage in this manner: “If I, the Lord, am with thee, if My faith is in thy heart, then shall I keep thee in all thy ways.”

The faith of the Jew has always protected him from extinction.

If not for its religion, for its Torah, our people would long ago have disappeared from the earth. Jewry remained intact because of the Jewish Sabbath, the dietary laws, the laws of family purity. Says Rabbi Z. H. Kalisher on the passage <span dir="rtl">והיה זרעך כעפר הארץ</span> “And thy seed shall be as the dust of the earth”: “The exile has made the children of Israel even as the dust of the earth. Although dust has many uses, still we tread upon it. The Jews have ever contributed more than their share to the uses, to the development of the lands they have lived in — yet they have been persistently trodden down. However, just as the earth survives every pressure, so will the Jew remain alive for all time.”

Jacob also heard these words from the Eternal: “Your wanderings will not continue forever. The time will come <span dir="rtl">והשיבתיך אל-האדמה הזאת</span> when I ‘will restore thee again into this land.’ <span dir="rtl">לא אעזבך עד אשר אם עשיתי את אשר דברתי לך</span> ‘For I will not forsake thee, until I have done that which I have spoken to thee.’ Palestine will be yours; and there you will scintillate as of yore.”

<span dir="rtl">וייקץ יעקב משנתו</span> “And Jacob awoke out of his sleep.” His dream had given him new hope. Before him stretched a radiant vista. He is convinced <span dir="rtl">אכן יש ה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">במקום הזה</span> “Surely the Eternal is in this place.” If he persists in his divine duties and observance of the rules of his Torah and religious faith, every place can become <span dir="rtl">שער השמים</span> “the gate to heaven,” leading to the holy land and the house of the Lord.
