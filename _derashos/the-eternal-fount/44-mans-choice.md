Thus spake Moses: <span dir="rtl">ראה אנכי נתן לפניכם היום ברכה וקללה</span> “Behold, I set before you this day a blessing and a curse.” Blessing will result from hearing and obeying the divine commands; and a curse from not heeding the words of God. This choice is entirely free to Israel; the people may choose as it wills.

The chief end of our Torah — so declares Rabbi Isaac Jacob Reines — is to elevate humanity to that stage wherein his moral and ethical concepts shall have the same power over him as have his natural instincts. For complete free will is ascribed to man, and indeed free will is one of the foundations of the Jewish faith. <span dir="rtl">הכל בידי שמים חוץ מיראת שמים</span> “All things lie in the hand of God except only the fear of God” (Berakot 33b). To have true fear of the Lord and to go in His ways, this is left to the individual’s free will alone. The Torah forces no one to follow its behests; it merely instructs men and shows them the path to happiness.

Were not this free choice given to man — thus continues Rabbi Reines — then all his acts would have to be dictated or indicated by outside agencies. In such a case, he would soon lose every attribute of humanity; or be altered either into an angel or superman, or into a wild beast or base creature. The Midrash relates that when God was contemplating the creation of man, he pondered, <span dir="rtl">אם אני בוראו מן העליונים הוא חי ואינו מת, מן התחתונים הוא מת ואינו חי</span> “If I create him out of celestial stuff alone, he will live on forever and never die; if only out of earthly material, he will die and never live” (Genesis Rabbah 14). For this reason man is made of body and soul; the body is composed of earthly elements, and the soul of heavenly elements. This fact renders man both “a little less than the angels,” because of the divine part in every created human, and always mortal, because of his mundane portion.

Thought, reason, deliberation before acting — all this is part of the soul. Will, desire, the effort to satisfy yearnings and passions — this derives from the body. The Torah does not seek to force man’s will; it gives him full freedom of action; it guides him concerning proper employment of reason before acting in a manner harmful to himself or to others. As reason frequently prevails over desire, bringing man to perform deeds of worth, such as freeing himself from the mastery of the ego, the Talmud sagely remarks,

<span dir="rtl">חשב אדם לעשתו מצוה ונאנס ולא עשה מעלה עליו הכתוב כאלו עשאה</span> “If a man plan to perform a righteous deed, and circumstances prevent him from accomplishing it, the Law considers him as worthy as though he had actually fulfilled his intent.” When any person is highly inspired, and has determined to actuate a righteous commandment, he is in ordinary circumstances enabled to carry through the will to complete it. But when extraordinary circumstances hold back accomplishment, this is not to be considered his sin; <span dir="rtl">מחשבה טובה מצרף למעשה</span> “Good intent is accounted as the deed” (Kiddushin 40a).

Moses is particularly insistent that the Israelites do not succumb to alien influences: <span dir="rtl">וסרתם מן-הדרך אשר אנכי מצוה אתכם היום ללכת אחרי אלהים אחרים אשר לא-ידעתם</span> “but depart from the way which I command you this day, to go after other gods, which ye have not known” (xi, 28). If you possess free will, you will not readily be seduced by alien ideas, such as are not in accord with the Law and faith of your people and your God. <span dir="rtl">כל המודה בעבודת כוכבים ככופר בכל התורה</span> “He who turns to idol worship, to foreign principles, is as one who has denied the Law entire” (Sifri).

It is true that the process of acclimatization in exile is most difficult for the Jew. Circumstances differ in every land; and not everywhere is it easy to carry into life the commands of the Torah. It is especially difficult to restrain the youth from succumbing to the influence of surrounding groups with whom they have contact. Every effort must be made to counteract this tendency, which moves to destroy Israel’s uniqueness, and to hold up the process of self-liberation.

The founders of Reform in Germany maintained that their new regulations were designed to facilitate observance by the younger generation of the major commandments of the Law. To this assertion Rabbi Ezriel Hildesheimer of Berlin replied after the following fashion: “The Torah declares, <span dir="rtl">לא תגרעו ממנו</span> — <span dir="rtl">לשמר את מצות ה</span>’ ‘Ye shall not diminish them’ — that is, no one may reduce the number of observances, even, as the rest of the quotation has it, in order to observe the commandments of the Eternal.’ ”

## Preserving Israel’s Unity

<span dir="rtl">בנים אתם לה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">אלהיכם</span> “Ye are children (sons) of the Eternal your God” (xiv, 1); and so must ye conduct yourselves. There must be no sects or classes among you; all must be equal. Children of one father should live in amity and be mutually helpful. The Gemara remarks, most aptly (Kiddushin 36a):

<span dir="rtl">נקראים בנים ונקראים עבדים</span>—<span dir="rtl">בזמן שאתם נוהגים מנהג בנים אתם נקראים בנים</span> “Why are the Israelites sometimes designated ‘sons’ and at other times ‘servants’? Because, when ye conduct yourselves as is proper for children of one Father, then can ye truly be called His sons”; but when you divide yourselves into different classes, with varied privileges, you are no longer as sons. Your observance of the Torah’s commands then becomes a mere form of worship. Such empty religion is more suited to servants and slaves than to divine offspring.

<span dir="rtl">לא תתגודדו</span>—<span dir="rtl">לא תעשו אגודות אגודות</span> “Ye shall not form parties — ye shall not make sects of various trends” (Jebamot 14). This breaks up the people’s unique quality and weakens its creative efforts. Parties, separate groups, are a product of exile, induced by life in various lands under various cultures. That tendency must be erased in Palestine; there all the people must be united in a single aim. <span dir="rtl">תשימו קרחה</span> — <span dir="rtl">כשם שעשה קרח</span> “Nor make any baldness” (xiv, 1) — just as did Korach” (Yalkut Shimeoni). (This is a play on the name Korach, since in Hebrew “baldness” has the same root letters.) Conflict among parties militates against national unity and against rapid national advancement.

The phrase “Ye shall not form parties” applies also to rabbis and other leaders. <span dir="rtl">כגון ב</span>”<span dir="rtl">ד אחד בעיר אחת, פלגא מורים כדברי בית שמאי, ופלגא כדברי בית הלל</span> “It is like unto a court in one city; half of the judges are faithful to the teachings of the House of Shammai, and half to the teachings of the House of Hillel; and each would pass judgment against the other” (Jebamot 14a). When rabbis differ greatly, the entire people is affected; the respect due from Jews for their religious law is undermined, and the entire religious life is upset.

<span dir="rtl">כי-יהיה בך אביון מאחד אחיך... לא-תאמץ את-לבבך ולא תקפץ את-ידך</span> “If there be among you a needy man of one of thy brethren. . . . thou shalt not make thy heart obstinate, nor close thine hand” (xv, 7). One must give him <span dir="rtl">די מחסרו</span> “enough to cover his lack.” The Maggid of Dubnow expounds this thought capably: “The Midrash Rabbah (Mishpatim) declares that David pleaded to God, <span dir="rtl">תיישב עולמך בשוה, העניים והעשירים</span> ‘I pray Thee, divide Thy possessions equally among the rich and the poor.’ Now this should apparently have been done; but in that event the more capable would still grasp all within their purview, and the rich would become wealthier at the expense of the poor. Hence does the Torah say, ‘If there be a needy man among you.’ This implies that his potential possessions are in your hands; since you hold them to his detriment, at least give back part of what is rightfully his.”

<span dir="rtl">לא ירע לבבך בתתך לו... כי בגלל הדבר הזה יברכך ה</span>’

“Thine heart shall not be grieved when thou givest unto him: because that for this thing the Eternal thy God shall bless thee” (xv, 10). <span dir="rtl">תנא דבי רבי ישמעאל</span>—<span dir="rtl">גלגל הוא שחוזר בעולם</span> “The school of Rabbi Ishmael thus taught: There is a wheel that revolves in the world” (Shabbat 151b). Every coin is circular; it rolls to one today and to another tomorrow. To maintain the law of God, one must always remember the needy and assist him with a fulsome hand.

On the matter of charity we read in the Talmud (Gittin 7), <span dir="rtl">כל הגוזז נכסיו ועושה מהם צדקה, ניצול מדינה של גיהנם</span> “He who pares his possessions to give charity therewith, is delivered from all harm of hell.”

<span dir="rtl">משל לשתי רחילות שהיו עוברות בנהר, אחת גזוזה ואחת שאינה גזוזה, גזוזה עוברת ושאינה גזוזה אינה עוברת</span> “This is likened to two ewes who wish to cross a river, one of which is shorn, while the other is unshorn. She that is shorn will pass, whereas she that is not shorn will not be able to cross over.” (The Hebrew word here translated “pares” and “shorn” are the same.) The man who did not allow himself to be “shorn” in prosperous days can expect no assistance from others in times of crisis; even the heavens forget him, for <span dir="rtl">כל המרחם על הבריות מרחמים מן השמים</span> “He who showeth mercy unto others, may himself hope for the mercy of Heaven” (Shabbat 151b).
