<span dir="rtl">וידבר אלהים אל-משה</span> “And God spake unto Moses” —disclosing a hitherto unknown bit of information. <span dir="rtl">כל מקום שנאמר לשון דבור, חדוש מקרא יש שם</span> “Wherever the biblical passage mentions speaking, there is an indication of some novel affirmation” (Jerushalmi Makkot 2). In this case, God imparts His right name to Moses. “When I showed Myself unto Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, I used the names El and Shaddai; but <span dir="rtl">ושמי ה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">לא נודעתי להם</span> I was not known to them by the Tetragrammaton — Jahweh — the Eternal.”

For the four letters of this name signify Eternal. The name, however, can be properly understood only after a long and troubled period of exile — despite all the misfortunes of which the nation has still remained alive. Then is it possible to comprehend the vast divine power which alone gives a people the ability to overcome persecution and assault.

<span dir="rtl">וידעתם כי אני ה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">אלהיכם המוציא אתכם מתחת סבלות מצרים</span> “And ye shall know that I am the Eternal your God, Who bringeth you out from under the burdens of Egypt” (vi, 7).

Asks the Midrash: <span dir="rtl">למה גלה אותו למשה</span> “Why did God reveal His name to Moses?” And the answer: <span dir="rtl">על שהלך לגאל את ישראל</span> “Because he was setting out to deliver Israel” (Tanhuma). Moses himself had been seeking the task, when entrusted by God with the mission to demand that Pharaoh free the Israelites and let them depart from Egypt. Moses was more fearful of his brethren than he was of Pharaoh. From the context one understands that he feared lest Israel mistrust him and withhold the aid requisite to his undertaking. For, in order to liberate any people from exile, they themselves must show their willingness to be freed and provide cooperation. Every member of the group must have confidence both in the justice of their demands and the worth of their leader. The earliest account of Moses’ encounter with God will here prove most enlightening.

## The First Divine Vision

Moses was in the wilderness, near Mount Horeb, tending the flocks of Jethro, his father-in-law. And suddenly the angel appeared to him <span dir="rtl">בלבת-אש מתוך הסנה</span> “in a flame of fire out of the midst of a bush” (iii, 2). There was wonderment in his glance when he saw <span dir="rtl">הסנה בער באש והסנה איננו אכל</span> “the bush burned with fire, and the bush was not consumed.” A voice came forth from the flame: <span dir="rtl">ראה ראיתי את-עני עמי אשר במצרים</span> “I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt” (iii, 7). <span dir="rtl">גם אני שרוי בצער כשם שישראל שרויין בצער</span> “I too am enveloped in care when Israel is filled with woe.” <span dir="rtl">אני מדבר מתוך הקוצים</span> “Hence do I speak from the midst of the thorns” (Exodus Rabbah 2). For God shares the wretchedness of Israel tangled in the briars.

But God offered Moses words of comfort. <span dir="rtl">צעקתם שמעתי וארד להצילו מיד מצרים</span> “I have heard their cry . .. and I am come down to deliver them out of the hand of the Egyptians” (iii, 7, 8). <span dir="rtl">כשם שהסנה בוער באש ואיננו אכל, כך המצרים אינם יכולים לכלות את ישראל</span> “Just as the bush burned in the fire and was not consumed, so will the Egyptians find it impossible to consume Israel” (Exodus Rabbah 2). God thus tells Moses that the bush is a symbol of Israel, burnt in the flames of its enemies, yet never to be destroyed.

<span dir="rtl">ועתה לכה ואשלחך אל-פרעה והוצא את-עמי בני-ישראל ממצרים</span> “Come now, therefore, and I will send thee unto Pharaoh, that thou mayest bring forth my people the children of Israel out of Egypt” (iii, 10). In the hands of Pharaoh, the king, lay the destiny of Israel. It was necessary that all arrangements for the deliverance of the people be made with Pharaoh. But Moses made reply to God: <span dir="rtl">הנה אנכי בא אל-בני ישראל ואמרתי להם אלהי אבותיכם שלחני אליכם ואמרו-לי מה-שמו מה אמר אליהם</span>? “Behold, when I come unto the children of Israel, and shall say unto them, The God of your fathers hath sent me unto you: and they shall say to me, What is His name? what shall I say unto them?” (13). Moses knew that Pharaoh was not his greatest problem; he would somehow manage with the king — but what could be done with Israel? How obtain the people’s obedience and assistance?

For Moses saw the true character of his charges most clearly. So soon as a movement is instituted to liberate the people from bondage, and to rebuild their national home, they divide themselves into parties, each with distinctive plans and ideas. To this day, when one addresses a Jew concerning his duty to his own, he immediately asks, “What is the name?” In the name of what party or sect in Israel is he being approached?

Then God replied to Moses: <span dir="rtl">כה תאמר לבני ישראל אהיה שלחני אליכם</span> “Thus shalt thou say unto the children of Israel, I AM hath sent me unto you.” The very God Who gave you life, Who gave you the strength to continue living during your long and difficult exile, will now bring you deliverance. But Moses refused to be convinced — he knew the difficulty of his task.

<span dir="rtl">ויען משה ויאמר והן לא-יאמינו לי ולא ישמעו בקולי</span> “And Moses answered and said, But, behold, they will not believe me, nor hearken unto my voice” (iv, 1). Jews are not easily convinced. Ordinary argument, however truthful and reasonable, will not insure results.

When God noted how Moses vacillated concerning the mission assigned him, He advised him to display miracles before the people. He could cast his stick to the ground, and have it changed to a serpent. He could pour out water, which would be transformed into blood. The Israelites, discovering in Moses a great magician, would at once become his partisans and hear him out.

But Moses had no liking for the role of magician. If the Israelites were unable to judge their own situation, if they took no steps to free themselves and become a nation like all others, Moses saw no reason for undertaking the task himself. Why should he befuddle them with unintelligible mumbo-jumbo? He pleaded, <span dir="rtl">בי אדני שלח-נא ביד תשלח</span> “Pardon, my Lord, send, I pray Thee, by the hand of him whom Thou wilt send!” (iv, 13). Free me of this unwelcome mission!

Finally God saw the justice of Moses’ objection. Other means were required to succor Israel. There must be agitation to awaken in each soul the spirit of freedom. Since Moses was “slow of speech,” and unable to talk freely to them, God suggested, <span dir="rtl">הלא אהרן אחיך... ודבר-הוא לך אל-העם</span> “Is not Aaron thy brother?. .. And he shall speak for thee unto the people” (14, 16). Even a man heavy of speech would suffice to interview Pharaoh; but for the people themselves only a popular orator would serve —to arouse enthusiasm for their coming deliverance.

## Israel Does Not Hearken

<span dir="rtl">וידבר משה כן אל-בני ישראל, ולא שמעו אל-משה מקצר רוח ומעבדה קשה</span> “And Moses spake so unto the children of Israel: but they hearkened not unto Moses through anguish of spirit, and through hard service” (vi, 9). There is an excellent comment of Rabbi Isaac Nissenbaum on this passage:

“When Moses transmitted to the people God’s assurance, <span dir="rtl">אעלה אתכם מעני מצרים אל ארץ זבת חלב ודבש</span> ‘I will bring you up out of the affliction of Egypt . . . unto a land flowing with milk and honey,’ they were certain that this meant an early deliverance. Within a short period God would free them from Egypt and at once transport them to the beautiful land of their fathers. They were eager to believe the news brought them, and hearkened to every utterance of their leader.

“But no sooner had Moses mapped out the process of their deliverance: as God said, ‘I shall bring you out from under the burdens of Egypt’ (a nation cannot achieve freedom if it devotes its energies to others); ‘I shall deliver you from your bondage’ (the nation must have a free spirit, untrammeled by alien influences); ‘and I shall redeem you with an outstretched arm’ (one must fight and bring sacrifices for freedom); ‘and I shall take you unto me as a people, and I shall be unto you as a God’; if you will attain honor and doff the impurities of Egypt and become worthy of Me as your God, ‘then will I bring you into the land’: — when the Israelites thus learned how long the process must be, how filled with difficulty and hardship, they regretted the entire project. The program outlined required patience and hard labor. ‘But they hearkened not unto Moses through anguish of spirit, and through hard service.’ Deliverance through miracles was different; but they had no patience for long effort in behalf of redemption. They were well accustomed to ‘hard service’ in Egypt; but to expend energy for their own people and its liberation —this was too natural and normal a process for a nation that had placed its reliance upon miracles.”
