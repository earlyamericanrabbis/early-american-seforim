<span dir="rtl">ואתחנן אל ה</span>’ “And I besought the Eternal” (iii, 23). <span dir="rtl">אעברה נא ואראה את הארץ הטובה</span> “I pray Thee, let me pass over, and see the good land” (25). The Talmud asks, <span dir="rtl">מפני מה נתאווה משה לכנס לארץ ישראל</span>? “Why did Moses so strongly desire to enter the Land of Israel?”

<span dir="rtl">וכי לאכל מפריה ולשבע מטובה הוא צריך</span>? “Was it that he wanted to eat its fruit and sate himself with its bounty?” It is not credible that this spiritual titan, who devoted his life to create an immortal people in a new land of romance, should meditate only on its material aspects. If his thoughts had turned to economic and political advancement alone, what was there lacking in the already conquered territory this side of the Jordan? Surely the people recognized their present site to be most productive, and its fortified cities were certain protection against all foemen. However, other considerations were involved.

What was Moses’ purpose? <span dir="rtl">אמר, הרבה מצוות נצטוו ישראל ואין מתקיימין אלא בארץ ישראל</span> “He said, Many commands have been given to Israel, which can be fulfilled only in the Land of Israel.” It was his desire to enter and see them carried out (Sotah 14). He was primarily concerned with Israel as “a kingdom of priests and a holy people” — with their spiritual and cultural development. In the spiritual center he envisaged for Palestine <span dir="rtl">שמיא וארעא נשקי הדדי</span>. a place “where heaven and earth join together.” It was to be <span dir="rtl">בית היוצר לנשמת האומה</span>, “a home of creativeness for the nation’s soul,” which would assuredly render it a holy people, fully capable of transforming this footstool into a kingdom of God on earth. In no other country could such a change be consummated. Only in Palestine was it possible. And Moses was bent on completing the labor he had begun.

Also Moses wished to look upon <span dir="rtl">ההר הטוב הזה, זו ירושלים</span> “this goodly mount on which shall rise Jerusalem” — Jerusalem, which would stand above all other cities of earth, since it was fated to become the cradle of all faiths (Berakot 48b). He desired as well to see <span dir="rtl">לבנון, זו בית המקדש שמלבין עוונותיהם של ישראל</span> “Lebanon (the white), the sanctuary which whitens all the iniquities of Israel” and makes Israel <span dir="rtl">עם סגולה מכל העמים</span> “chosen among all the peoples” (Sifri).

Moses protested, “Eternal God, Thou hast begun to show Thy servant Thy greatness” (24). At Mount Horeb, when God’s mission had been broached to him, he had laid down this condition —<span dir="rtl">שלח נא ביד תשלח, ביד מי שיכנס לארץ</span> that he who brought Israel out of Egypt should be fated to accompany them into the Promised Land. And God had replied, <span dir="rtl">לך נחה את העם</span> “Go, lead my people.” Why would not God now keep the promise thus implied? (Maharam Schiff).

But God replied, <span dir="rtl">ששה עוונות עשית</span> “Six transgressions hast thou committed” to alter the mind of the Eternal. The first was his answer, <span dir="rtl">שלח-נא ביד-תשלח</span> “Send, I pray Thee, by the hand of him whom Thou wilt send” (Exodus, iv, 13). This sounded like reluctance to undertake the work of Israel’s deliverance; like a desire to be absolved from the task, at the hands of another.

Second, at the first disappointment in negotiating with Pharaoh, Moses returned to the Lord to complain, <span dir="rtl">הצל לא הצלת את עמך</span> “Neither hast Thou delivered Thy people at all” (v, 23). He appeared to wish the miracle to take place at once, even before the people and himself made any real effort to accomplish their deliverance.

Third, when the Israelites demanded meat, Moses cried out angrily, <span dir="rtl">הצאן ובקר ישחט להם ומצא להם</span> “Shall the flocks and herds be slaughtered for them, to suffice them?” (Numbers xi, 22). Herein he seemed to doubt the ability of God to provide food for the people.

Then, during the conflict with Korach, Moses assembled the congregation and proclaimed, if Korach were to die a natural death, <span dir="rtl">לא ה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">שלחני</span> “then the Eternal hath not sent me” (Numbers xvi, 29). By these words, he proved himself prepared to appeal to the people on nationalistic grounds alone — a sin in God’s eyes, since Jewish nationalism must never be dissociated from the Jewish faith.

The fifth transgression concerned the incident at Marah, where Moses angrily smote the rock, crying, <span dir="rtl">שמעו-נא המרים</span> “Hear now, ye rebels!” In striking the stone while intending the Israelites, he proved himself unworthy of leadership.

In rebuking the younger generation Moses committed his sixth fault. <span dir="rtl">והנה קמתם תחת אבותיכם תרבות אנשים חטאים</span> “And behold, ye are risen up in your fathers’ stead, a progeny of sinful men” (Numbers xxxii, 14). It is not proper thus to describe the fathers to the sons. One must be cautious in calling up the past, if one wishes to retain the people’s unity (Tanhuma).

Moses added to his plaint: “Joseph, who devoted his entire life to Egypt and its enrichment, was worthy to have his remains interred in the Land of Israel; while I, who have through all my days sacrificed so much for the Land, have not been adjudged worthy of this distinction.” To this God replied, <span dir="rtl">מי שהודה בארצו נקבר בארצו, ומי שלא הודה בארצו אינו נקבר בארצו</span> “He who has acknowledged his land, is buried therein; he who has not acknowledged his land, will not be buried therein.” Joseph never denied his origin, even when Potiphar’s wife cried, <span dir="rtl">הביא לנו איש עברי לצחק בנו</span> “He hath brought in a Hebrew unto us to mock us” (Genesis xxxix, 14). He suffered greatly for his admission; and when telling his story to the butler, he declared, <span dir="rtl">גנב גנבתי מארץ העברים</span> “I was stolen away out of the land of the Hebrews” (xl, 15). Moses, however, did not declare his land and origin. When Jethro’s daughters informed their father, <span dir="rtl">איש מצרי הצילנו</span> “An Egyptian man delivered us out of the hand of the shepherds,” Moses would not refute their designation. Therefore he could not be buried in Palestinian soil.

## Israel Maintained by Law

<span dir="rtl">ועתה ישראל שמע אל-החקים ואל המשפטים אשר אנכי מלמד אתכם לעשות, למען תחיו</span> “Now therefore hearken, O Israel, unto the judgments which I teach you, for to do them, that ye may live” (iv, 1). The Maggid of Kobrin comments: “Why does God address them in the singular? Because to hearken, they must become as one. Only as a completely unified people are they enabled to carry into life the statutes and judgments taught by the Torah. ‘That ye may live’ proclaims that if all Jews everywhere observe the Law as one, they will never vanish from the earth.”

<span dir="rtl">מלכות נכנסת ומלכות יוצאת וישראל עומד לעולם</span> “Kingdoms rise and kingdoms pass, but Israel remains forever” (Ecclesiastes Rabbah 1). History is replete with stories of governments which persecuted the Jews and sought to annihilate them; but these have disappeared, while the Jew lives after them.

<span dir="rtl">לא תוספו על-הדבר אשר אנכי מצוה אתכם ולא תגרעו ממנו</span> “Ye shall not add unto the word which I command you, neither shall you diminish ought from it” (iv, 2). Says Rabbi Jacob Reines, “A Jew must seek no reward, either in this or the other world, for observing the Law. Neither should the pious seek an excess of otherworldliness by adding to the severity of the statutes; nor should the Reformists diminish and reduce them in order to find a greater share of this worldliness. There are two seemingly contradictory phrases in Psalms: <span dir="rtl">כי גדל מעל-שמים חסדך</span> ‘For great above the heavens is Thy kindness’ (cviii, 5); and <span dir="rtl">כי גדל עד-שמים חסדך</span> ‘For great, even unto the heavens, is Thy kindness’ (lvii, 11). The contradiction is explained by the words of Pesahim (52b): <span dir="rtl">כאן בעושים לשמה וכאן בעושים שלא לשמה</span> ‘A command performed for its own sake and not for reward is great above the heavens; but one performed for the sake of reward reaches up to the heavens but no higher’ (Sefer Arakhim).”

<span dir="rtl">ואתם הדבקים בה</span>’ <span dir="rtl">אלהיכם חיים כלכם היום</span> “But ye that did cleave unto the Eternal your God are alive all of you this day” (iv, 4).

<span dir="rtl">אפילו בשעה שכל העולם מתים</span>—<span dir="rtl">אתם חיים</span> “Even at such a time when all the world shall die, ye shall remain alive” (Sanhedrin 90b). Other nations lay greatest stress on their economic status; this engenders wars, which spread death and destruction. But those who hold to God’s word are to be delivered from the death drama and given eternal life.

There is an old grandmother’s tale covering this matter. “On the Messiah’s advent, all other peoples will embark on a bridge of steel, yet will fall into the abyss. Israel will proceed on a paper bridge, yet will cross in safety.” The cannon and other steel weapons with which the nations maintain themselves will some day drown them all. But the Jews, who live only by the writings of the Law, mere paper supports, will always pass over undisturbed.
