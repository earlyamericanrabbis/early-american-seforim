<span dir="rtl">אם בחקותי תלכו ואת מצותי תשמרו</span> “If ye walk in my ordinances, and keep my commandments” — this is the condition wherewith God would treat with the Israelites, if they desired the continuance of their national existence. Heaven and earth would serve their cause if they conducted themselves according to the precepts of the Torah. <span dir="rtl">ונתתי גשמיכם בעתם ונתנה הארץ יבולה</span> “Then will I give you rain in its season, and the land shall yield its produce” (xxvi, 4). Then would they become economically and politically powerful and achieve independence and approbation, for they would remain the possessors of all they had labored for.

<span dir="rtl">ואכלתם לחמכם לשבע וישבתם לבטח בארצכם</span>

“And ye shall eat your bread to the full, and abide in your land safely” (5).

If, however, the Israelites refused to accept this condition, they would be forced out of the land and, in alien territory, fail to benefit by the blessings outlined to them. No matter how well it might be with them, no matter how wealthy they might wax, so long as they drew their sustenance out of strange earth, their food would lack savor and they could enjoy no true satiety. Life in exile has no permanence; it is too dependent upon the doubtful good will of neighboring majorities. There is no ground under one’s feet, there is no assurance of security.

<span dir="rtl">בארצכם אתם יושבים לבטח ואין אתם יושבים לבטח חוצה לה</span> “Only in your own land are you able to abide in safety; but you cannot remain secure in lands without its borders” (Torat Kohanim).

“If ye keep my commandments,” you will ever ascend to higher spiritual planes. <span dir="rtl">ונתתי משכני בתוככם</span> “And I will set my dwelling amongst you” — my presence will still further elevate you. Then will the life of the people, glorified by godliness and idealism, be distinguished from the ways of other nations. And the name of the Lord will be exalted. <span dir="rtl">והייתי לכם לאלהים ואתם תהיו לי לעם</span> “And I will be your God, and ye shall be my people” (12). These facts are mutual and interdependent — when Israel has its own national home, God Himself is possessed of a dwelling place.

<span dir="rtl">כל הדר בחוץ לארץ דומה כמו שאין לו אלקים</span> “He who dwells outside the land is like unto one who has no God” (Ketubot 111). The Divine Presence cannot find Himself in foreign parts — as though He were also in alien exile. Regardless of piety and spiritual progress, the excellences of Jews in strange lands is accounted neither to their nation nor their God. The very process of acclimatization robs them of individuality. Alien cultures show strongly in their spirits; they lose their distinctiveness.

The demands of Exile are tremendous. It retains all virtues and spiritual attainments for itself, and leaves to the people only failings and defeats. Their geniuses, artists, and spiritual leaders are reckoned as part of the several nations among whom they dwell; to the credit (or rather, discredit) of the Jew are reckoned only those attributes conducive to disgrace, and which may be employed as justification for attack and persecution.

<span dir="rtl">ויסרתי אתכם אף אני</span> “When I punish you, I also punish Myself,” reads a passage from the “Tania.” Faith and culture thrive in a nation only when it is spiritually independent, and is not compelled by material and political considerations to subject itself to the influence of others, to alien thoughts and attributes. <span dir="rtl">ולא תגעל נפשי אתכם</span> “And My soul shall not loathe you.” The Lord wishes to remain God of his people, and that the people remain forever His. The Midrash declares: “All the blessings and curses in this portion begin with a waw conversive, which transforms a future verb to a past or a past to a future. The thought here is: <span dir="rtl">אם זכיתם הריני הופך לכם קללות לברכות</span> ‘If ye are worthy, I shall transform all your curses into blessings’ ” (Vayikra Rabbah 25).

## Few Blessings; Many Curses

The Talmud deplores the fact that in the present portion Moses gave voice to too few blessings and too many curses. <span dir="rtl">ברכם בשמונה וקללם בעשרים ושתים</span> “He gave them but eight blessings, and of curses twenty-two” (Baba Batra 88b). The reason for this disparity is simple — blessings are all-embracing; the fortunate man has luck in everything. He need not worry about details; without undue effort he rises steadily. <span dir="rtl">זכו מלאכתן נעשה ע</span>”<span dir="rtl">י אחרים</span> “Those who have good fortune find that even their work is performed by others.” But of misfortunes and curses there are a plethora. He whom bad fortune pursues finds no rest; he must guard every step, insomuch as one undue event brings on another, and nullifies his every effort.

<span dir="rtl">ותם לריק כחכם</span> “And your strength shall be spent in vain” (20).

<span dir="rtl">דומה למשיא את בתו ונותן לה ממון הרבה, ולא הספיקו שבעת ימי המשתה לצאת עד שמתה בתו</span>—<span dir="rtl">נמצא קובר את בתו ומאבד את ממנו</span> “This is likened to one who marries off his daughter and gives her much money, and she dies ere the end of the seven days of feasting — he has thus both buried his daughter and lost his money” (Torat Kohanim). This is the tragic fate of those who live in exile. They labor for the sake of their children, and seek their happiness by bestowing upon them all the results of that toil, yet permit the youthful minds to be wed to alien ideals and beliefs. Ere one can look about, the children are departed, and the money expended on them is lost. All the efforts of the parents go for naught.

<span dir="rtl">ואכלתם בשר בניכם</span> “And ye shall eat the flesh of your sons.” When they are young, instead of sending them to the proper schools to study the Torah, you place them in shops and stores to earn their living, and in latter years must look to them for sustenance. <span dir="rtl">ונאספתם אל עריכם</span> “And ye shall be gathered together within your cities” (25) — Rabbi Solomon Aaronson, late chief rabbi of Tel Aviv, thus translated this passage — “You shall have to concentrate in the cities, for you will not be admitted to the villages and to work on the soil.”

<span dir="rtl">והשימותי את מקדשיכם</span> “And I will bring your sanctuaries unto desolation” (31): <span dir="rtl">לרבות בתי כנסיות ובתי מדרשות</span> “This phrase also includes your temples and synagogues” (Torat Kohanim). You will always be driven for a livelihood, and this will cause you to forsake your houses of worship and your academies, the immemorial citadels against assimilation and extinction. <span dir="rtl">ואבדתם בגוים</span> “And ye shall perish among the nations” (38), and your children will rush from land to land with <span dir="rtl">מורך בלבבם</span>, “with fear in their hearts.” and a deep despair.

As is commonly known, Rabbi Levi Isaac Berdichever always loved to engage in discussion with the Lord. Once during the week that the <span dir="rtl">תוכחה</span>, God’s chastisement, was read, he suddenly threw off his phylacteries in the midst of prayer, and cried, “Oh, Master of the universe! The Talmud relates that You too wear tefillin, and that in these is inscribed the words, Who is like unto Thy people Israel! Now I ask — even when the tefillin of an ordinary Jew fall to the floor, he lifts them up and kisses them. Why then do You permit Your tefillin, the people of Israel, to welter in the mire and become everywhere a shame and a byword?”

## Yet We Shall Not Die

Though the curses are fearsome and life in exile is highly tragic, yet the Jews are assured of perpetuation. No matter in what lands or situations they find themselves, God will not utterly forsake them; he will visit them from time to time. <span dir="rtl">ואף גם זאת בהיותם בארץ אויביהם לא מאסתים-לכלותם</span> “And yet... when they be in the land of their enemies, I will not cast them away... to consume them” (44). This is likened by the Zohar to one who espouses a bride living in a street of dyers, from which rises an evil odor. Ordinarily he would never come there, but he visits the street for the sake of his bride. So God himself, although His Jews dwell in the land of their enemies, unpleasing and unworthy of His sojourn, will come to Israel as though the people were His beloved bride. (The word *lekalotam*, meaning “to consume them” or “make an end of them” has the same root letters as *kallah*, “bride.”)

It is certain that the land will not be permanently occupied by others, but will wait for its Jews. Even though <span dir="rtl">והשמותי אני את הארץ</span> “I will bring the land into desolation” and <span dir="rtl">ושממו עליה אויביכם</span> “your enemies shall be amazed at it” (32). <span dir="rtl">זו מדה טובה</span>—<span dir="rtl">הארץ לא תתן כחה לזרים</span> “This is a pleasing fact — the land will not yield its strength to strangers” (Torat Kohanim). All the nations that sought settlement of Palestine have at length been compelled to forsake it. The land would surrender itself to none of these, until its own children, the Jews, returned to establish themselves thereon. <span dir="rtl">אף אילני סרק עתידים להיות עושים פירות</span> “Even withered trees will bear fruit in days to come” (ibid.).

Under the ministering of Jewish hands the land of Israel is attaining new life, and once again becoming “a land flowing with milk and honey.”
