Many of the aggadists of Talmud and Midrash discover in the story of Noah an analogue to Jewish life in exile. The generation of the flood represents the nations of the world; and the flood is a great catastrophe brought about by the peoples themselves. Noah is depicted as the Jewish people, and the ark as the home wherein he and his children find salvation.

It will be well to select from these many legendary accounts the details that will best depict Noah and the flood.

The humans who populated the earth before the deluge found themselves in a wondrous state. Nature provided them richly with all its abundance; they lacked for nothing. The fields were replete with grain, their trees with fruit, and the meadows with sheep and cattle. But the very riches granted them by God were instruments of their downfall. “They began to pursue new desires and corporeal lusts, gradually losing their mutual regard and becoming presumptuous even toward God” (Sanhedrin 108).

Envy and hatred ruled the world. The powerful man, observing that the weaker had a handsome home or domestic animal or wife, had no compunction against depriving him of his possession. All classes of men indulged in petty theft and robbery. Morality was at its lowest ebb. Even the <span dir="rtl">בני אלקים</span> “sons of the gods,” the higher and more learned classes, became <span dir="rtl">נפילים</span> “fallen angels,” depraved characters undermining the fundaments of human society. If on the highways they noted <span dir="rtl">בנות האדם כי טובות הנה</span> “the daughters of men, that they were fair,” <span dir="rtl">ויקחו מכל אשר בחרו</span> “they possessed themselves of all that they chose” without qualms, and <span dir="rtl">בלי נשואים ובלי חיי משפחה</span> “without marriage or proper family life” (Sanhedrin 57; Midrash Rabbah 31).

## Man of Peace and Honor

At that time there lived an honorable and peaceful man, <span dir="rtl">צדיק תמים</span> “righteous and perfect,” called Noah. He was also known as <span dir="rtl">נחמני</span> “the comforter,” forasmuch as he had invented the iron plow, which so greatly facilitated men’s work in the fields and increased the bulk of their produce. Of him they said: <span dir="rtl">זה ינחמנו מעצבון ידינו</span> “This man has given us comfort from the melancholy of our hands.” Before his time men had been forced to plow the fields with their hands or with sharp sticks, so that they suffered from callouses and open wounds. His discovery lightened their labors and enriched the world’s agriculture (Midrash Aggadah 8).

When Noah beheld the evil acts of his neighbors, he entreated them to better their conduct, and warned that their wickedness would bring on a catastrophe that would destroy all humanity. But they ridiculed him and continued on their sinful course, until <span dir="rtl">ותמלא הארץ חמס</span> “the earth was filled with violence” (Midrash Rabbah 31).

To preserve himself and his offspring from the imminent danger, he constructed an “ark,” a special home and school, in which he taught his children to remain pious and well-behaved, and to cleave to the traditions of their ancestor Enoch, who because of his goodness and piety had been transported unto Heaven. The ark was built of cedar wood, which God assigned for that purpose; and no matter of evil was permitted to approach it (Sanhedrin 109).

When the others saw that he had separated himself from them, and would not permit his sons to marry strange women or otherwise follow in their paths, they assailed him in his dwelling, in an effort to injure him and destroy the ark. He defended himself successfully, however, and with God’s aid was enabled to maintain his home and the purity of his family (Midrash Shochar Tov).

## Death and Destruction

At length the catastrophe befell. A fearful deluge overwhelmed <span dir="rtl">את כל היקום</span> all creation, and continued over the earth for an entire year. Cities were laid waste. Houses, fields, forests, were wiped off the globe. Men and beasts succumbed alike. There were swept away the labor and energy of many generations. All things fell before the great billows of doom.

Noah and his family were forced to uproot themselves from their birthplace, and in the ark waft over all the seas of earth. So well constructed was the ark that it withstood the forces of wind and wave and preserved the family of Noah and all who were with him.

The ship too had its vicissitudes. At times it attained terrific heights, as when it floated over Mount Ararat, highest mountain of that era. But never was Noah tempted to forsake the ark, for he knew that so long as he dwelt therein, in accord with the precepts of God, he was protected against dissolution (Zeror Hamor 17).

After this long period of death and destruction, the flood gradually subsided. <span dir="rtl">וישכו המים</span> the water lost its turbulence and the earth began to breathe once more. But Noah still feared to forsake his refuge. He sent forth a dove to see whether <span dir="rtl">הקלו המים</span> “the waters were abated” for the safety of them all. The dove, however, brought back the depressing message <span dir="rtl">ולא מצאה היונה מנוח לכף רגלה</span> she had “found no rest for the sole of her foot.” For the remainder of humankind the deluge had ended; only Noah and his own were to abide in unchanged isolation.

## The Dove — Symbol of Israel

The dove remains the symbol of the entire Jewish nation: <span dir="rtl">נמשלו ישראל ליונה</span> “Israel is likened unto a dove.” Day on day she flits about, seeking a resting place for herself, for Noah and his descendants, for the ark of Israel — but to no avail. She returns at length with an olive leaf, and with this plaint:

<span dir="rtl">רבש</span>”<span dir="rtl">ע, יהי מזונותי מרורין כזית ויהיו מסורין בידך, ואל יהיו מתוקים כדבש ומסורין ביד אדם</span>

“Master of the universe! May my food be as bitter as this olive leaf, only that it come from Thy hand, rather than it be sweet as honey, but come from the hands of men!” (Sanhedrin 28). She prays for the time when she and the upright Noah may be redeemed from the gloom of exile, and the people of Israel achieve an independent state like other nations, with their own home on their own land.

The Jerusalem Talmud (Sabbath) thus poignantly replies to the query <span dir="rtl">למה נמשלו ישראל ליונה</span> “Wherefore is Israel likened to a dove?” <span dir="rtl">מפני שכל העופות כשהן עפות ויגעות הן נחות על גבי אילן או על גבי סלע</span> “For all other birds, as they fly and grow weary, rest themselves atop a tree or a rock” <span dir="rtl">אבל יונה זן קופצת אחת מאגפיה ופורחת באחת מאגפיה</span> “whereas the dove merely folds one of her wings and flies with the other.”

All other peoples of the earth must have the use of two wings in order to maintain themselves — land and culture. Whenever one has lost its land, its culture has died as well; its children have been assimilated among the more powerful and affluent nations —the “tree” and the “rock” — and all living traces of the people have disappeared. But when the people of Israel lost their land, they did not yield up their culture, their Torah. With the one remaining wing — a spiritual gift from Mount Sinai — Israel has remained aloft and unbroken as a distinct and functioning ethnic being. On one wing this people has soared through centuries of exile in many lands, ultimately to fly back to its own home, the land of Israel.
