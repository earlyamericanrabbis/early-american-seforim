The two particular outstanding features in the lives of the ancient Hebrews from Abraham to Moses, were: the belief in Monotheism, and the Revelation upon Mt. Sinai. These two events directly concerned the religious belief and practice of the people of Israel. Now, in order to give an outline of the growth of the belief in One God and the progress of the religion proclaimed upon Sinai, it is necessary to penetrate deeper and to advance further in the history of the development of Judaism as a religion, and of the laws of the Torah as a civilization.

In the Introduction to the Mishnah, Talmudic sources were brought to relate the story of how Moses instructed the people. Moses himself instructed each law three times; once in the presence of Aaron, once in the presence of Aaron's sons, and once in the presence of the Seventy Elders. Then Moses departed, and Aaron gave the instructions as he had heard it expounded by Moses. Then Aaron retired, and his sons instructed; then his sons retired, and the Elders instructed. These instructions referred to the interpretation of the Torah as enunciated by the authors of the Mishnah and the Talmud. But the question remains: How did the people of Israel practice their religion prior to the construction of the Mishnah and the Talmud?

In order to be able to get an elementary understanding of what Judaism as a religion consists of it is necessary first to understand the conditions under which the people of Israel lived in Palestine.

Some people believe that the entire business of the Jews as a nation always consisted of religion and wars on account of their God. Most histories of the Jewish people, even that of Josephus in ancient times and of Graetz in modern times, make it appear that the entire Jewish question was based upon religion; that every war which the Jews fought was on account of religion; that every rebellion against a dynasty was on account of religion; and that Jewish life began with religion and ended with religion. An ordinary layman may ask: "What did the Jews do when they came into Palestine? How did they make a living? There had to be some people to till the ground, to move the crops from the fields into markets where the city dweller could obtain it; who, then, were their farmers, their tailors, their shoemakers? They needed houses; who were their stonemasons, their working men in the stone quarries? Why have no records of their lives as they did live during that time been given by the historians?"

Educators find fault with the method of teaching the Jewish youth, because the entire subject of instruction covers only the limited narrow channel, that of religious observances, and the youth remain entirely ignorant as to what happened in Palestine when Joshua finally succeeded in conquering all of the natives of the land and in allotting different sections of Palestine to the different tribes, besides the recorded failures or triumphs of the Jewish religion.

But there are facts which no historian brought to light but which are found in our religious writings.

We have a law in the Bible that Palestine (and that was the only homeland for the Jews mentioned in our Torah) must observe a Sabbatical Year after every six years of work. In other words, during every seventh year the ground must be left fallow. No one was permitted to till the ground during a Sabbatical Year. Every farmer had to open his gates; every orchard, every vineyard, every field was practically ownerless in so far as the crop was concerned. Cattle and all beasts of burden went free all through the country, on every farm. The poor, and there always were poor, came in at will and took the fruit of the trees and the second crops of the fields without hindrance. It was theirs to glean and gather; it belonged to nobody and to everybody. Back of that law is a great study in agriculture. To leave the ground of a given country fallow one year out of every seven and permit the stock to roam about all over, is not only an improvement, but it actually is the very life of a farm. A traveller through the New England States will find in Vermont, New Hampshire, and in part of Massachusetts, many farms completely abandoned; log cabins with window panes broken, the walls almost falling apart, ownerless, no one to rent or even take the farm as a gift. The land, although not very old, comparatively speaking, as for instance, European or Palestinian land which had been tilled thousands of years before America was discovered, seems to refuse to yield a fair return to the farmer. Why? There is a natural reason. The surface of the ground has a richness which causes production. The thickness of that surface of the soil is not more than from 6 to 10 inches. A modern tractor does not make furrows deeper than about six inches. When the surface of the ground is continuously worked, year in and year out, the soil becomes poor, and the problem of nitrate, phosphate, or ordinary fertilizer becomes a matter of life and death to the soil. If the abuse be too severe, the ground will never yield a fair return after deducting the expenditure on fertilizer. Such conditions really were prevalent on the farms in New England; they had to be abandoned because the surface of the soil was entirely used up. The preventive measures of crop rotation and of leaving the land fallow were not taken, and the result was deplorable.

Now, the first law that Joshua proclaimed was concerning the Sabbatical Year. Sabbatical Year in Hebrew is called "Sh'mitah." The word in Hebrew means "abandoning." Fourteen years elapsed from the time the Jews entered Palestine till they observed the first Sabbatical Year. This delay was because Joshua wanted first to conquer all the natives before making the allotments of the land to such of the tribes who had as yet no territory of their own. That took seven years; and, as the people had not yet settled in their respective allotments, the law concerning Sh'mitah was inapplicable until the first year after their permanent settlement on the land was fully established. Con sequently, they spent seven years in conquering the country and in allotting the different sections of the country to the different tribes; then they tilled the ground six years, and on the fourteenth year they observed the first Sabbatical Year as a nation in the Promised Land. That was the first religious law affecting the lives of the people Joshua ordered them to observe. Now, agriculture, the stay and staff of the world's population, has a great deal to do with religious observances, although it is not religious in the sense that the non-Jew understands it, because after the Jew planted his vineyard, seeded his crop, and waited until it ripened, he could not come to his field, his orchard, or his vineyard, take it to his barn, cellar or courtyard, and ear it, press it or sell it; it was religiously impossible for him to do so.

When he came to the vine to glean the clusters of grapes he had to remember the poor to leave some gleanings for them; when he came to the field to reap the harvest he had to remember the poor and leave some corner standing for them; and when he was about to take the sheaves of grain to his barn he had to leave a sheave for them. It was mandatory to leave gleanings, to leave a corner of the field, and to forget a sheave. These three precepts are called in Hebrew: (1) Leket (gleaning); (2) Shikha (forgetting); (3) Peah (corner); that was the share of the poor, not a donation, not charity, but their just share in the yearly crops of the land during the working years.

When the Sabbatical year came, there was a complete abandonment of indebtedness, or "prescription." According to the laws of Sh'mitah every debtor was automatically released from his creditor. The time-limit of any financial obligation was the Sabbatical year; in other words, no creditor could demand a judgment against a debtor in any court of justice throughout the land of Israel the moment a Sabbatical year was ushered in.

At a much later date, when the reins of government had already been in the hands of the Romans, Hillel the Elder, instituted a corrective measure for the benefit of both debtor and creditor to prevent economic losses as well as restraint to trade which the law of prescription during the Sabbatical year usually brought about. That remedy is known as Prusbul, or Prusbulibuti, the literal meaning whereof in Persian is: "a proclamation for high and low" (rich and poor). The remedy of Prusbul consists of a written notice filed by the creditor in the presence of a tribunal or witnesses, the following of which is a copy of the form used:

" I, (naming himself) do hereby, in your presence (naming tribunal or witnesses), give public notice that I will demand and collect my debts without a time-limit, at any time I will desire" (then the witnesses or the members of the tribunal sign the instrument).

The laws of Sh'mitah are inapplicable to loans made payable on demand.

But that remedy to avoid prescription of debts during the Sabbatical years, was it was pointed out, was instituted at a time when the Jews were already governed by the Romans. The laws of Sh'mitah were in operation almost fourteen centuries in Palestine before the Prusbul was instituted.

At the time when the first Sabbatical year was celebrated in Palestine there was no debtor and no creditor. The people took the land as it was given to them, each one receiving that section which fell as his lot. In addition to the laws of Sh'mitah, Leket, Shikha, Peah, the Jews in Palestine had other laws to comply with before they could partake of their crops. There was the tribe of Levi to be provided for. That tribe did not share in the land-allotments, and therefore, provision had to be made for their sustenance.

The tribe of Levi was selected from the very beginning of Jewish history to serve the nation in a spiritual capacity. The academy founded by Eber was presided over successively by Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. Before his demise Jacob appointed his third son Levi to the presidency of that institute. Levi, in turn, charged all of his descendants to devote their lives to intellectual and spiritual work. The Egyptians, even when plotting the destruction of the children of Israel, left the tribe of Levi unmolested. While the Children of Israel lived in Egypt they followed the custom of having the first-born in every family attend to their spiritual needs; but when they came forth out of Egypt and were encamped on the Sinaitic peninsula, Moses received the command to take that spiritual function away from the first-born of all tribes and invest the tribe of Levi with that sacredness.

Therefore every first-born was, by virtue of his birth, the priest and teacher of his family. That change, perhaps, more than any other recorded incident in connection with the Exodus left a recorded chapter of the history of the inner life of the Jewish people preceding their liberation. It is recorded that the number of first-born among all the tribes who were the spiritual heads of the people totaled 22278, and that the number of the Levites totaled 22000. But not all of the Levites were priests. That distinguished function belonged to the direct descendants of Aaron.

After the ordination of the tribe of Levi, the services they were to render took on a more organized form than the prevailing custom among the first-born, whom they suppressed. The sons of Aaron and their descendants were assigned to the most solemn duties. They were named Cohenim. The descendants of Gershon, Kohath, and Merari, sons of Levi, were assigned to lesser duties. They were the Levites. During the wandering in the wilderness, the work was divided among the Levites in three parts as follows: Their chief occupation was to transport the Tabernacle from Camp to Camp. The Cohanim, first took the Tabernacle apart and prepared it for transportation, then the order of moving it was arranged among the Levites in three sections. The descendants of the family of Gershon were entrusted to carry the container of the Tabernacle, and the tent of meeting, its covering, and the covering of sealskin that is above upon it, and the screen for the door of the tent of meeting; and the hangings of the court and the screen for the door of the gate of the court, which is by the Tabernacle and by the altar round about, and their cords, and all the instruments of their service. The means of transportation supplied to the sons of Gershon were two wagons and four oxen. The descendants of Kohath carried the ark of the covenant, the Menorah, the altars, the table of the Show Bread, all the vessels of the ministry, wherewith the priests (Cohanim) ministers in the sanctuary. This being the most sacred service among the Levites, no means of transportation was given to them, and they had to carry their charge upon their shoulders. The descendants of Merari carried the boards of the Tabernacle, and bars thereof, and the pillars thereof, and the sockits thereof; and the pillars of the court round about, and their sockits, and their pins, and their cover, even all their appurtenance, and all that pertained to their service. To them were given four wagons and eight oxen.

Thus were the Cohenim and the three grades of Levites employed in the dismantlement of the Tabernacle and in moving it from Camp to Camp.

When the Children of Israel pitched their tents and rested, the Tabernacle was set up and the Cohenim and Levites were stationed around the Tabernacle as follows: the sons of Aaron (Cohe-nim) to the east of the Tabernacle; the descendants of Gershon to its west, the descendants of Kohath to its south, and the descendants of Merari to its north. When the promised Land was reached and the Tabernacle was no longer moved from place to place, the duties of the Cohenim and Levites changed accordingly. Then, only part of their number remained in actual service, and the rest of them settled in the cities which were assigned to them at the time of the conquest. The Cohanim and Levites who were not in the service of the Tabernacle devoted their lives to study and instruction, to heal the sick with the scientific means at their command, regulated by the Levitical laws of hygiene, and to spiritual leadership which kept the people united and encouraged them in times of danger and adversity. Their material needs were supplied to them by means of tithes (Ma'aser). Before anyone could partake of his crops or vintage, herd or flock, he had to give one-tenth thereof to the Levites.

In all there were three kinds of tithes: (1) That given to the Levites, which was called " first tithes" (Ma'aser Rishon); (2) the tithe which was to be taken to Jerusalem and there consumed by the landowner and his family, which was called "second tithes" (Ma'aser Sheni), it being taken from what remained after the first tithes had been appropriated; (3) The tithe given to the poor (Ma'aser Ani). Therefore two tithes had to be taken every working year, as follows: First and second tithes were taken in the first, second, fourth and fifth years; and first and third (Ma'aser Ani), in the third and sixth years.

Consequently, the laws pertaining to agricul ture, before any other religious laws, touched the very vitality of the people as a nation. The duties of Cohen and Levite were of such character which kept them in constant touch with all of the people, and gave them the opportunity to lead the people forward in every field of spiritual, material, social and governmental endeavor.

The four centuries of Jewish history between Moses and David teach much more than it appears in the pages of the historian. The progress of Judaism, as such, does not appear on the surface to have extended any further than the endeavor of David to exchange the Tabernacle for a Sanctuary, the "Tent of Meeting" for a " House of God,' the "Mishkan" for a " Beth ha-Mikdash." During that time, in addition to the laws pertaining to agriculture and the several different pursuits that the peoples followed for their economic well-being, and in addition to the system of priesthood and Levitical observance, prophets and prophecy, a new form of government-a monarchy, and many other signs of cultural and sociological development appear upon the scenes of the Promised Land! From Joshua to Samuel the people of Israel continued to build upon solid rock, and it is, therefore, not at all strange to hear the cry of a determined nation for a leader who would be the civil head of the government, for a king. From Samuel to Nathan and Gad the prophets there coursed through the veins of the people a new stream of blood, a regeneration. Saul's experience at the house of the Witch of En-Dor, the very fact that in the time of distress he resorted to obtain the knowledge concerning the hidden mysteries of life and death to means unholy and contrary to the specific law of the Torah forbidding such practice is best evidence that Judaism had awakened the pure thoughts of the people long before that era. Saul himself had ordered the destruction of the purveyors of fanaticism and superstitious practices.

The universal character of Judaism does not lend itself to isolate one or more of its particular branches or phases. It is, therefore, impossible to sum up given instances of progress or failure and render judgment on its full scope. In government Judaism is secular, in the service of the Temple it is sacred; in education it is scientific, in observances it is religious; on economic questions it is business-like, on a question of humanity it is poetic; in making out justice it is stern, in bestowing mercy it is all heart.

The universal character of Judaism became a fact in the days of Solomon. Of the First Temple, when he dedicated it he spoke of, "My house is a house of prayer for all nations."

Abraham laid the foundation for a spiritual TEMPLE within the bosom of man. He proclaimed the belief in ONE God. Faith was his ideal. Solomon built a physical Temple, a house of worship. He proclaimed that faith should find expression. That expression was to come about in " a house of prayer."
