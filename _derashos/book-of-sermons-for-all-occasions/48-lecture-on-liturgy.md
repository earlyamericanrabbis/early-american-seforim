There is a great doubt in the minds of ecclesiastical historians as to when the human being commenced to express his gratitude, thanksgiving, or supplication by word of mouth, by prayer. The early Egyptians, Persians, Chinese and all other ancient races who have had religious practices prior to Abraham, or prior to recorded history, as evidenced in the unearthed fragmentary remains of their temples, altars and pillars, rarely expressed their feeling in prayer. The form of worship offered to Mercury was not praying to it, imploring it, or giving thanks to it, but it was expressed by means of action. Strange as it may sound to modern people the form of service was by casting stones at the image of the deity. Others offered sacrifices in honor of their deity usually sacrifices of human beings, praticularly children. The latter pactice was in honor of as it is known in Hebrew Molech.

That indicates that the primitive mind did not express itself by word of mouth when inspired with religious zeal or when called upon by the priests to worship the deity.

According to Talmudic authorities there were three main forms of worship practiced by primitive races: (1) Prostration on the ground; (2) Sacrifices; (3) Bodily expression, by some member of the body, or by the whole body.

Liturgy in Judaism is divided into two branches: (1) Instruction; (2) Prayer. Abraham gave instructions and delivered prayers. Of Adam it is related that when God appeared to him and asked him: "Where art thou?" Adam did not deliver a prayer for forgiveness. Then again when Cain killed his brother, Abel, and God asked him "Where is thy brother Abel?" it is not recorded that he prayed and became a penitent. Instead, he answered "Am I my brother's keeper?" So it is evident that primitive men did not have the conception of expressing themselves in prayer. Again, it is not recorded that during the deluvian period, none in that generation, not even Noah prayed for deliverance. It, however is recorded that when the ark finally settled down, and the land became day, Noah offered sacrifices. The first prayer on record is the one Abraham delivered for Abimelech, king of Gerar. Isaac and Rebekah were childless and the Bible relates that both of them prayed to God; and Jacob prayed when he was on his way to Haran. It is traditionally related that Abraham offered a prayer to God early on that morning when he proceeded to Mount Moriah there to offer his son Isaac as a sacrifice. From that the Talmudists infer that Abraham instituted the morning prayer (Shaharit).

It is related in the Bible that when Rebekah was approaching Abraham's house, Isaac went out to the field toward evening to deliver himself of a prayer. The Talmudists infer therefrom that Isaac instituted the afternoon service (Minhah).

Jacob prayed. He prayed at night. It is related that when Jacob fled from the anger of his brother, Esau, and went to his mother's brother, Laban, he prayed at the place where he alighted to spend the night. Herefrom the Talmudists inferred that Jacob instituted the evening service (Mairib).

In this way prayers commenced to develop. Historically, there is no doubt that Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob prayed, but the prayers were not a fixed set. Mirian, the sister of Moses and Aaron, spoke against Moses because he had married an Ethiopian woman, Ziporah, daughter of Jethro, the Medionite priest. God punished her, and she was stricken with leprosy. Then Aaron appealed to Moses, saying to him; "O, my Lord, lay not, I pray thee, sin upon us for that we have done foolishly and for that we have sinned. Let her not, I pray, be as one dead of whom the flesh is half consumed when he cometh out of his mother's womb; and Moses cried (prayed) unto the Lord (the first prayer on record in the Bible, saying: "Heal her now, O Lord, I beseech Thee." Moses prayed to God to heal his sister, Miriam. He employed eight words. In Hebrew it is five words: "El na repha na lah."

Of course, Moses prayed before that, when the Jewish people had made a golden calf and worshipped it, and God wanted to destroy the entire people. His prayer there was of greater length than this; but that was a plead for the entire people of Israel on an extraordinary occasion, which called forth much pleading and supplication. Every prayer has three elements: (1) thanksgiving; (2) praise; (3) supplication. For example: when we rise in the morning we thank God because He permitted us to live another day; that is thanksgiving. When we glorify God by acknowledging that He is all-powerful, that is praise. When we implore God to spare our health, that is supplication. Every prayer is composed of these three ele ments.

It is evident from the Bible that there were no fixed sets of prayers, either for every day or for the Sabbath, or Holidays. Every individual, as the occasion required delivered a prayer. When one had some happiness come to him he gave thanks to God but expressed it in his own words, in his own way. Hannah who was childless, came to the Tabernacle in Shiloh and prayed unto the Lord. "And it came to pass, as she prayed long before the Lord, that Eli watched her mouth. Now, Hannah was speaking in her heart, only her lips moved, but her voice could not be heard; therefore Eli thought that she had been drunken." Eli should have known the difference between a kneeling woman in prayer and a drunken woman in a stupper. But the whole procedure was not in accordance with the custom in vogue. People came to the Tabernacle to offer sacrifices. Hannah's afflicted soul taught her to pour out her overburdened heart to God; that was something Eli could not make out; such devout meditation was not a daily occurrence; he therefore, considered her a drunken woman. And Hannah answered, and said: " No, my Lord, I am a woman of sorrowful spirit. I have drunken neither wine nor strong drink, but I poured out my soul before the Lord." Hannah, when a male child was given to her by the Lord delivered herself of one of the finest prayers the world treasures. First is thanksgiving: "My heart exalteth in the Lord. My horn is exalted in the Lord. My mouth is enlarged over mine enemies. Because I rejoice in Thy salvation." Next is praise: "There is none holy as the Lord For there is none beside Thee, Neither is there any rock like our God." Then comes supplication: "He will keep the feet of His holy ones, But the wicked shall be put to silence in

darkness; For not by strength shall man prevail. They that strive with the Lord shall be

broken to pieces; Against them will He thunder in heaven; The Lord will judge the ends of the earth; And He will give strength unto His king, And exalt the horn of His anointed. " Part of the Jewish liturgy was produced by the prophets. This contribution, however, was confined mainly to instruction. In the time of Isa iah the people were assembled in the courts of the Temple to receive instruction from the prophets and to pray.

It is quite true that there appears no record of a uniform ritual in the pre-exilic period; but it is likewise true that the foundation for the prayers was laid at the very earilest time in Jewish history. In the Temple services were conducted daily, morning and evening. The Kevites recited prayers of thanksgiving and praise during the morning and evening sacrifices. The recorded fact that Daniel " Kneeled upon his knees three times a day and prayed, and gave thanks before his God, as he did aforetime," is best evidence that the services at the Temple inspired individuals to pray three times daily. The afternoon services corresponded with the sacrifices offered by individuals between the Tamid sacrifice morning and evening. The Tamid was a continual burnt-offering of the entire people. The priests offered it morning and evening. The offerings were purchased with money contributed by every adult in the nation each of whom gave a minimum of a Shekel.

Of course the outstanding liturgy in the Temple were the psalms of King David. In fact what prophecy did to advance one division of liturgy, namely, instruction, Psalmody did for the other division of liturgy, namely, prayer. The eminence that Moses attained in prophecy David attained in Psalmody.

Now, there is a commandment charging the Children of Israel to offer a sacrifice daily. That commandment refers to the sacrifice known as Tamid. Tamid in Hebrew means "a continual burnt offering," the offering was brought in the morning and at dusk. Since all the people were charged to observe this commandment, and since the continual burnt-offering was purchased with the Shekels which were contributed by the entire nation, means had to be found how to secure the presence of the people at the time the Tamid sacrifices were offered. The presence of the one who offered the sacrifice was required under a mandatory law of the Torah.

To that end the Temple liturgy was divided so that the participation by all of the people therein was secured. Moses instituted the system of sections among those who conducted the services at the Temple. He divided them into eight sections, Samuel and David, and all the prophets immediately succeeding them after the Temple had been dedicated extending the sections to twentyfour.

The liturgy was thus participated in by three groups:

\(1\) Priests (Cohanim).

\(2\) Levites (Leviyim).

\(3\) Israelites (Yisraelim).

\(1\) The services of the priests were in connection with both public and private offerings.

\(2\) The services of the Levites consisted of the singing of the hymns and psalms.

\(3\) The services of the Israelites both at Jerusalem and throughout the country consisted of the reading the first chapter of Genesis.

For the purpose of conducting the services the entire nation had been divided into twenty-four sections, so that to each division of priests (Cohanim) there corresponded one division of Levites (Leviyim) and one division of Israelites (Yisra-elim), each section served for a week in the Temple, and this period was a time of fasting for the Israelites, that is to say that fasting was ordered on Mondays, Tuesdays, Wednesdays and Thursdays. They did not fast on Friday because it was the eve of the Sabbath, and of course no fasting was permitted on the Sabbath itself; neither was any fasting done on Sunday, so as not to order the people to enter into a contrast from the luxury of the Sabbath into a complete abstination of food, drink or any other pleasure immediately after the Sabbath. This form of service, accompanied by fasting was practiced by the section of the Israelites, appointed for services, both in Jerusalem and throughout the rural districts. Every day during such service a different portion of the first chapter of Genesis was read.

Some of the prayers instituted in early Palestinian life are mentioned by the prophets in connection with the climatic conditions of the country. The frequent scarcity of rain greatly distressed the people, for it meant famine and death to man and beast. At such times public assemblies for prayer and fasting were held as early as the time of the prophets in which old and young, the bride and groom took part (Jeremiah, 9.11; Joel I; 2.16-17).

The priestly benediction (Numbers 6.24-27) was pronounced four times daily during the sectional services, on fast days and on the day of at-tonement, when there were delivered four prayers at different times of the day, namely: morning (Shaharit), mid-day (Musaph); evening (Min-hah); and at sun-down (Neilah).

The main source wherein the development of early liturgy is described covers an entire tractate of the Talmud, namely: the tractate Ta'anit, both the Mishnah and the Talmudic text being devoted chiefly to the regulations of fast days and to the liturgical practices of the Jewish people from the earliest time till the close of the Talmud.

At the morning sacrifice of the burnt-offering (Tamid) the priests read the ten commandments and the verses of the Bible commencing with the word " Shema" (Numbers 6.4-9). It is therefore safe to maintain that the prayer of "Shema" was instituted after the First Temple had been built.

Contrary to the established rule that services on all Sabbaths and Holidays commence the preceeding evening at sunset, the Temple services began with sunrise, and not with the evening or the appearance of the moon. The rule was then established to watch the sun-dawn and immediately on report of the watchman that the day was breaking the Tamid was sacrificed. This being the rule there was some fear that some people might think that the sacrifice was in honor of the sun-god of the Persians, it was therefore required that the congregation should be addressed as follows:

"Hear, O Israel, the Eternal is our God;

The Eternal is one." This is the opening sentence of the "Shema", which sustains the accepted theory that the " Shema" was instituted as a public prayer after the dedication of the First Temple.
