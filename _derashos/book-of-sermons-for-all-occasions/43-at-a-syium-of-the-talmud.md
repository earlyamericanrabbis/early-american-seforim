Talmud is an ancient scholastic term of the Tannayim, and is a noun formed from the verb "Limmed "-to teach. Prmarily, Talmud means teaching, but it also denotes learning. Talmud and Torah is applied to the study of the Law, both in its wider and in its more restricted sense. To designate the study of Judaism the word Talmud is used in contrast with the word Ma'aseh, which connotes the practice of religion. Talmud is also used as an idiom together with the word " Lomar, " denoting instruction by means of the text of the Torah and of the exegetic deduction therefrom.

The Talmud as used in this sense is a post-Mishnaic production extending from the third to the close of the fifth century C. E. It is an elucidation of the Mishnah. The Halakah is not at all confined to strictly religious laws. The 613 commandments of the Torah cover fundamental law of every description. The Mishnah elucidates and interprets these 613 commandments and gives the laws concerning each and every commandment in a clarified form. That part was the work of the Tannayim. The Talmud still further elaborated and expanded the fundamental laws as found in the Torah and in the Mishnah, and is the work of the Amoraim. The Halakah of the Talmud covers:

1\. Revealed, or strictly religious laws.

2\. Natural laws.

3\. Public laws.

4\. Private laws.

\(1\) Revealed laws refer to every practice of a religious character, such as prayers, sacrifices, erection of a temple, priesthood, tithes, Sabbaths, Holidays, New Moon, tokens, etc.

\(2\) Natural laws: Such as the laws pertaining to the seasons, sun, moon, reckoning of time, intercalation, planetary system, relationship of the sexes, purification, contamination, hygiene, tracing descent through the father, and all other matters which have to do with the things which are natural.

\(3\) Public laws: Such as form ofgovernment, appointment of judges, officers, kings, Synhidrion, sanitation, conduct of warfare, rights of aliens, international relationship, etc.

\(4\) Private laws: Such as personal injuries, defamation, claims, inheritances, credit, measures, weights, and all kindred subjects which may affect the individual.

In addition to all of these the Talmud contains an inexhaustible mass of material which afforded the Amoraic academies a basis both for the interpretation and for the criticism of the Mishnah. The Talmud was further augmented by the inclusion within it of the views other scholars expressed in the course of their public, judicial, and other activities as well as by the data regarding their private lives and their religious practices which were discussed and memorized in the academies.

The Halakah contained in the Talmud is divided into two branches:

1\. The laws which received the sanction of all schools from the days of Moses till Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi, as well as during the Amoraic period. This is called in Hebrew " Halakah M'kuyemet."

2\. Rejected laws, which are called in Hebrew "Halakah D'huyah."

These two kinds of law were included in the Mishnah by Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi. The ancient scholars copied all that which they were instructed in, whether it was the governing law, or the rejected law. When Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi commenced to copy and edit the mass of material before him, he was forced, for the purpose of bringing out the governing laws, to include also the rejected laws, for had he done otherwise, his contemporaries or scholars in later generations would have quoted notes of former generations to contradict the Mishnaic law. It is so stated in the Tractate Ediyot, chapter 1, paragraph 5.

The G'mara covering all material points in order to clarify the Halakah, consists of twenty-one elementary parts, namely:

1\. TOSEFTA: Material which was left out by Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi, and which was subsequently added to the Mishnah to indicate in the Talmud that such material is derived from Tosefta. Such a paragraph begins with the word Tana. When such material appears in the Talmud immediately following the Mishnah it is considered a Halakah M'kuyemet.

2\. BARAITA: Material which the ancient scholars copied after the Mishnah was edited, as for example, the Mishnayot of Rabbi Hiyya; of Rabbi Oshaiah; and of Rabbi Eliezer ben-Jacob; the Mehilta of Rabbi Ishmael; and the Otiot of Rabbi Akiba; the Torath Cohanim which is the Sifra on Leviticus; the Safre on Numbers and Deuteronomy and their like.

To identify that such material comes from Baraita, the paragraph begins with the words "Tanu Rabbanan," or "Tany Hada," or "Tania Edach," and when such Baraita appears in the Talmud without a challenge, such is the law; but when the statements contained in such a Baraita were challenged, the law is followed according to the traditional decision.

3\. PIRUSH: Clarifying material which immediately follows the Mishnah, in order to elucidate the context of the Mishnah.

4\. SHAALAH: Seeking authoritative opinion. If it be one community from another, the matter commences with the words "Ebayah L'hu". If it be a community from an individual, the matter commences with the words "Bauh Minai. " If it be an individual from an individual, the matter commences " Boah Minai."

5\. T'SHUBAH: Response to a shaalah, and in accordance with the decision rendered by those responding, the law is established and becomes a Halakah M'kuyemet.

6\. KUSHIA: Interrogatory material concerning certain traditional interpretations con tained in the Mishnah. Such interrogations never appear concerning a Halakah M'kuyemet. If the controversalists were a number of scholars, the question commences with the word "Metebey," and if an individual scholar propounded the question, it begins with the word "Etebey."

7\. PIRUK: A rebuttal to a Kushia, and when the rebuttal remains unchallenged it becomes a Halakah M'kuyemet.

8\. TYUBTA: A contradiction supported by clear evidence; if such evidence remains uncon-traverted, it becomes a Halakah M'kuyemet.

9\. SIYUAH: Supporting evidence produced to strengthen a given law and to make it a Halakah M'kuyemet. Such support usually commences with the words "Layma M'syah Leh."

10\. RAMYAH: A differentiation which is produced in order not to sustain a comparison between two subjects. Such matter always begins with the word "V"reminhu."

11\. HAHAZRAKAH: Supporting matter brought to sustain a comparison between two subjects. Such matter always begins with the word "Zerikah."

12\. HAHATKAPHTA: Questions pro pounded by Amoraim. Such matter usually begins with the word "Matkiph," and the rules regarding the establishment of such a law is that of Ku-shia (Paragraph 6).

13\. MAASEH: A precedent brought to sustain a law propounded by someone; when the precedent brought for that purpose is analagous to the law at issue, such law becomes effective and is thenceonward considered binding.

14\. SHMATETA: A designation of every law which covers a subject mentioned in the Torah, but in no other matter.

15\. SUGYA: Proper reading of a given text of the G'mara and understanding it thoroughly, including the questions and answers in such texts.

16\. HILKETA: When there is a difference of opinion concerning a matter of law, and it became necessary to end the argument by saying that such was the law.

17\. TAYKU: When there is an even doubt concerning a given law and, therefore, impossible to render a judicial decision, such law is called Tayku, which is a Hebrew abbreviation, meaning: The Tishbite (the prophet Elijah), will solve all matters which were left unsolved (Tishbi Yeta rez Kushiyot V'abayot). The procedure, however, in questions of this kind is as follows: If the law is concerning property, it is decided in a minor premise and the sum involved is divided equally between the two contending parties. But if it affects a prohibitive matter, for example, connubial relationship, or forbidden food, it is decided in a major premise, and the subject at issue remains forbidden. Others, however, are of the opinion that when the right of property is questioned, the plaintiff must produce evidence and the defendant is not called upon to give even half of the sum involved. In forbidden matters, however, it is an established rule to continue to hold the matter at issue in a forbidden state.

18\. SHENUYE: When a scholar contends against the Mishnah, and that contention is disputed by others, he must support his contention by clear evidence. The designation that such scholar is called upon to bring forth such evidence begins in the Talmud with the word Maany, meaning: according to whose contention will the opinion expressed in the Mishnah be decided?

19\. HAGADAH: Every exegetical inference or elucidation which is brought in the Talmud to clarify any subject, not derived from either a mandatory or prohibitive commandment, is called Hagadah. Therefrom nothing is derived directly save only what the logical conclusion would support. It is necessary here to know that whatever the Talmudic sages confirmed as law, if such law touches the commandments enumerated by Moses in the Torah as he received it from Almighty, must not be added to nor diminished from; but whatever interpretation each and every Talmudic sage gave concerning passages in the Torah as he understood it, or arrived at a conclusion by his own reasoning, may be extended by means of inference, induction, or deduction. All the rest of Hagadic matter are not brought in as support of any law.

20\. HOROAH: Traditional pronouncements made by sages in their respective communities, either during trials or in sermons, on subjects touching any commandment, is called Horoah.

21\. SHETAH: A certain opinion formed by a group of sages who were not sustained by the governing law, but they all, nevertheless, abided by that opinion, is called Shetah; but the opinion of any one of them is never taken as part of the existing law.
