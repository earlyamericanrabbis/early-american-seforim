**III .**

"And they shall not appear before the Lord empty" (Deut. 16.16.)

"Empty-without Zedakah" (Yalkut Shimony, Torah, 857).

It is a beautiful custom in Israel to make offerings for Zedakah before memorial services are conducted. Zedakah offers an escape from death-Zedakah Tazil mi-Movet!

What is Zedakah?

Why is only Zedakah a means of life and escape from death?

And, is it really an escape from death?

Do people who give alms with a full measure, and with both hands open to give, never die?

The Rama (R. Moses Isserlis) attests that the custom of offering certain sums of money before memorial services are held is mandatory. Why before memorial services is not Zedakah in order at all times?

Moreover, why do we as a people bid farewell to our most happy holidays by inviting the souls of our ancestors to be present at the event?

\(1\) Zedakah is not alms, and is not charity. The word is derived from the same root as the word which stands for righteousnes. We must not give because it is a pity, because we have too much, because we must "live and let live." All of these sentiments, while praiseworthy in others, is not to be recommended to Israel. Zedakah is, giving the neighbor, the institution, the Talmud Torah, the Yeshivah, the hospital, the orphanage, the home for aged, the sheltering home for strangers, the aid for immigrants, their rightful share in whatever of God's blessings we are privileged to have. Even the poor who lives on Zedakah, must give Zedakah. He can not escape the commandment of giving at least one half of a shekel annually.

\(2\) There is no meaner person on earth than the one who has and refuses to give. His life is a chain of torture, and his death a pit of hell. He is dead when alive, and doubly dead in his grave. His name rots away even before his body does. Soul he had none. So there is nothing of him that lives either in this world or in the world-to-come. But one who gives Zedakah is quickened himself by reviving others; thus he has a means of life in this world. Besides, he escapes from death; he is ever remembered; his name never dies; he is immortal; thus he inherits a share in-the-world-to-come.

\(3\) It is a certain escape from death. Israel's life, in the final analysis, depends upon his own membership, as does the life of a synagogue. We have witnessed it during the relief drives. American Israel saved millions of lives of their brethren. Zedakah is a certain escape from death, but not only for the recipient; the donor, too, escapes the fate of his brother; he is blessed of Almighty; he can give; his life is safe, secure.

\(4\) People who give to their full ability never die. Moses Monteffiore is not dead. Joseph Nassi is not dead; Donna Gracie Mendosa is not dead; Don Isaac Abrabanel is not dead. Neither is the actual poor man, who shares his little with his fellow, dead. Their deeds live forever, enshrined in the hearts of an afflicted people.

\(5\) Zedekah is in order at all times; but we invite now our ancestors; we ask their souls to come near us. Let them behold that we carry on the work upon earth which they have so beautifully established. It is mandatory, because we show the souls of our ancestors that we, too, have the noble ambition to continue among the living-among the immortal.

\(6\) Our holidays are different than the holidays of all other people. It has been rightly said that the Hebrew language has no actual present tense-past and future, yes; but hardly a present, in its grammatical sense. What is true of the Hebrew language is also true of the Hebrew people. We hardly have a present. We live either in our past or in our future. We have lived through another passover; we lived in our past. The holiday is now being terminated. We did not feel the present of the holiday. But we desire to express in a symbolic manner our hope in the future. Our future is immortality, indestructibility. Can any one tear out from our hearts the love we carry for our ancestors, for our near and dear ones? We invite them their souls to visit us. We remember them; we pray to God to remember them among the immortals. By a language that no human speech can express we tell our own souls that we believe in our future, that we believe that our life upon earth is established until there will be no sun nor moon, until the mountains will disappear and the oceans dry up. This is our hope on earth; and the memorial service for our departed ancestors reminds us of our latter end-of our goal in the Godly make-up of eternity.
