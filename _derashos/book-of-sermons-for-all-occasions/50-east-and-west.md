"These are the generations of the heaven and of the earth when they were created, in the day that the Lord God made from earth and heaven." (Gen. 2.4).

Alexander of Macedonia propounded ten questions to the Elders of the South:

\(1\) " Is it farther from heaven to earth than from east to west?"

"From east to west; as testimony, behold, when the sun is in the east, everybody can look into it, but when it reaches the highest point heavenward no one can look into it."

\(2\) "Were the heavens created first, or the earth?"

"The heavens; for, it is said: In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth."

\(3\) Which was created first, light or darkness?" "This question can not be answered... They feared lest he continues to ask them: What is above, and what is beneath, what was before and what will be after?" (4) "Who should be considered a wise man? " "He who foresees an issue correctly is a wise man." (5) "Who should be considered a strong man?" "He who can subdue his own passion is a strong man." (6) "Who should be considered a rich man?" "He who is content with his share of worldly goods is a rich man." (7) "What should a man do that he may live?" "He who defies death will continue to live." (8) "What should a man do to invite death?" "He who values life merely to live on will be overtaken by death." (9) "How should a man conduct himself in order to be popular?" "He who is at a distance from kings and rulers, will not be envied by his fellow-men, and will, in the end, be popular."

"Said he to them: ' I have a better advice: Let one be near to and court the favor of kings and rulers for his fellow-men, and by being good to them they will be his friends. "

\(10\) "Who among you is the wisest?"

"We are all alike in wisdom; as evidence we point to our answers, which we gave as one." (Tamid, chapter 4, 31b-32a; Yalkut Shimony, Genesis, 4).

This highly philosophical dialogue leaves two questions but partly answered, or not answered at all.

The wise men, as the Talmud comments on their reply to Alexander's question concerning light and darkness, did not fear to give a direct reply concerning heaven and earth, because they thought that he selected that question at random, but they could not think that way when he continued the line of questioning affecting creation. They could have replied that darkness was created first, as it is said: "And the earth was without form and void, and the darkness was upon the face of the deep," and after it is said: " Let there be light"; only their fear of what would come next, in such line of questions and answers, as the Talmudic authors, declare, prevented them from quoting the Torah. But that must bring the seeker of truth to a presumption that Alexander was entirely ignorant of the Biblical text.

The second question left in doubt is the one referring to causes of popularity.

But even some of the other replies invite comment; as, for instance, about the distance from heaven to earth and from east to west, concerning which, the Tanaites differed from the Wise men, by saying that the reason one can not look into the sun when it is in the highest point of heaven is because it is then unobstructed, which is not the case when in the west.

The secret of creation, despite, the well accepted theories of evolution, is still fascinating the simple mind of the child and perplexing the well-developed brain of the philosopher. We know the great services the sun renders to us, and to all life and vegetation; we know of the good results the works of the moon and the stars, and the seas and the mountains produce; we are taught to know the intricacies of the formation and of functions of each; but, let us be honest with ourselves, do we really know it, or do we merely believe the text books which we are instructed from?

The truth is, as the Biblical text indicates, that the Torah teaches us that both, darkness and light were; it merely relates that darkness prevailed amidst a chaos, and, order could not be, save only when light came. Are darkness and light, too, results of evolution? Of course not; both are natural phenomena, regulated by natural laws.

Evidently what Alexander wanted to know was: Which was created first, or, rather, was there ever a time in nature when darkness alone reigned?

This question, as the Wise men understood it, rested upon a system of philosophy evolved from the false mythology of the Greeks and Romans, and, in a measure, upon certain religious beliefs that God himself is engaged in fighting the Devil. Darkness, the capitol of the Devil, was first, according to some mythological doctrines and pagan belief. And, only when God created the Light was the Devil conscious of the presence of another Power. So is the belief of many till this day.

To that question, the Wise men were disinclined to make reply, saying instead, that it can not be answered. Conscious of the power of Alexander, they did not court a theological disputation with him. They rather left it to him to quote the Bible passage concerning darkness and light. He did not do it, evidently not because of his ignorance of the text, but because he understood that his argument could not be sustained, because of the faulty premise it had to presuppose.

Indeed, east is farther from west than from earth to the highest point of heaven. It is so among the nations; and it is so among Israel. The western Jew is further removed from his eastern brother than is the earth from heaven.

The eastern Jew believes that when under the yoke of the nations, nothing should be done, save submit to every cruel law. By inviting every danger, they secure life. Assimilation reaches out its inviting wings only among the Jews who partake of the western life, who meet the oppressor half, or more than half way. The philosophy of life of the Wise Men of the South differs from the philosophy of life of the Greek, the Roman, the Macedonian. Strange, indeed, but true, nevertheless, that the gulf between the Jew of the east and the Jew of the west has not yet been bridged. The aristocracy of ancient Ephraim still clings to some branch of Jewry!

Courting the favor of the powers was a bone of contention throughout the history of Israel, both, in Palestine and in Diaspora. The popularity of Israel's representatives in Rome, in Egypt, and elsewhere did not last very long. The hof-jude, the court-Jew, was not looked upon with favor. The Shetadlon was always a person more feared than either honored or respected. Here again we meet the problem of implanting western ideas upon eastern soil. The Wise Men of the South differed radically from the opinion of Alexander.

From the very nature of things the sages were able to derive great lessons for their fellow-beings. It is much further from east to west than from earth to heaven! The mystery of life upon earth is more difficult to penetrate than the mystery of heaven. No one can look at the sun when it reaches the highest point of heaven. Neither can any one rationalize creation, miracles, spirituality, religion generally, or the the things and beings which are heavenly. No one can look at all these with the naked eye; no one can perceive it with his senses, or understand it with his intellect. Faith solves the problem. We believe that we do reach a point where our brain ceases to function, and we must hand matters over to our heart, to our soul.

But the amazing thing in life is that we can not see things on our own planet, that we can not fathom the mysteries upon earth, that the most intimate neighbor of ours, man, has so many complexities that he seems to be further away from our understanding than heaven. Man, our fellow whom we see so clearly daily and hourly, is the greatest enigma in creation to us. He toils for us, ploughs the ground, seeds it, raises grain, fruit and vegetables, milk, meat and honey; makes our shoes and clothes, our houses and our furnishings; provides every comfort for us; he teaches us, preaches to us, entertains us, governs us, protects us, wars for us, mourns for us, rejoices with us; we mind him, love him, follow him, honor him and hate him! Man becomes a saint and a devil, sometimes the two in one; he uses one instrument on the operating table to relieve distress and disease, and another to slaughter his fellow-beings; he chains his brain to the mysteries of nature and makes her do his bidding, and uses the same blessed forces to destroy that which others constructed; he subdues the beasts and makes the forests safe for babes, and becomes himself a beast of prey; he solves all mysteries, and remains the greatest mystery in creation; he raises himself, oftentimes, higher than heaven itself, and falls deeper than the abode of demons in hell; he makes a heaven upon earth one while, and the next turns hell inside out and offers it as an abode for his next door neighbor; he defies the power of all nature and humbles it to his will; and he himself is never sated with power; he unearths all wealth and proves that it is naught but pieces of carbon, mine ral, and dirt, and he himself is never satisfied with all the treasures he can get; he discovers the low value of his own body, its sugar, its sulphur, its fat, its iron, and spends billions in money and millions in lives to humble one body, if it happens to be that a king!

And, notwithstanding all, man is the most precious creature in all creation. The Torah testifies to it:

"These are the generations of the heaven and of the earth when they were created, in the day that the Lord God made earth and heaven... And there was not a man to till the ground." Both heaven and earth needed man. That is the reason why it is more difficult to unravel the enigma of man upon earth than to reach a solution to the problems of heaven.
