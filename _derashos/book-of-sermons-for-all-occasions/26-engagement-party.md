The people of Israel does not tolerate the practice that a young man and a young woman should meet, and, on the spur of the moment become man and wife. The young man and the young woman, are important, indeed, in a matter of this kind; but the parents and near relatives on both sides must not be lost sight of. Hasty marriages, without giving the couple an opportunity to learn something about one another is not a healthy procedure, (although not forbidden under the Jewish religious code. That is the traditional end of the situation.

But there is a far deeper significance to the ceremony of Te'naim. Te'naim means-conditions. Instead of a companionate marriage, or a contract marriage, or marriage a la mode-and there is nothing new under the sun, for these practices had been resorted to many, many generations before the Jazz-age, before Bolshevism, before the free-love era-the people of Israel inaugurated the system of engagement-Te'naim. Sometimes an engagement took place while both the bride and the groom were still in the kindergarten age. Even such marriages took place, of course not by the actual parties concerned, but between the parents on both sides, who acted as proxys. But there was the power of veto-Mi'un; if the girl, when she got her senses, said: No, it was no, if she actually was serious about it about the "No," I mean. There was such thing as veto-divorce "Get Mi'un. "

Thus, you will see that the people of Israel tried almost every known lawful way to have the boys and the girls married off, and be through -Potur! as the more accurate expression of such event really is.

Such step, that is agreeing upon a marriage, promising marriage, accepting a proposal of marriage, when executed in the presence of a full moon, and the stars, and some flowers forcing their way amidst the trees of a park, is usually not altogether a calculated affair. Even if the minds of the two parties to such agreement do not meet, the moon wins anyhow. The trouble that occasionally follows such engagements, however, is not ended before the man of the moon; it rather ends before a stern-faced judge.

That kind of engagements the people of Israel do not favor. Here, where electricity, and not the moon, furnishes the light; where fathers and mothers and relatives and friends, and dishes and things are together; where the groom sees that there must be something to eat, where the bride sees that there are dishes to wash, here under such prosaic environment, if both the hearts of the young man and the young lady says: "Yes! " We all may say heartily-Mazol Tov!
