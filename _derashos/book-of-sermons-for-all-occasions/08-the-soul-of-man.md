"On the eighth day shall be an holy convocation; it is a day of solemn assembly; ye shall do no servile work therein." (Lev. 23.36.)

This is a day of solemn assembly. It is a separate holiday, which has neither the Sukko nor the Four Kinds to illustrate its lesson, to bring home its teachings, to give expression to itself in a token which the eye can see and the mind perceive.

We make special mention of the day in our prayers, read special chapters of the Torah and of the prophets. Otherwise there is nothing of the nature of a physical reminder of the holy time we observe.

In one instance, however, this day is singled out. After the season of rejoicing, after the Sukkot and the Four Kinds aided us in finding the joy, or a reflection of the joy Israel once experienced when he was master of his own soil, shaper of his own destiny, and framer of his own government, we appear before our Maker in an entirely spiritual character. Even the joy is not because our bins are filled with grain, our vats with wine, our barns with provendor, or our stables with stock. Instead our joy is completely based upon spiritual ground. We rejoice because we have studied the Torah; we are happy because we are about to conclude the last Verses of the Torah, and commence reading it anew. This joy is to be set apart, in a class by itself. No nation has anything like it.

And, amidst the joy of our soul, after we concluded the Sukkot festivity in token of our earthly well-being, we are ordered to have a memorial service for the dead. We are, moreover, holding this service on this day of solemn assembly without having prepared our souls for the occasion, as, for instance, we did on Yom Kippur.

Maimoni says: "The soul of all flesh is the form which God gave unto man, and the high intelligence which is found in the soul of man is the form of the man who is perfectly intelligent." He further cautions the student, saying: "Oftentimes this form is called " soul" and " breath of life", and therefore, it is necessary to mind the process of naming them, to prevent man from error, for each and every name applicable thereto, one must learn from the context thereof."

The "breath of life" is not the "soul of man." A serpent has a breath of life, but not a soul. Instinct, fear, love, hatred, jealousy, revenge, and kindred tendencies do not come from the soul. There is a number of elements which contribute to the life of a person; spiritual, if we may employ this term. The behavior of a person, even his psychological make-up comes to him unconsciously, naturally, without it being out of the ordinary process of natural development. Drawing lines is a purposeful act; but it is not art; when the artist draws lines, makes a sketch, and finishes a painting, then we know the difference between a soul and a mere breath of life.

All the natural acts of a person are varied forms of expression of the several degrees of life-giving agencies within him. But when a person acts only with his intelligence, when his acts are entirely unselfish, not for the sake of self-preservation, glory, wealth, power, or pleasure, then we see the soul in action.

All other life-giving agencies in a person are subject to death; they are inborn, a part of his make-up; they are destroyed when life ceases; a pistol-shot, or disease, or old age makes an end to all these. But the soul is a distinct heavenly being, with an independent existence, a direct gift of God. That can never be destroyed by natural causes; it is not subject to the diseases common on this planet, for its origin is not from here; it is not subject to death or dissolution, for it is part of eternity, and has no connection whatever with the earthly elements.

The great mercy of God, in giving a soul to man, is hidden beneath the manner in which He gave it to him; man must develop it, must feel and know its power before he actually can realize its presence within his make-up. We speak of an evil-minded person as a being without a soul; he has a soul, but he never uses it, never gives it the liberty of self-expression. A tyrant could be merciful; he merely choked every atom of mercy within his bosom.

The soul has the measure of freedom of self-expression which man permits it to enjoy. If all life-giving agencies within a man's make-up are occupied in natural matters of any character whatever, the soul is then isolated, alone, as if it had left its abode within man's being.

Man ever speaks of things as being "his". Ask a man:

Whose is this hand?

Mine.

Whose is this head?

Mine.

Whose is this heart?

Mine.

Whose is this property?

Mine.

This wife and children?

Mine.

Who is that first person, singular, of whom man speaks with such certainty as being the owner of his hand, head, heart, property, wife and children? Who is the master of it all?

Unconsciously man tells of that which governs, or should govern his life, and of that which survives him of his soul!

Thus, lest we pass on from holy to profane time without entertaining our soul with its kind of food, with the spiritual nourishment so essential to its being, we appear to-day in solemn assembly, and give our souls full charge of the occasion. We give it full sway to rejoice because of the conclusion and commencement of the reading of the Torah. And, in order to manifest our unselfish devotion to all that is soulful, to everything which is eternal, we hold memorial services for the dead, or rather for the living souls in heaven. We invite them here to come and observe and be testimony for us before the Throne of God from beneath of which they were hewen, that we are celebrating this day as an holy convocation solely for the sake of our souls.
