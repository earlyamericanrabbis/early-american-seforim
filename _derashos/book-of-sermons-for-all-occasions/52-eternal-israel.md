B. Benedict Glazer, M. A.

We somehow or other obtain the idea that Judaism does not function as it should; that it consists of a study which is not very important in so far as it is related to our every day life. Now, Judaism is not a phenomenon which happened in the past and which has ceased to have significance for our modern life. On the contrary it is a force which can be made to operate today as really and as effectively as it did in the past. And the reason for this is very clear. Judaism has always concerned itself with the practical problems of every day life. If you will look through the Bible you will see that you are coming in contact with a program for human behavior on a highly ethical and spiritual but at the same time practical plane. The basic laws of this program dealt with problems and situations which we meet with constantly in our own day. For example, do the 613 com mandments deal with abstractions beyond the experiences of men? Not at all. They have been given to govern the conduct of man in his daily life. Moreover, when new situations developed as the centuries rolled by and the Jews found themselves in different countries and under the rule of various nations, the laws of Judaism were interpreted or embellished in such a fashion as to allow its followers to observe them in the face of changing environments. This enabled Judaism to survive. We know that an organism will perish unless it can adjust itself to changing environments. For example, if a plant is indigenous to the climate and soil of America, and is transplanted to Africa, unless that plant can adjust itself to the climatic conditions of Africa the plant will perish, will it not? So it is also with systems of belief unless you can adjust them so that they will meet with new situations and new conditions of life, they will disappear into oblivion. Because Judaism could adjust itself to life anywhere in the world, it is still a power in our lives today and functions in the modern world as a living, throbbing reality.

With the foregoing facts in mind, I have chosen the subject of Judaism and the Philosophy of Pessimism because it involves one of the outstanding and pressing problems of our own time. Now Judaism can be made to present an attitude on any question that involves human welfare and human progress today. For example, let us examine for a moment the problem of peace. We have been involved in the past in great wars which have threatened to destroy society. Today we have arbitration committees meeting all over the world trying to devise ways and means of ending war. All sorts of pacts have been formed and plans evolved to outlaw war, yet we have no better program of peace than the one presented by the Jewish Prophets thousands of years ago. What they said is as true and real today as it was in their own day. Their dreams, their hopes and their ambitions for humanity have not been fulfilled and mankind with great profit to itself may still go back to them for counsel and inspiration.

Now let us turn our complete attention to the subject of Judaism and the Philosophy of Pessimism. Many of you probably have read or heard of a certain philosophy which regards this world as a machine made up of an infinite number of parts. This machine is blind, purposeless, and meaningless and you and I who are kegs within the wheels of this machine live a purposeless, meaningless, existence, and the world are composed of the same kind of atoms of matter in different combinations and in different degrees of complexity. Now this particular view came to be held by some thinkers very early in the history of thought. It is one particular way of looking at life. The Greek philosopher, Leuccipus, was one of the first to advance this theory about the nature of man and the world. He was followed by a man named Democritus, one of our earliest systematic philosophers and the first man to develop the atomic theory. This type of philosophy is called materialism or mechanism and finds no place in the world for spirit or soul. It has developed in a more or less systematic way since the time of Democritus and reached the climax of its expression in the eighteenth century just prior to the French Revolution. At that time it was the purpose of many to present these theories of materialism in such a way that they could lay the foundation for the overthrow of the ruling class of France whose power was dependent on the theory of Divine right of kings, of clergy, and of property. The materialists believed in no such Divine right; they did not believe, for example, that the clergy received their right to leadership from powers beyond themselves, but were human beings like themselves. All three concepts of Divine right mentioned were to be destroyed by the ideas of materialism. Right before the French Revolution, therefore, we have the most outspoken type of materialism. After the French Revolution there was a unfavorable reaction to this kind of thinking but in the early part of the nineteenth century with the development of Darwinism, and the Industrial Revolution, impetus was again given to the theories of materialism. Now, in our own day, these theories are worked out on a more elaborate scale than ever. For we are living in a machine age. Everything is done through machines. Less and less work is being done by hand; everything seems to move on wheels at a tremendous rate of speed and everything appears to be shaped in form of iron and steel. It is very easy to see then how it is possible for a materialistic interpretation of life to gain sway.

All these evidences of materialism and mechanism have contributed to a pessimistic view of life. Because if you believe that you come from chaos and that life is just a bridge between emptiness and emptiness, that it has no purpose, that man is just a collection of atoms thrown accidentally together in the world, then hope and courage and sanity leave you to become the victim of overwhelming despair. Materialism is thus the philosophical basis of pessimism.

The second basis of pessimism is derived from the practical experiences of life. There are many people in the world who have never read a book on philosophy and who know nothing about materialism. Yet they say that life is not worth living. They feel they have had nothing but trouble in their lives and that the little amount of happiness which they obtain is completely overbalanced by the pain and sorrow of existence and that life, therefore, is not worth living.

The German philosopher, Schopenhauer wrote many essays describing the suffering of human beings and to prove thereby that life offers new compensations for living. According to him, when one meets a friend on the street, the greeting should be " Hello, fellow-sufferer"!

Judaism has a reply to both of these foundations of pessimism. In the first place the philosophy of the Bible and of the literature which came after the Bible is in direct contrast to materialism, the theoretical basis of pessimism. If you read the Bible very carefully, you must come to the conclusion that it certainly does not in any way encourage a materialistic interpretation of life. This is said with the realization that the Bible does not advance a completely idealistic interpretation of life which is divorced from the practical. It does not deal with spiritual and supernatural matters alone as Plato, the Greek philosopher, did. Plato said all that is true exists in the heavens in the form of "ideal patterns," and what we see on earth are only copies of these ideal patterns and are not real. As I have indicated, Judaism does not present such a belief. Judaism represents a "practical idealism," in contrast to materialism and is a fortunate combination of the material and the spiritual. It does not neglect either one. For example, the Jewish conception of God is, of course, idealistic, Naturally, our law-givers and prophets have advanced certain definitions concerning the nature of God. They say, for example, God is holy, God is just, God is merciful, God is righteous, etc. These are man's conceptions of the Deity. All are idealistic. But all definitions are made to operate in a practical way in the life of man, God says: "Ye shall be holy for I am holy." Human beings who worship God are therefore commanded to be holy also. If God is merciful, if God is righteous, then we must try to be merciful, righteous people. In this way we put into practical application the idealism which has been developed by our religion.

Following this method of procedure, Judaism has developed laws and codes which govern the life of man on the foundation of its basic idealism. Therefore, being a practical idealism, it is com pletely opposed to materialism. We can thus see how impossible it is for one to say that Judaism in any way presents a philosophical or a theoretical basis for pessimism, and so if an impartial observer were to ask me "Will you, as a representative of the Jewish people, tell me what Judaism has to say today on the matter of pessimism?" I would answer, "Judaism cannot countenance such a conception. Judaism represents the very antithesis of pessimism. It is a religion of hope because its entire development is an outgrowth of a practical idealistic interpretation of life."

Now what does Judaism say about the everyday experiences of life which seem to support a pessimistic belief? When we read history, we find that all people who should have every reason to feel discouraged, to feel that life is not worth living, it is the Jewish people. Evidence of suffering and persecution is given all through the history of our ancestors. Now I can just imagine some impartial person coming from another planet upon earth and reading the volumes of Jewish history; and after he is through a man would come and say to him: "You have never seen a Jew, you have never talked to a Jew,-tell me what you think, in the light of what you read, what should his attitude toward life be?" His answer would doubtless be: "A Jew must be a bitter, hopeless, very dead sort of individual. He must be, above all people, an individual who has a right to say that his life has not been worth the price he has had to pay for the privilege of living. " But our impartial visitor will be wrong in his conclusion because no people has exemplified more completely the spirit of hope, which is the death of pessimism, than the Jewish people. All through their history they have never given up hope. They retained it in the face of whatever life brought them. No matter what occurred, they said: "Next year it will be better; next year we will be taken care of; next year God will improve our lot." They put their trust in God and that expresses the historical attitude of the Jew.

Now let us look at the Jews today. We find Jews disagreeing on everything except on the matter of hope; they are the most optimistic people under the sun. No other people is engaged in more impractical pursuits than the Jewish people, pursuits which represent the most intense degree of hope. Even Jews who accept the philosophical basis of pessimism do not employ these ideas to govern their lives. From the Jewish communist, who has no religion whatsoever, to the most religious type of Zionist runs the connecting chain of soaring optimism. Jewish life all over the world is still saturated with high hope for the future, no matter how dark or dreary the present may be. To summarize, Judaism is a practical idealism entirely opposed to the materialistic basis of pessimism. It is as expressed in the life and the literature of the Jew, a most hopeful philosophy of existence.
