"Get thee up into the high mountain;

O thou that tellest good tidings to Jerusalem,

Lift up thy voice with strength;

Lift it, be not afraid;

Say unto the cities of Judah:

'Behold your God."

This Sabbath is called-Sabbath Nahamu-" Comfort ye," because the first word of the prophetical reading of the book of Isaiah which we read this morning begins with the word: Nahamu.

A people which can mourn must also be able to rejoice, to be happy. Eyes which weep, must also smile. One who can survive adversity, should be able to enjoy prosperity. One who can cross the desert with bleeding feet, a thirsty throat and a heavy-laden burden with a taskmaster's yoke, should be able to enjoy travel over a paved road, in a powerful automobile, driven by his paid chauffeur. But the main point is that he should be able to enjoy it while dreaming about it at the very time he suffers tears of adversity, or a forced march across the desert.

In our present situation as a people we have no more reason to feel the joy of our future on Sabbath Nahamu than we had reason to suffer the agonies of our past on Sabbath Hazon. But we, nevertheless, do both.

Why?

Merely because we are different than other people; we think different, live different, hope and dream different. We lived through the Hurban-destruction of our national existence in Palestine during the Three Weeks, and we are now ushering in Seven Weeks of Comfort-Shibah de'Nehamata. And, of course, we are going to feel the happiness of comfort.

Are we everlasting actors?

No; but we are sons of prophets.

In like manner as we do, our ancestors, who were real prophets, did. They suffered voluntarily, to experience the suffering which awaited their people, as an example to their people, giving them a warning of impending danger. If that is acting, then we are actors.

We are not now, nor were we at any time during our long history, eager to create new pleasures; our greatest source of pleasure is either to repeat in our imagination the happy times we had in the past, or draw a picture of the happy times of our future. It may be acting; but it is to us realism of the first rank. It actually is our food and drink.

We can not suppress this characteristic; we must proclaim it, regardless of what other people say or think about us.

We feel along with the one who makes us weep; Jerusalem is indented in our make-up. But our constant companion is the one who makes us smile even while the tears are yet on our cheeks, in our eyes. We love our Isaiah. Therefore-

Get thee up into the high mountain;

O thou that tellest good tidings to Jerusalem.
