Friends:-

The custom of installing a Rabbi is a new one. At least in the form we practice this custom in American and in Western Europe. But I believe that this custom is a bit of an improvement upon tradition.

In Europe, the whole population, or most of them, went out to greet the incoming Rabbi of the community. Before the town was reached the Rabbi was welcomed; his family was brought to their new home, and the Rabbi himself was taken to the synagogue, and there, in the presence of as many of the townspeople as could crowd themselves in the edifice, was handed his Rabbinical commission-Ketab Rabonut. The Kiddush, on the Sabbath following, was not given by the community, but at the Rabbi's house. The Rebetzin, aided, of course, by the leading ladies of the town, prepared the Kiddush, and the selected few knew by virtue of their prerogatives that they are wanted there, and they went there.

That ended it all, at least during the lifetime of the Rabbi, or when a larger community came during some dark night and stole him, and his books away; for his love for his people was so strong that he would not, otherwise, part with them, even though his living consisted of but the barest necessities.

Here the thing is different entirely.

We invite the Rabbi and his friends and associates to a banquet, after some speech-making at the synagogue. The Rabbi knows that he is our guest, and not we his geusts. We show his wife that we expect our Rabbi to partake of the best food, and take life easy. We also feel certain that the Rabbi will not have to leave us because of our inability to support him materially.

Now for the serious aspect of our conduct. We are busy man and woman. We have not the ability or time to cope with a situation such as confronts us in this age. The Rabbi is more than he ever was in Jewish history. He must create both the field, and the means to plant that field with whole-1 some and selected seed. His labor on virgin soil is difficult indeed. We have selected a great Rabbi, a real leader, a preacher of renown, and a scholar who knows what to teach and how to teach. He finds no tradition of a chain of predecessors, or a paved road upon which to travel. He has to build all that himself. His task is difficult. It is for this reason that his material necessities must be provided in the fullest measure so that he will have no extra worry on his head.

A Rabbi in Israel today is more than a spiritual head of a congregation. He must be the real ambassador of God to his people. In addition to the care he has to give to the old and to the young he also has to participate in every national movement for the advancement of Judaism and the Jewish people. He must be first and foremost in every local, city-wide, state-wide and nation-wide undertaking, whether it be of a religious, educational, sociological or philanthropic character. He is the spokesman of his community. Upon his judgment we make our proportional contribution to every worthy cause. Besides this, he is a Rabbi, a scholar, and he can not neglect his studies. Of all the work that a Rabbi does now, no Rabbi in the history of Israel ever dared even dream of. With changed conditions, every phase of our lives changes accordingly, and due to all of these changes the spiritual leaders must carry a burden undreamt of by our ancestors.

But, after all, the Rabbi is never alone in his work. Here are a number of his friends and associates, ready to give him their advice and aid in whatever undertaking he may be wanting it; and here are the men and women of our community ready to come to the aid of the Rabbi in matters which they may help.

Such installation is now a necessity. It furnishes a perspective of the entire religious field both, to the Rabbi and to his people.
