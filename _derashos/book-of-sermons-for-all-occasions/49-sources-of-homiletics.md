The original meaning of the Hebrew word Midrash was "to investigate", "to search", "to inquire, " that is the sense in which this word is employed in the Torah, (example, Leviticus, 10. 16). A later meaning of the word was "exposition", and "exegesis", (example II Chronicles, 13.22-19.27). A still later meaning of this word seems to combine its original as well as its secondary meaning. In the Talmud, Sanhedrin, 34b, the word Midrash is employed in this combined meaning. There it is compared "to a hammer which awakens slumbering sparks in rocks," so Midrash awakens inspiring meanings in the words of the Torah. Midrash now is universally used in contrast to the word Peshot. Peshot means the ordinary meaning of words. Midrash brings out from the self-same words, altogether a new meaning. The first to make that distinction were Rab Kahana (Shabbath, 63a), and Rab (Yebamot 24a). Today the meaning of Midrash, or rather "Drush," is homiletics, sermons, as well as exposition, and exegesis. Maimonides believes that Midrash is in offshot from Halakah, and Nach-monides believes just the reverse, that Halakah sprank forth from Midrash. The Midrash had three periods of development: 1. Sophrim; 2. Tannaim; 3. Amoraim.

1\. One source, however, Shekalim, 6.8, gives evidence that there was a still earlier Midrashic period, as it speaks of the Midrash of Ye-hoyada Kohen Godol, who, of course, was before the period of the Sophrim. That Midrash was a current branch of Jewish learning during the time of the Sophrim, appears in Kiddushin, 3la, and Yerusalmi Shekalim, 48c. That it was so is evidenced in Nehemaiah, 8.8, wherein it is stated: "So they read in the Book, in the law of God, distinctly and made them to understand." Consequently, Midrash was a form of exposition, or exegesis on the Torah.

It certainly is indicative that the Sophrim were much more than mere translaters. Traces of the Midrash on the Torah employed by the Sophrim are found in Negaim, 12.5; Sotah, 8.1; and Ma'asar Sheni, 5.7. Accordingly, such Midrashic works as the Sifra on the Sidrah Emor, 9.3, and of Shemini, 5.8, and Behar, 4.4, and Mahilta Mishpatim, 3, originated from the Sophrim.

2\. Tannaim. The Midrashic literature of that period often makes mention of the "Zekenim Harishonim" (First Elders) whose date cannot be definitely determined; but three Midrashim of the Tannaitic era have been preserved, namely: Sifra Vayikra, 12.1; Mezora, 11.12; Mehilta Amalek, 2. Likewise a few Midrashim by Judah, son of Tabbai, and Simon, son of Shetah, both of whom lived in the first century, B. C. E. So it is indicated in Mehilta, Mishpatim, 20; Tosefta, San-hebrin, 8.b; Makot, 5.b; and Yerusalmi Sanhedrin, 22.b.

The opposition of the Sadducees, who rejected the oral law, and who were attacked by Tabbai and Shetah, naturally led to an attempt to base this form of exposition of the law on the Torah, thus encouraging and increasing Midrashic exegesis. The well-known interpretation of the passage "An eye for an eye",-the sentence being a fine instead of an eye-(Exodus, 21.24), contradicting the view of the Sadducees who wishes to apply the law literally, gives evidence of a free and profound conception of the text of the Torah even at that early date.

In the succeeding period after Tabbai and Shetah, Shemaiah and Abtalion are mentioned as "Darshanim Gebolim," which means "Great Ex ponents" of the Midrashic form of interpreting the Torah (Pesahim, 70; and Mehilta Beshallah, 3). Of the period of Hillel and Shammai only a few Midrashim, all simple in character, have been preserved. (Sifra, Shemini, 9.5; Negaim, 9.16; Yeru-salmi Pesahim, 33a; Tosefta Erubin, 6.7; Shabbath, 19a; Kiddushin, 43a). But this presupposes that there was much more of the Midrashic literature of that period, but that most of it, unfortunately, was lost.

3\. The Midrash of the Amoraim in Babylon, is of more simple method than that of the Palestinian Tannaim. Of the Midrashim of that period, the following have been preserved almost intact:

\(1\) Mehilta to Exodus, which originated in Rabbi Ishmael's school, and which begins with Exodus, chapter 12, and is the first legal section in the Book of Exodus. The exegesis is continued with the omission of the few verses down to chapter 23.19.

\(2\) The Sifra to Leviticus; originated in the school of Rabbi Akiba, with additions belonging in part to the school of Rabbi Ishmael, was finally edited by Rabbi Hiyya. The Sifra contains a small proportion of Midrashic matter and is mainly legal in character.

\(3\) Sifre to Numbers: is in its main part, a Midrash of the school of Rabbi Ishmael, like the Mehilta.

\(4\) Sifre to Deuteronomy: is part of the school of Rabbi Akiba, and part of the school of Rabbi Ishmael. The purely Hagadic Midrashim of the earliest periods are contained in the Midrash Rabba.

The Hagadic Midrashim are divided and subdivided as follows:

A.

1\. Mehilta; 2. Sifra; 3. Sifre to Numbers; 4. Sifre to Deuteronomy.

B.

I-Purely Hagadic Midrashim:

1\. Bereshit Rabba; 2. Echah Rabbati.

II-Homiletic Midrashim:

1\. Pesikta; 2. Vayikra Rabbah; 3. Tanhuma Yelammedenu; 4. Pesikta Rabbati; 5. Debarim Rabbah; 6. Bomidbar Rabbah; 7. Shemot Rabba; 8. Agadat Bereshit; 9. Hashkem.

III\. The Exegetical Midrash to Canticles, Ruth, Ecclesiastes, and Esther:

1\. Shir ha-Shirim Rabba; 2. Midrash Ruth; 3. Midrash Koheleth; 4. Midrash Megillet Esther.

IV-Exegetical Midrashim not dealing with the Pentateuch:

1\. Midrash Samuel; 2. Midrash Mishle; 3. Midrash Tehillim; 4. Midrash Yeshayah; 5. Midrash Yonah; 6. Midrash Iyyob.

V-Special Hagadic works:

1\. Pirke D'Rabbi Eliezer; 2. Seder Eliyahu.

VI-1. Yalkut Shimoni; 2. Yalkut ha-Machir; 3. Midrash ha-Godol.

VII- 1. Midrash Abkir; 2. Midrash Al Yeth-allel; 3. Midrash Aseret ha-Dibrot; 4. Dibre ha-Yamim shel Mosheh; 5. Midrash Eleh Ezkerah; 6. Midrash Eser Galiyyot; 7. Midrash Esfah; 8. Midrash Hallel; 9. Midrash Lechu Nerannena; 10. Midrash Ma'aseh Torah; 11. Midrash Petirat Aharon; 12. Midrash Petirat Mosheh; 13. Midrash Ta'ame Chasorot V'yetorot; 4. Midrash Tad-she, also called Baraita de Rabbi Pinehas, son of Ya'ir; 15. Midrash Temurot. This Midrsah consists only of three chapters. It develops the view that God, in His wisdom and might, has created all things on earth as contrasted pairs which mutually supplement each other. Life is known only as opposed to death, and death as opposed to life; and, in like manner, if all were foolish, or wise, or rich, or poor, it would not be known that they were foolish, or wise, or rich, or poor. Therefore God created men and women, beauty and deformity, fire and water, iron and wood, light and darkness, heat and cold, food and famine, drink and thirst, walking and lameness, sight and blindness, hearing and deafness, sea and land, speech and dumbness, activity and repose, pain and pleasure, joy and sorrow, health and sickness, and the like; 16. Midrash Vayechulu; 7. Midrash Vayes-sa'u; 18. Midrash Vayosha.
