"And ye shall take you on the first day the fruit of goodly trees, branches of palm trees, and the boughs of thick trees, and the willows of the brook. " (Levi, 23.42.)

The Four Kinds consist, as they were identified throughout the ages, of the Etrog (fruit of the goodly trees); the Lulab (branches of the palm-trees); Hadassim (myrtle boughs of the thick trees); and Arabot (willows of the brook.)

The Etrog, if not torn off by hand, continues to hang on to the tree all the year around, defying the wind and other forces of the weather; it is called Hadar, firstly, because the tree upon which it grows is a beautiful plant, and, secondly, because its connection to the tree continues for such long period.

The Lulab grows upon a palm-tree, only in tropical, or semi-tropical climates; its shadow is refreshing to many weary travelers; the least wind causes a vibration which seems to whisper a prayer to its Maker not to be disturbed from its tranquility.

The Hadas grows upon a thick-tree; leaves, leaves and leaves; layers of them on every branch; it seems to fear exposure; its shyness is so conspicuous that hardly a part of its trunk remains uncovered; moreover, the leaves seem to protect one another.

The willows of the brook grow in any valley where there is water near; they require moisture continuously; they thrive under the shadow of dew, although being nursed constantly by water; but they fear the frost, their greatest enemy; for the minute frost strikes the branches, they remain naked, without leaves; the leaves freeze and fall off, changed in color, almost black.

And these Four Kinds are the emblems of the Feast of Ingathering (Hag ha-Asiph), which must be bound together as if they all belonged to one and the same family of plants; taken in both hands, fruit in the left, plants in the right; bring them together, and bless God for having commanded us to observe this precept.

Says Rabbi Simeon ben Lakish: "This people is likened to a vine; the branches are its citizenry; the clusters, its Scholars; the leaves, its mass of toilers; the dried, forked twigs, its idlers." (Hullin, 92b.)

The Etrog, Lulab, Hadas and Arabah, by borrowing the fine simile of Rabbi Simeon ben Lakish, might also be joined together in a similar bond. The Lulab typifies Israel's citizenry; the Etrog, his scholars; the Hadas, his toilers; the Arabah, his idlers, who love to absorb, to spunge, who fall away from their branches the moment their feet get a bit cold.

Even so may the entire human race be divided into four classes; the citizenry, the scholar, the toiler, the trouble-maker.

The races of mankind, too, differ in four fundamentals; color, speech, manners, and belief.

Thousands of years of progress, culture, education and government failed to unite these four kinds. The man of another class is still suffering the burden of prejudice; the race of a different religion is still suffering the burden of persecution; the nation of a different language, is still misunderstood; the group of a different mannerism is still mocked at.

Even in Israel, within his own ranks, there exists a gulf between the different elements; the scholar is as yet not fully emancipated; he is still "patronized" by the citizen; the citizen is still looked upon with distrust by the toiler; and, who can stand the chronic loafer, the first to run away, the first to protest, and the first to surrender?

But all are, or can be made to be, useful for the growth, fertility, and endurance of the vine. Even the loafer, or, to give the name ben Lakish gave him, "Kenoknot," serves a purpose, and can be employed in a more beneficial capacity, both for himself and for society at large.

The remedy is to bind them together, to unite them. When the " willow of the brook" will smell the sweet aroma of the Etrog, it will improve in worth.

Man is, and must be, what his environment makes him, unless he be irrational.

And, as we all face the same end, the identical temporariness, as the festival we now celebrate so plainly indicates, it might be best for all concerned, for the society of Israel as well as for the society of all mankind, to bind the Four Kinds together, and in harmony pronounce a benediction to God, the Father of all.
