Fast and Feast of the Chevrah Kadisha on the 15th of Kislev.

"Abraham buried his wife in the cave of the field of Machpelah" (Gen. 23.19.)

Thus Abraham ordered that the dead shall be disposed of by giving repose to the remains in the earth. Such became the Hebrew form of inhumation at the very inception of the History of Israel.

During the Palestinian era, both at the time of the First and the Second Temples, the rites of burial varied according to the station of life every individual enjoyed, or according to his means, or according to the provisions made in his last will and testament. Gradually abuse in burial became intolerable to the living; the rich made a display which was the envy of their neighbors, and the poor were treated but little better than the beasts of the field.

Then, Rabban Gamliel II, when he assumed the presidency of the Synhedrion, ordered that all the dead, rich and poor alike, shall be buried in plain linen. This did away with the luxurious and extravagant funerals of the rich, and with the miserable forms of burial of the poor..

The existence of a Hebrah Kadishah (Sacred Society) in Jewish communities is very old. Mention is made of such society in the Talmud (Moed Katan, 27).

In the more modern times, during the 18th century, mention is made of the Vienna society, which instituted the 7th day of Adar as a fast day to be observed annually by its members. At the close of the 18th century (1793), the Kiev society adopted the 15th day of Kislev as the fast and feast day of the Hebrah Kadishah, which is now held annually by most burial societies in Europe and in America.

The best adopted name for such society is: Hesed Shel Emet (True Loving-kindness.)

This organization in Israel is composed of the most unselfish group of men and women any nation can show. It is the pride of Judaism, to be able to boast of such nobility of character. The poor and the rich receive the same consideration at the hands of the members of this society. At a time when the immediate family is broken-hearted this society enters with heart and soul, first to look upon death as a fact, and, second, to look after the work which is the duty of the surviving members of the family.

Such society is really deserving of both names, Sacred Society, and, True Loving-kindness Society. It does in sacredness a true work of lovingkindness without the least expectation of earthly reward.

The fast day is observed to implore Almighty for the living, to stay the hand of the Angel of Death.

The feast at night is partaken of by the members as a token of joy and not mourning, of life and not death, of health and not disease, of plenty and not famine.
