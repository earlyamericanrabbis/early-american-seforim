"For on this day shall atonement be made for you, to cleanse you; from all your sins shall ye be clean before the Lord." (Lev. 16.30).

Such sin, which no one recognizes as sin, save only God, is forgiven on the Day of Atonement." (Ke'ritot, 26; Yalkut Shimony, Torah, 578.)

On this night we annul vows, oaths and promises; and remarkable, indeed, the most solemn night in our calendar year was named Kol-Nidre night; to emphasize that Kol Nidre is the feature of all the soul-inspiring prayers we pour out to our Maker. Kol Nidre is neither a Psalm, hymn, supplication, nor a prayer. It is a simple declaration, saying: "Be they all (vows, oaths, promises) absolved, cancelled, null and void, without power or binding force."

On its face it is the strangest prayer in all of our liturgy. Opposition to its introduction in our liturgy was not limited to fear only, that is, the fear "lest the rest of people will say that we are, as a people, because of this dispensation, irresponsible, unreliable." During the Gaonic period, it invoked controversy. Attacks were directed against it by some religious forces, outside of our ranks, on the ground that such dispensation free every Jew from whatever oath or obligation he might make or enter into with a non-Jew. The melody, however, survived in all synagogues and temples; but the prayer itself was expurgated from many prayer books of the west-European and American Jews.

Its origin can not be traced to a particular period in the History of Israel since the dispersion. It is evident, however, that hardly a generation of scholars past by without taking notice of this strange prayer on the most solemn night in the Jewish calendar year.

It is an established fact in Judaism that no Jewish authority has the power to give dispensation of any law in Judaism, and annul it permanently. Such is the acid test of the true prophet. He may, as Elijah did, standing upon Mt. Carmel and offering sacrifices without the Temple-court, temporary, dispense with a law, for the sake of sanctifying the Name of God. But if a prophet advocates the abrogation of even a single law permanently, he is a false prophet.

No branch of the commandments of the Torah was so jealously guarded as that branch which deals with vows, oaths, or promises. In both Babylonian schools, Sora and Pumbeditha, and by some of the Gaonim the study concerning these subjects, was discouraged, and its public instruction forbidden.

But the masses of the people, the greatest majority of the Rabbis, and the main wing of Jewry recognized in this prayer-if prayer it must be called something which contains soul-stirring material, something which brings about the sacredness of the day in its full measure, something which elevates the human mind and fills the human heart with a spirituality no other prayer can accomplish.

What is it?

The entire basis of religion rests upon metaphysics. It can not be rationalized. There is no rationalized religion in existence. But there is a religion for rational beings, and a religion for fanatics; a religion of pure belief, and a religion of superstition.

Every force in nature desires and achieves self-expression of a sort. The blade of grass breaks through a stone in order to get to the sun; the pyoplania will force its way into that part of the body where its virulence will be most effective; the hyena's laughter drives its intended victim to its place of concealment, where it is most conveniently found and consumed; the web of the spider is faster than the wing of the fly.

So it is with man, with groups, with nations, with races, with the entire human family. They all find self-expression in the form of their individual characteristics. In fact they are so classified because of their particular forms of self-ex-ression.

The soul, too, has its self-expression. We do not notice it, because it is not for the physical eye to view it. It comes spontaneous; it is in a people's psychology; it is in the life of the individual.

To sum up the greatest spiritual achievement of Israel does not mean to analyze his physical ability to survive. That is a phenomenon which has to do with his ability of self-control. But in his eternal life, when an analysis of his different characteristics is made, when we set him up on spiritual pedestal, we discover the evidence of the metaphysical round about us. Israel gave his word. He said upon Sinai: "All that the Lord hath spoken we will do and hearken. " That was a promise. Can any one discover a force in nature which would, or could, make Israel break that promise? Can any one find a law to offer a dispensation to Israel, to free him from that promise? It is the outstanding example of fidelity in all history. Religions have changed; peoples became extinct trying to force religion upon others, but Israel merely chronicled the facts and marched onward. Every new instrument of torture, diabolically invented by creatures of hell, was visited upon Israel in order to force him to break that promise; the instruments are but relics of barbaric ages, but Israel is still faithful. Every tyrant made laws directed at that promise; both tyrant and laws are no more, but Israel is here as true as when he stood beneath Sinai!

So are all vows, oaths and promises taken, made, or given to another, sacred to all Israel. The height of ethical conduct is the keeping of one's word. When a pledge, or treaty, of agreement, or covenant becomes a mere scrap of paper, life upon earth becomes unsafe, and civilization extinct.

A vow, a promise, an oath, is a covenant made by the soul. When the oath is given, the vow taken, or the promise made to another, as a means of an obligation to be carried out, or a service to be rendered, or a contract to be fulfilled no power on earth, or in heaven, can annul it. It affects another person, and only that person can, of his own free will and accord, annul it. The Day of Atonement does not cleanse one from the transgressions perpetrated against a fellow-being, not unless that fellow-being will forgive it. Almighty God Himself never took that prerogative to offer dispensation in cases of vows, oaths or promises taken, given, or made to a fellow-being. Not, unless that fellow-being, after pardon was asked of him, and satisfaction given, refuses to forgive, then God Himself grants forgiveness.

But there are vows, oaths, and promises made, given, and taken upon one's ownself. It is binding upon his soul. But the soul never had a say in the matter if it be done in haste, under duress, or thoughtlessly. As long as the soul does not assert itself, and much is done in a week-day manner, without thinking twice, without reflection; for human action is purposeful to the extent that it may satisfy a passing irritation, or momentary fancy. Such can not be said to exist when one acts toward a fellow-being. Then, the action is purposeful in every detail, unless treachery was contemplated, which, surely, can not be condoned.

Now, we are assembled to usher in the Day of Atonement, to afflict our souls, or, rather, to afflict our bodies, so that our souls may assert themselves and gain self-expression. Before the fast of the day touches us, before we even realize what direction to follow, which path to adopt for our future lives, comes this prayer of Kol-Nidre-the self-expression of our soul, which establishes direct communication with God! But words may create a gulf between ourselves and our own souls. To prevent it, to give the soul full power for its self-expression, to permit the spiritual within us to take precedence over everything else, we declare our future vows, oaths, promises, if made, given, or taken, upon ourselves to do or not to do certain things for our own sake, to be null and void. Wisely have the sages said: "Such sin, which no one but God recognizes as sin, is forgiven on the Day of Atonement. "
