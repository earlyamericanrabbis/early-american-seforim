The Torah has two different forms of Divine Law, namely: Natural and Revealed. The seven Noachian Laws, namely: 1. Belief in God. 2. Not to practice idolatry. 3. Not to commit murder. 4. Not to commit incest. 5. Not to eat meat cut from a live animal. 6. Not to rob. 7. Establishment of courts of justice. cover all elements of Law, viz.: Private, Public, Natural, and Revealed. Now, we come to the two elementary questions which must be answered before we can advance to the serious study of the Torah. Who was the author of the Torah? Can the source and origin of the Torah be traced? The Torah, as it was handed down to us traditionally from generation to generation, was the direct charge of Almighty God to Moses. Almighty God spoke and dictated to Moses and he wrote down every law contained in the Torah. The whole Torah was given to Moses upon Sinai. The language of the Torah, because God spoke to man, and because the Torah is intended as a guide to regulate the action of men, is the language of men, so that man may understand it.

The origin of the Torah is a divine work covering every human activity, and every possible human expression of this life; its very source emanated from Almighty God, and it never was the work of man.

Now, it is not sufficient that we hear some one telling us that it is so, or that we read in books that some one is maintaining that it is so. The term Torah is herein limited to the five books of Moses, namely: Genesis, fifty chapters; Exodus, forty chapters; Leviticus, twenty-seven chapters; Numbers, thirty-six chapters; Deuteronomy, thirty-four chapters.

The books of the Prophets are called Nebiyim, and the Scriptures are called Kethubim. The Torah is the Pentateuch-the five books of Moses, and a general outline of the contents of the five books of Moses is essential to the subject.

First, there is a chronological record of twenty generations which follows the story of creation. These generations disappear, and Abraham becomes the outstanding figure. Here it is necessary, in order to understand what these twenty generations mean to Jewish history, and in order to present a clear outline and a good view of the construction of the Torah, to list the names of the leading figures of these generations. It is part of Jewish history in addition to being part of the Torah, because by knowing the process of development of the people of Israel prior to the Revelation on Sinai, many conflicting theories are avoided. The ten generations between Adam and Noah, are: 1. Adam; 2. Seth; 3. Enosh; 4. Kenan; 5. Mahalalel; 6. Jared; 7. Enoch; 8. Methuselah; 9. Lamech; 10. Noah.

These ten names represent the heads of the ten intervening generations. But it is not to be understood by reading these chapters of Genesis that we are expected to accept words in their literal sense and limit the population of the world between Adam and Noah solely to the names mentioned in the Bible. The lapse of time during the intervening generations was considerable. In the Torah it is said that some of these men lived a long time. Methuselah, the man said to have lived longest in all history, has a recorded life of 969 years.

There are some denominations who take every word in the Bible literally; they are called Fundamentalists; and their errors are a mess of stupidity, because they do not understand the spirit of the Torah, and because they study it in languages other than Hebrew, so that they cannot see it in its true light.

The Talmud has given us a clear conception of the Torah. The beginning of recorded history has nothing to do with the creation of the world. The formation of the universe, perhaps, took mil-: lions of years. God created the sun and the moon and the stars; each planetary system, according to the Talmud, received its own atmosphere, peculiar to its own region. For example, the atmosphere on our earth is altogether different than the atmosphere on the moon, or on Mars, or on Saturn, or on any other planet. There are countless solar systems; no human mind has ever been able to calculate their number. Since creation scientists have come and gone, many things have happened, and no one has yet discovered that this statement of the Talmudists is erroneous. No one has as yet been able to master the secret of time and space, save only to learn that both are infinite. And, because of that, it is immaterial whether it has taken millions of years before the earth was created in its present form, whether it is the result of evolution, or whether it is the result of the great long process of creation. The Torah tells us the simple story that God created everything. It was the masterful hand of God which has made possible all of this to be. That belief is fundamental with us, not the length of time it took to form this massive universe in its present state.

Now, as to the question of the flood: the fundamentalists maintain that the flood covered the globe; consequently, they ought to arrive at the conclusion that the earth is flat, and, if that should be considered a Biblical doctrine it is a contradiction to a scientific fact. On such premise science and the Bible can never be united; and there will always have to be people to believe that the earth is flat in order to respect the Bible. Nothing is further from the truth than such doctrine in Judaism. In the Talmud it is stated that the flood did not cover Palestine, but that it did cover Mesopo-tomia, because Palestine is higher, and the flood could not reach it. Abraham " ascended" to Palestine, and Jacob "descended" from Palestine into Egypt. Thus it is obvious that fundamentalism is not a necessary doctrine in order to believe in and revere the Torah, or that Judaism fears the light of science.

There are millions of people in China. In that country's annals only few names are outstanding. The American Republic consists of a hundred and twenty million people. How many names, out of these, are found in history books of the schools of the country? From the almost two hundred million people in Russia, only a few figured during the recent revolution there. Consequently, when the story of the development of the Jewish people is related in the Torah, for it is with the Hebrews that the Torah is primarily concerned, it is but natural that only a few names should survive. After Adam and his son Seth are mentioned, all his children and grand-children are forgotten. The same is true of the next and of the other generations.

Between Noah and Abraham again ten generations elapse; the heads of the generations being: 1. Shem; 2. Arpachsad; 3. Shelah; 4. Eber; 5. Peleg; 6. Reeuh; 7. Serug; 8. Nahor; 9. Terah; 10. Abraham.

After Abraham was born, the sons of Noah and their descendants are eliminated, and the story is centered around Abraham's descendants. And there were six intervening generations between Abraham and Moses: Isaac, Jacob, Levi, Kehot, Amram, and Moses. Thus the twenty-six intervening generations between Adam and Moses are eliminated and the chief concern remains only for that branch of Semitic race which sprang forth from Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob and his twelve sons, but not by Abraham through Ishmael, or by Isaac through Esau.

This forms a chapter of the history of the development of the Jewish people; how they came to Egypt; how they were liberated; how they were led through the wilderness; how God appeared to them on Sinai; how they approached the borders of the promised land, and how Joshua succeeded Moses.

The legalism of the Torah consists of two distinct branches: one is strictly legal; and the other is advisory. The legal matter is divided into two grand divisions: 1. Mandatory commandments; 2. Prohibitive commandments. Of the mandatory commandments there are in the Torah 248; of the prohibitive commandments, 365, a total of 613 commandments, which form the constitutional provisions of the Pentateuch. In fact, it is the source of all civilized law. These 613 commandments of the Torah embrace every conceivable law and are divided according to Maimonides, into fourteen divisions:

1\. Knowledge.

2\. Love.

3\. Seasons.

4\. Sanctification.

5\. Women.

6\. Ethical conduct as regards promises, vows,

obligations, and oaths.

7\. Agriculture.

8\. The Temple, its services, public worship,

prayers.

9\. Sacrifices.

10\. Contamination and purification.

11\. Torts.

12\. Barter and Finance.

13\. Civil Laws.

14\. Executives, Legislature, and Judiciary.

There is a distinction between "upright conduct" and being "within the law." The Torah advises man how to conduct himself toward others. We call that in Hebrew Liphnim Mishurat Hadyn. It means above, or deeper than the law aims at. If a man is charged to observe a certain law, he merely does his duty; if he refrains from committing a crime, he simply obeys the law; but If a man goes out of his way and does another man a favor, and conducts himself in such a manner that others imitate and follow his example, he acts higher than the law, Liphnim Mishurat Hadin. That is the advisory element of our Torah. The Advisory Element of our Torah is again divided into five grand divisions: 1. Ethics. 2. Morality. 3. Neighborliness. 4. Abstinence and Sobriety. 5. Honesty as it has been conceived by the ex-

ponents of our Torah.
