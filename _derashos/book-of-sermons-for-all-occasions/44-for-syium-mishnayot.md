Mishnah is a noun formed from the verb Shanah. The verb Shanah, which originally meant to repeat, acquired in post-Biblical Hebrew, the special force of "to teach" and "to learn," that which was not transmitted in writing but only orally. The noun Mishnah has seven distinct meanings (1) Instructing the teaching and learning of the traditions; (2) In a concrete sense the content of that instruction, the traditional doctrine as was developed down to the beginning of the third century, C. E. The Mishnah, in this sense, is used to designate law which was transmitted orally in contrast to Mikra, the written law; (3) The Mishnah connotes also the sum and substance of the teachings of a single Tanna, for example: we find in the Tractate Gittin, 67a, and in the Tractate Yebamot, 49b, and 50a, the expression "Mishnat Rabbi Eliezer ben Jacob", which means the teachings of Rabbi Eliezer ben Jacob; (4) The view of a Tanna in regard to some one mat ter, for example: we find in Menahet, 18a, the expression "Mishnat Rabbi Eliezer," which means the view of Rabbi Eliezer. We also find the expressions "Mishnah Rishonah" and " Mishnah Aharonah" which mean the earlier and the later view, in Hagigah, 2a; (5) A single tenet as we find it in Baba Mezia, 33b, Baba Kama, 94b, and Shabbath, 123b, being in this sense parallel to the expression Halakah; (6) Any collection of such tenets as for instance, "Mishnayet Gedolot" of Rabbi Akiba, Rabbi Hiyya, Rabbi Hoshiah, and Bar Kappara; (7) The collection of laws made by Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi. For the purpose of making a survey of the Mishnah, the noun is employed in its meaning just given.

Judah, I, or Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi, editor of the Mishnah, was born about 135 and died about 220, C. E. He was the first of Hillel's successors to whose name the title of hereditary dignity, ha-Nasi, meaning the prince, was added as a permanent epithet. To a great extent his name is hardly ever mentioned, but he is simply called Rabbi, meaning the Master Par Excellence. Occasionally he is referred to as Rabbenu, our Master. The epithet, ha-Kadosh, meaning the holy, was occasionally added to Rabbenu. Two of his prominent disciples, Rab and Levi, in speaking of him, added to the term Rabbi the explanatory sentence: "Who is this?" and the reply was: "Rabbenu ha-Kadosh," as it is found in Pesahim 37b and Shabbath 157a. It is related in Kiddushin 72b that he was born on the same day on which Rbabi Akibba died a martyr's death. The place of his birth is not known, nor is it recorded where his father, Simon son of Gamallel II, sought refuge with his family during the persecutions under Hadrian. He spent his youth in Usha which, after order was restored in Palestine, became the seat of the then central academy. It appears from Sotah, 49b, that Rabbi included Greek in his studies. This fitted him for intercourse with the Roman authorities. He had a predilection for this language, saying that the Jews of Palestine who did not speak Hebrew should consider Greek as the language of the country, while Syriac (Aramaic) had no claim to that distinction. In his house, however, pure Hebrew was spoken, and the choice speech even of "the maids of the house of Rabbi" became famous, as it appears in Megilah, 18a, Nazir 3a, and Erubin, 53a.

The Mishnah consists of a collection of the traditional interpretation of the Torah. In Erubin, 54b, it is related that Moses instructed the interpretation of the Torah as he had ascended on the Mount Sinai in the following manner: Moses en tered his tent, followed by Aaron. He then instructed Aaron in a given commandment, accompanied by its oral interpretation and meaning. Aaron then took a seat at the right of Moses. After that, Aaron's sons, Elazar and Ittamar, entered the tent. Moses then repeated to them the lesson he had given to Aaron. The two sons then took seats, one to the left of Moses and the other to the right of Aaron. Then the seventy elders came into the tent, and Moses repeated to them the lesson which he had given to Aaron and his sons. After that, the public was admitted, and Moses instructed the public in the lesson that he had given to Aaron, his sons, and to the seventy elders. Consequently, Aaron heard the commandment and its interpretation from the mouth of Moses four times, Aaron's sons, three times, the seventy elders, twice, and the general public once. Moses then left the tent, and Aaron took up the seat of instruction. He, in turn, instructed all those present identically as he had heard it from Moses four times. Then he departed. His sons, who by now had received the instructions four times, three times from Moses and once from their father, in turn instructed as they had heard it, to those present, and they departed. By that time the seventy elders had heard the commandment and its oral interpretation expounded four times, twice by Moses, once by Aaron, and once by Aaron's sons. The seventy elders then, in turn, instructed the public as they had heard it. By that time the entire public had heard it four times, once from Moses, once from Aaron, once from Aaron's sons, and once from the elders. Thereafter, the general public departed to repeat what they had heard and made notes upon parchment. In addition, appointed officials were sent throughout the camps of Israel to instruct them in that particular commandment and its oral interpretation.

This became the traditional law of the succeeding generations, so that each generation understood the meaning of every commandment in the Torah. Without these interpretations the Torah itself and its commandments could not be properly understood. For example: there is a commandment, saying: "Ye shall dwell in booths seven days" (Leviticus 23). Under this commandment it would mean that everybody, men, women, and children, must move out of their houses, and in all kinds of weather, under all sorts of conditions, they must dwell in booths. This commandment was orally interpreted by Moses to mean: 1. That the commandment is applicable only to male adults; 2. That the sick are not obliged to live in booths; 3. That its roof must not be covered with any other substance save that which grows from the ground. In this way all of the commandments of the Torah, were orally interpreted.

In the fortieth year after the exodus from Egypt, on the new moon of the month of Shebat, Moses gathered the people and said to them: "The time of my demise is drawing near. If there be among you such who had heard a law and forgot it, let him come and ask me and I shall explain it to him; and whosoever has a doubt concerning some question, shall come to me and I shall make it clear to him." Before his demise Moses, by his own hand, reduced to writing the entire Torah and he wrote thirteen copies of the Torah, from the first letter of the beginning of the Torah till the last letter of the end of the Torah, and he gave one copy to each of the twelve tribes, so that they may follow it and live according to its interpretation of life, and the thirteenth copy he gave to the Levites; but the oral interpretations were not reduced to writing, but each one preserved it by transmitting it orally to his successor. Some of the scholars preserved notes, others made entire collections of the traditional interpretation of the Torah.

So it continued from the time of Moses until our Holy Master, Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi collected all these oral interpretations and became its re dactor and what he considered the purest of all traditional matter he compiled in six Orders, or Sedarim.

The Mishnah contains the entire structure of Halakah which are divided into five grand divisions:

1\. Traditional interpretations attributed to

Moses which have a basis in the Torah and which may be deducted by pure reasoning. Such laws are not controversial and when one maintains that he had received it so traditional-

ly, no one may contradict him.

2\. Laws which tradition attributes to Moses, he having been so instructed upon Mount Sinai, although they are not supported by any evidence in the Torah. Even such laws

are not controversial.

3\. Laws which were derived by means of pure reasoning, concerning which there were controversies and a decision was reached by strength of a majority.

4\. Ordinances and safeguards which the prophets and scholars proclaimed and enacted

in each generation so as to strengthen the Torah.

5\. Laws which were enacted by common consent in matters affecting men and men, wherein there is found neither adding to nor diminishing from any commandment. Such are called by-laws and rules of procedure. Such by-laws and rules of procedure are very numerous. Moses was the first one to ordain some rules of procedure and conduct, and so it went on until the days of Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi. Consequently, all the laws which are mentioned in the Mishnah are of one of the enumerated five categories.
