In the first part of the Introduction to the Talmud, it was pointed out that the Halakah, which means law, and with which we are now concerned, consists of two distinct elements: 1. The laws which received the sanction of all schools from the days of Moses till the completion of the Talmud. Such laws are known under the term of Halakah M'kuyemet, which mean recognized and sustained law. Such law regulates and affects all branches of the religious, social and cultural life of the Jewish people. 2. Rejected Law, known under the term of Halakah D'huyah. The inclusion of the rejected law, which now constitutes an integral part of the Talmud, was necessary in order to preserve a record of all proposed laws, thereby teaching posterity that any law which was rejected must not be recognized, inaugurated, or proposed. During the centuries that these laws were being developed, not the entire Jewish people were settled in Palestine. Most of these laws were enacted in Babylon. The Talmud is, therefore, known as the "Babylonian Talmud." some of these laws became a part of the Talmud before and some after the Maccabean struggle for independence. When the Romans conquered Palestine and dispersed the greater number of the Jewish people throughout the neighboring countries, first the Tanaim and then the Amoraim who continued to store up knowledge and increase scholarship, became the source of strength which sustained the Jewish people throughout the centuries of dispersion and persecution.

This part of the Talmud described as Halakah, both the sustained and the rejected, is called G'ma-ra. The G'mara, is an interpretation of the Mishnah. The G'mara, in its construction, consists, in so far as that part known as Halakah is concerned, mainly of the 21 different sources of mat-terial enumerated in Part I of this introduction. The Talmud, in its entire composition, covers the full scope of Jewish learning from the days of Moses until Mar, son of Ashi, who, together with Rabina, edited the Talmud at the close of the fifth century, C. E. The different sources of material of which the Talmud is constructed, that is to say, the 21 different sources, are identified by certain words to indicate their origin, and governed by certain rules, so that the Talmudic scholar, when studying a page of the Talmud, is able to tell what kind of material that particular subject is made of, when and by whom it was composed, and whether or not it is sustained or rejected law. For example:

The Tosefta, which mean "addition", consists of parts of controversial law which was left out by Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi and which he did not include in the Mishnah. When the Talmud was edited this mass of material was included. In order to identify this material, its source, and its authenticity, whether it was sustained or rejected law, whether it is from the Tosefta or not, means of identification were provided as follows: When such material appears in the Talmud immediately following the Mishnah, it is considered Halakah M'kuyemet, Sustained Law. That is to say, a quotation from the Tosefta which follows the Mishnah, is in itself indicative, if it be a law, that such law was sustained and remains binding. First, is the Mishnah. Then, if the sentence following the Mishnah is a quotation from the Tosefta, it is self-evident that such law established by the Talmudists as binding. In order to identify the source that it really is a quotation from the Tosefta, the quotation begins with the word "Tana." Consequently, a paragraph immediately following the text of a paragraph from the Mishnah which begins with the word Tana means that the source of such paragraph is the Tosefta.

At the time that Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi was the president of the Academy he had a number of disciples and associates who decided that the Tosefta be not included in the Mishnah, but that its authentic matter be considered an addition to the Mishnah, as the word Tosefta implies "addition."

At the same time and for a number of years thereafter, the material out of which the Baraita is composed was collected. The chief redactor of the Baraita was Rabbi Hiyyah, the outstanding disciple of Rabbi Judah-ha-Nasi. The Baraita also includes the private Mishnaic collections of Rabbi Oshaiah Rabba (The great), Rabbi Eliezer ben-Jacob, Rabbi Ishmael, and Rabi Akiba. The text of the Baraita, however, was not included in the instructions given by Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi at his Academy. The teachings enunciated by the authors of the Baraita were considered matters belonging outside of the Academy. The term Baraita implies a work produced outside of the Academy. The Mishnah of Rabbi Eliezer ben-Jacob, which is spoken of as being clear of controversial matter and free of errors, is included in the Baraita. (Gittin 67a). The Mishnayot of Rabbi Oshaiah Rabba, too, are included in the Baraita. It is evident from many Talmudic sources that Rabbi Oshaiah the great was the chief collaborator of Rabbi Hiy-yah in the construction of the Baraita. It is authoritatively stated that any part of the Baraita which escaped the editorial censure of Rabbi Hiyyah and Rabbi Oshaiah, is erroneous and must not be included among the teachings in the Beth-Hamed-resh. (Rashi, Hulin, 141b). The Baraita also includes the text of the Mehilta of Rabbi Ishmael, of the Otiot of Rabbi Akiba, of the Sifra on Leviticus, and of the Sifre on Numbers and Deuteronomy.

The editors of the Talmud inserted identification words at the beginning of every quotation from the Baraita. Of these there were three outstanding headings: 1. Tanu Rabanon; 2. Tana Hada; 2. Tanya Edach.

Whenever a Baraitic text is quoted in the Talmud, if no controversial matter follows it, it is indicative that the law is sustained as quoted.

For the purpose of elucidating the manner by which the Talmudists expounded the Torah there were, of course, necessary to create and adopt certain exegetical principles or hermeneutical rules. A number of authors have compiled such rules, chief among the pre-Mishnaic period being Hillel the Elder, who had defined and adopted seven hermeneutical rules; but the rules enumerated in the Baraita of Rabbi Ishmael is the best-known and the most used in the Talmud. The text of Rabbi Ishmael's Baraita concerning the hermeneutical rules is as follows: "Rabbi Ishmael says: There are 13 exegetical principles (hermeneutical rules) by which the Torah is expounded: 1. The inference from a minor premise to a major premise. 2. The inference from a similarity of phrases which appear in different places in the Torah. 3. A general law may be derived by deductive means from different cases which, occurring in one and the same, or in two or more, verses of the Torah, have, nevertheless, some feature in common. 4. A general proposition followed by the enumeration of particulars already understood from the general proposition, in which case the scope of the proposition is limited to the particulars only. 5. An enumeration of particulars followed by a general proposition in which, of course, the particulars, too, are included, in which case the scope of the proposition extends also to the things not particularized. 6. Two general propositions, separated from each other by an enumeration of particulars, include only such things as are similar to the particulars. 7. An inference drawn from a general proposition complemented by a particular term, and an inference drawn from a particular term complemented by a general proposition. 8. If anything is included in a general proposition and is then made the subject of a special statement, that which is predicated of it is not to be understood as limited to itself alone, but is to be applied to the whole of the general proposition. 9. If anything is included in a general proposition and is then singled out in order to be made the subject of a special statement, similar to the general proposition, this particularization is intended, so far as its subject is concerned, to lessen and not to add to its restrictions. 10. If anything is included in a general proposition, and is then singled out in order to be made the subject of a special statement, not similar to the general proposition, this particularization is intended in some respects to lessen and in others to add to its restrictions. 11. If anything is included in a general proposition and is then made the subject of a new statement, not in harmony with the former, the terms of the general proposition will not apply to it unless it is distinctly indicated in the Torah that they shall apply. 12. The meaning of a verse in the Torah may be deduced from its context, or from some subsequent verse. 13. Similarly, when two verses are in contradiction to each other, the explanation can be determined only when a third verse, capable of harmonizing the two, is found. Upon these hermeneutics hinges the great structure of the research work done by the Talmudists, and by these means the truth was arrived at. Each of these thirteen hermeneutical rules are to be defined and illustrated by quotations from the Talmud in the future lectures on the Talmud.
