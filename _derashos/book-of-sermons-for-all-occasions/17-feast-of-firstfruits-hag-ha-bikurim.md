"Ye shall bring out of your dwellings two wave-loaves of two tenth parts of an ephah; they shall be of fine flour, they shall be baked with leaven, for first-fruits unto the Lord." (Lev. 23.17).

Rabbi Ziera inquired: "Wheat which dropped down from the clouds, may flour be made thereof and used for the wave-loaves? Shall the commandment of, 'Ye shall bring out of your dwellings two wave-loaves' be interpreted to exclude only such wheat which grew outside of the Holy Land, but not that of the clouds, which comes down with rain, or exclude all kinds of wheat which came not " of your habitation? Can anything like this happen? Indeed, it can. It happened to Bar Eddy the Arab; wheat a hand high covered his field of three square miles." (Me'nahot, 69b; Yalkut Shimony, Torah, 643).

In interpreting Rabbi Ziera's inquiry, Rashi contends that it is based upon rational groud. A boat-load of grain was sailing upon the ocean; a cloud swallowed up the ship, the grain and all, and then carried it inland, where the rain dropped the grain down. The Tosphite Tam questions the correctness of his grandfather's interpretation, saying: " If the grain originally was from outside of Palestine, what good would the cloud do; there must be grain "of your dwelling," and there is none. Conversely, if the grain was originally from Palestine, what harm could the cloud do to it to disqualify it? But it appears to me that the clouds brought the grain down by miracle, even as it happened to Rabbi Simeon ben Halaphta, when two quarters meat came down from the clouds to save him from hungry lions (Sanhedrin, 59b), which was interpreted as miracleous. (Tosafot, Me'nahot, 69b).

On this day, the anniversary of Revelation, the thinking mind deepens itself into things more heavenly than earthly. But, strange as it may appear, this great anniversary is also connected with the agricultural yield of Palestine. Prosaic, indeed. In addition to the holiday offerings, wave-loaves of fine flour were brought as an offering. It was a tribute in return for bounty, an offering to God for remembering the needs of man.

With all our devotion to the spiritual, we can not separate our being from things earthly, from the material. We are composite beings of matter and spirit, in constant strife with ourselves. Never in all history was there a man or a woman with normal intelligence who lived out his or her alloted span of life without partaking of such strife.

The greatest struggle, in fact a war, is the experience of a real materialist. He has to argue in and out of season that his contention is right. When he is alone, the argument is within his own mind and heart. The atheist knows no peace, either when well or when sick. Every atheist is an advocate for his ideas, and as such he looks for an audience to listen to him. He keeps on denying everything which may turn the balance in favor of a spiritual possibility. He is not in doubt, he claims; he knows, he asserts, that this world, the whole universe, is nothing more than a material machine, driven by force. He is an intolerant fanatic who stops at nothing to carry out his ideas, and to force others to accept them. And, wherever he controls the destiny of his fellows he knows of no compromise. There is no middle-ground for the fanatical atheist.

The same is true of fanatical zealots who, in the name of religion, stop at nothing in order to force their belief upon others.

Judaism disowns both extremes. It neither advises to go near the pillar of fire nor near the pillar of snow. This is the earth, and not the heaven; here are men and children of women, not of angels. This life is a natural life; it can not exist without the material things to be found on earth. There is the golden path, the middle-chamber of the earthly edifice, between the pillar of fire and the pillar of snow.

Our ancestors stood beneath the mount; they were warned not even to try to ascend it. "Take heed to yourselves, that ye go not up into the mount, or touch the border of it," was the admonition given to Israel. Not every one is able to reach out to the mount. Moses and Aaron were the only two among all Israel. "Go, get thee down, and thou shalt come, thou, and Aaron with thee; but let not the priests and the people break through to come up," was another admonition at the time of Revelation. The inner "Vineyard-Pardas-is not for every one to enter and come out sound.

This earth could not have been populated with a race which would be able to reach out to the top of Sinai; it would cease to be the earth; it would become part of heaven-nay higher than heaven itself. Not every one desiring to obtain the name of prophet may come and take it. We must never lose sight of the fact that our feet must reach the ground in order to be able to stand up. The minute we forget this we stumble, yea, we fall.

This is the anniversary of Revelation. True, we commemorate that on this day we were given proper instructions how to be men and women upon earth; by being so, we attain the goal set up for us by Him who sits in heaven.

We bring firstfruits. It is the holiday of firstfruits. We give of what we receive. We bring wave-loaves which grew "in our dwelling-place." We dwell on earth, and we realize that. We can not bring an offering from heaven, but to heaven.

Rabbi Ziera, being anxious lest the every-day nature of reducing this holiday to firstfruits would be conducive of lessening its spiritual significance, inquired: "Wheat which dropped down from the clouds, may flour be made thereof and used for wave-loaves?"

Rashi, the great thinker, could not conceive that Rabbi Ziera would advocate heavenly offer ings for earthly beings. He rationalized the investigating character of Rabbi Ziera's inquiry, by declaring: "Clouds first absorbed the wheat and then dropped it down together with rain. A composite offering, the dew of heaven and the fat of the earth."

His grandson, Rabbi Tam could not conceive this. He maintained that such is possible only by means of miracles. Rabbi Ziera's question was never decided; it remains till this day unsolved; it is an enigma for the prophet Elijah-Teku-but not soluble by ordinary mortals.

It is true that God, at times, changes nature. But that happens only when nature becomes helpless. The lions were hungry; it was night-time; the only morsel in sight was the body of Rabbi Simeon ben Halaphta. His life would have been lost, had nature taken its course. But a miracle happened; meat came down from the clouds; the lions ate to their heart's content, and ben Halaphta was saved.

Israel witnessed similar miracles throughout the generations; but for his offerings he ever used earthly fire, earthly ingredients, earthly animals, and earthly messengers to bring them upon the altar of God.

The Feast of Firstfruits is the self-expression of the holiday; it brings home the lesson that we can be godly, by being what we are-merely men and women.
