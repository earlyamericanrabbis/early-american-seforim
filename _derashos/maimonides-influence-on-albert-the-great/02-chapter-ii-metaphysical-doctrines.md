Albert the Great, the most eminent German philosopher and theologian of the Middle Ages and the real founder of the scientific tendency within the order of the Dominicans, was hardly surpassed by any of his contemporaries in wide reading and versatility of knowledge. Albertus Magnus devoted special attention to Jewish literature in so far as it was accessible to him. He utilized the Talmud and owed to it many useful suggestions,—this through the instrumentality of Jewish authors.[^35]

The famous Jewish physician and philosopher, ISAAC ISRAELI, the elder, (832-932), whose views, mostly taken from his works, DE DEFINITIONIBUS, and DE ELEMENTIS, Albert often quotes,[^36] and to whom he ascribes the identification, adopted by the philosophers, of ethereal spirits with the angels of the Bible,[^37] is considered by him as a chief representative of the Jewish philosophy of Maimonides.[^38]

Albert the Great devoted also special study to the FONS VITAE of Avicebron (1020-1070), who, for centuries, had been believed to be a Christian scholastic philosopher, but who, only recently, has been clearly proved by Munk to be identical with the Jew, Rabbi Solomon Ibn Gabirol.[^39] In a critical survey of the views of the elder philosophers, which is found at the beginning of his work DE CAUSIS ET PROCESSU UNIVERSITATIS, not only the doctrines of the Epicureans, of the Stoics, of Socrates, and of Plato, but also those of Avicebron, are thoroughly examined. As a Dominican, he contests very strongly from the peripatetic standpoint most of the Neo-Platonic doctrines of Avicebron.[^40] These doctrines were adhered to and formed the basis of the Platonist Franciscans, but were rejected by the Aristotelian Dominicans. In particular, Avicebron's doctrine of universal matter, "which the first Franciscan teachers had borrowed from Avicebrol",[^41] and which was later revived by Duns Scotus, was firmly combated by the Dominicans, led by Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas. Albert, nevertheless, recognizes the originality of the system sketched out in the FONS VITAE. Not only does he acknowledge the originality of Avicebron in his doctrine of the first matter and the first form,[^42] but also in his doctrine of human free will. He shows this by calling Avicebrol the only philosopher who represents the first principle as acting through an individual will.[^43] His opposition to Avicebron's general doctrines did not hinder Albert, however, from appropriating in certain points, as, for instance, in the division of forms, the doctrines outlined in the FONS VITAE.[^44]

Quite different, however, from his attitude toward the doctrines of Avicebrol is the attitude of Albert the Great towards those of Moses Maimonides, Talmudist, philosopher, astronomer and physician, who was born at Cordova, March 30, 1135, and died at Cairo, December 13, 1204.[^45] Like Maimonides, standing essentially upon the ground of Arabic-Aristotelian philosophy, Albert the Great, in his effort to harmonize the doctrines of Biblical revelation with opposing Aristotelian tenets, followed in many points the RABBI MOYSES AEGYPTIUS, as he is generally wont to call him, from whose MOREH NEBUKIM, which he quotes usually under the title of DUX NEUTRORUM, he "not only took single passages, but entire sections, and incorporated them into his works."[^46]

The influence of Maimonides upon Albert the Great, with respect to metaphysical doctrines, is already apparent when Albert teaches that God, as the Infinite Principle of all things and because of the infinite fullness of His light, is exalted above human comprehension, just as the sun which, because of its immensity, cannot be truly perceived. "Sicut tamen lumen," he writes in his SUMMA, "secundum immensitatem, quam habet in rota solis, et secundum immensitatem potestatis, qua omnia visibilia comprehendere potest, non potest capi vel comprehendi a visu, ita et intellectus divinus secundum excellentiam qua excellit in seipsa, et secundum potestatem, qua illustrare potest super omnia, etiam super infinita intelligibilia, capi vel comprehendi non potest ab intellectu creato."[^47]

In practically the same words Maimonides writes in his MOREH that "God cannot be the object of human comprehension....He....is invisible to us through the intensity of His light like the sun which cannot be perceived by eyes which are too weak to bear its rays."[^48]

More expressly, however, does Albert refer to Maimonides in many passages where the question is discussed as to how much of a knowledge of God is attainable through negative attributes. Thus he cites the view of Maimonides: "Quia tamen, <u>ut dicit RABBI MOYSES</u>, negatio restringitur ad cogniti naturam, iste alius modus est cognitionis."[^49] Again, he writes: "Si forte dicas, quod negatio facit innotescere, <u>ut dicit RABBI MOYSES in libro, qui dicitur dux neutrorum</u>, praecipue in divinis"[^50] etc.

While Albert was undoubtedly influenced by Maimonides' Doctrine of Negative Attribution, as, indeed, he has occasion to refer to this doctrine many times,[^51] he only agreed with the latter's doctrine to the extent that negation was one of several ways of attaining knowledge of God. He did not, however, agree with Maimonides' allegation, which shall immediately be indicated, that, except by divine grace, there is no other knowledge of God possible except through negative qualifications.

Maimonides, indeed, devotes several chapters in his work to a thoroughgoing exposition of his Principle of Negative Attributes.[^52] He firmly holds that "the negative attributes of God are the true attributes: they do not include any incorrect notions or any deficiency whatever in reference to God, while positive attributes imply polytheism, and are inadequate."[^53] "We cannot describe the Creator by any means," he asserts, "except by negative attributes."[^54] "It is clear," he writes further, "that He has no positive attributes whatsoever. The negative attributes, however, are those which are necessary to direct the mind to the truths which we must believe concerning God; for, on the one hand, they do not imply any plurality, and, on the other hand, they convey to man the highest possible knowledge of God."[^55] He keeps on emphasizing the fact that "by each additional negative attribute you advance towards the knowledge of God" and that "every time you establish by proof the negation of a thing in reference to God you become more perfect."[^56] Devoting a whole chapter[^57] to illustrate "the propriety of forming as many negative attributes as possible, and the impropriety of ascribing to God any positive attributes," he urges that "you will come nearer to the knowledge and comprehension of God by the negative attributes."[^58] He indeed claims that it is "a well-known fact that even that knowledge of God which is accessible to man cannot be attained except by negations,"[^59] putting it, paradoxically, thus: "Our knowledge consists in knowing that we are unable truly to comprehend Him."[^60]

In conformity also with Maimonides, Albert holds as the fundamental principle of his doctrine of the knowledge of God that, as between the finite and the infinite there exists not a complete analogy, therefore the same attribute, applied to the finite and infinite, does not signify the same thing, and is used in a partly different sense. "Propter quod," writes Albert, "non est univoce proedicatio, quando haec de primo et de secundis praedicantur....Ex hoc fit, quod non una ratione praedicatur de causa et causato, sed ratione, qua praedicatur de causato, negatur de causa, et in ratione, qua praedicatur de causa, negatur de causato."[^61]

It is clearly evident that Albert was profoundly influenced in this by Maimonides who devotes a full chapter[^62] to prove that even the "essential attributes in God, viz., Existence, Life, Power, Wisdom, and Will....when applied to God, have not the same meaning as when applied to us, and that the difference does not only consist in magnitude, or in the degree of perfection, stability, and durability. It cannot be said....that His existence is only more stable, His life more permanent, His power greater, His wisdom more perfect, and His will more general than ours....The terms Wisdom, Power, Will and Life are applied to God and other beings by way of perfect homonymity, admitting of no comparison whatever....There is, in no way or sense, anything common to the attributes predicated of God, and those used in reference to ourselves; they have only the same names, and nothing else is common to them."[^63]

In reference to the names of God Albert has again occasion to cite Maimonides. The former, as the latter, holds that since the essence of God is unknowable, His full essence can, therefore, not be expressed by any name. Among all the names that are attributed to God, however, the most appropriate one is—as he calls to witness Maimonides—the Ineffable Name of God, that is the Tetragrammaton, because in it the pure essence of God, without any other qualification superadded, comes to expression.

"Et nota," Albert writes, "quod propter simplicem intellectum entis creati, ut dictum est, <u>dicit RABBI MOYSES in libro de duce neutrorum</u>, quod: Qui est, est nomen ineffabile Dei et praecipuum inter omnia nomina, quia hoc nominat, quod ipse verissime est simpliciter sine additione aliqua."[^64]

Albert has summarized here what he learned from the three chapters[^65] which Maimonides devotes to a thorough discussion of the Names of God. In the first of these[^66] Maimonides shows that "the Tetragrammaton....is applied exclusively to God, and is on that account called SHEM HA-MEFORASH, 'The nomen proprium.' It is the distinct and exclusive designation of the Divine Being;[^67]... It denotes God Himself, without including in its meaning any names of the things created by Him....All other names have reference to qualities, and do not signify a simple substance, but a substance with attributes, they being derivatives";[^68]...The Tetragrammaton....is the only name which indicates nothing but His essence."[^69]

In order to properly understand the reference made by Albert to Maimonides with respect to the doctrine of emanation, it is necessary first to consider the position of Maimonides with regard to this doctrine. The Jewish philosopher sets himself up against the theory of emanation[^70] on the grounds that this view, by stipulating that all things proceeded from God by the fixed laws of nature and in a gradual process of development, no explanation was offered how from the first and simple Principle the manifold and the complex had unfolded.[^71] Since, he argues, "Aristotle and all philosophers assume as an axiom that a simple element can only produce one simple thing,"[^72] and since "The first Intellect is undoubtedly simple,"[^73] the question arises, "How then can the compound form of existing things come from such an Intellect by fixed laws of Nature, as Aristotle assumes?"[^74] He feels justified, therefore, in concluding: "It must now be clear that this emanation could not have taken place by the force of the laws of nature."[^75] Maimonides, finally, solves the difficulty thus: "If we, however, assume design and determination of a Creator, in accordance with His incomprehensible wisdom, all these difficulties disappear."[^76]

Albert, however, strangely enough, believes that Maimonides makes this last statement only from a tendency to 'theologize,' but that the latter's viewpoint very well allows itself to become identical with the doctrine of emanation propounded by the Peripatetics.

Albert discusses this matter at some length, among other things saying: "Hoc ergo modo secundum antiquos Peripateticos causatur numerus et ordo entium: differentia enim intelligendi causat numerum, distantia vero procesuum et fluxuum est causa ordinis primarii ad secundarium. <u>RABBI MOYSES</u> autem ivit contra hanc viam theologizare volens: <u>dixit</u> enim sapientiam causae primae causam esse numeri et ordinis rerum, quod mirabile videtur. Quamvis enim dicat Aristoteles, quod sapientis est ordinare et ordinari, tamen hoc verum est de ratione, quae concipit rationes ordinatorum, quae non uno modo se habent ad ordinata. Talis autem non est sapientia primae causae, quae non causatur a ratione ordinatorum, sed ab ea uno modo se habente constituuntur ordinata,

did not create things all equidistant from Himself, but rather in a certain progressive order and at various distances, in order that each thing should be a partaker, according to its capacity, in the Divine Good, and that nothing should be excluded from the Divine Essence, which otherwise would not be the universal Cause of all things.

Thus Albert writes: "Cuius (primi boni) gradus et ordinis, <u>ut dicit MOYSES AEGYPTIUS</u>, sapientia lumine suo determinans universa et reductionem eorum in unum potissima est causa. Tanta enim est largitas primi et tanta nobilitas et tanta amplitudo bonitatum eius, quod nihil secundorum capere posset eas, secundum quod in ipso sunt, et propterea sapientia prima non produxit res ex aequo distantes ab ipsa, sed secundum ordinem procedentium et gradus minus et magis distantium, ut omnis capacitas rerum impleatur bonitate divina et nihil eorum sit, quod esse divinum non participet secundum uniuscuiusque propriam analogiam."[^77]

In another chapter of the same work Albert again cites Maimonides with reference to the same subject, saying: "Causa enim prima non est tantum producens res, sed gradus et ordines rerum praedeterminat, <u>ut dicit MOSES AEGYPTIUS</u>, nisi enim omnium capacitatem impleret, non esset dives majus omnibus, nec esset causa universalis."[^78]

Albert also cites the view of Maimonides, with which he agrees, that in all natural things a fourfold relationship of matter must be distinguished, matter and form first being thought of as logically separate, then hastening, by an affinity for each other, as it were, to unite, then united, and, finally, individuated by a specific quantity. "Quia omnis materis rei naturalis," Albert says, "quatuor modis se habet, <u>ut dicit RABBI MOYSES in duce neutrorum</u>,[^79] scilicet aliter extra formam terminantem et aliter currens ad ipsam, quando jam trahitur a virtute formativa et aliter formata et aliter consolidata in quantitate perfecta."[^80]

Among all the accidents of substance, Albert teaches with Maimonides, quantity is the first to be denoted. "Inter praedicabilia," says Albert, "quae sunt de natura accidentium substantiae, primum occurrit praedicabile, quod est quantitas eo, quod hoc immediate sequitur, <u>ut dicit RABBI MOYSES</u>."[^81] Albert bases this upon the fact that Maimonides in citing the various accidents of substance mentions quantity first. The latter states that "The accidents attributed to material objects are quantity"[^82] etc.

Citing Maimonides as his trustworthy authority, Albert holds that suffering is the result of the nature of matter. "Ex materia," he writes, "hoc est subjectum, et principia subjecti sunt, ex quibus oritur passio. <u>Dicit enim RABBI MOYSES</u>, quod verius dicitur materialis, quae est in corpore vel in materia vel ex materia sicut subjecto et origine."[^83] This is based upon the statement made by Maimonides, in the course of a thoroughgoing discussion of the nature and origin of evil to which he devotes four whole chapters,[^84] that "all corruption, destruction, or defect comes from matter,"[^85] and that "man's shortcomings and sins are all due to the substance of the body"[^86] which is "the substance of dust and darkness, the source of all defect and loss."[^87]

Albert cites the GUIDE as authority for the doctrine, so current to Aristotelians, that everything which is compounded is a body. Albert quotes Maimonides' work thus: "Praeterea dicitur in libro ducis neutrorum cap. de uno Deo quod omne compositum est corpus."[^88]

Albert here refers to the statement of Maimonides that "Material objects are always composed of two elements (at least)";[^89] that "Every corporal object is composed of matter and form,"[^90] and that "Nothing corporal can be a unity."[^91]

Albert further cites Maimonides for the view that all movement in the natural and sublunar world is reducible to the motion of the heavenly bodies. Albert thus writes: "<u>Dicit enim philosophus RABBI MOYSES</u>, quod omnis motus huius naturae reducitur ad motum caeli, qui fecit moveri hanc naturem."[^92] Maimonides discusses this theory many times in his work. "The outermost sphere," he writes, "by its motion rules....over all other parts of the universe....Every motion in the universe has thus its origin in the motion of that sphere."[^93] "It is a well-known fact," he asserts, "that the philosophers, when they discuss in their works the order of the Universe, assume that the existing order of things in this sublunary world of transient beings depends on forces which emanate from the spheres. <u>We have mentioned this several times</u>."[^94]

Among the reasons given why the sun warms more than other stars, Albert refers also to that given by Maimonides, that the sun, its nature being still warm and dry, sets the sphere of fire in motion. "Secunda causa est," says Albert, "<u>quam dat RABBI MOYSES</u>, quod sol natura sua calidus est et siccus et habet movere sphaeram ignis."[^95] Maimonides does speak in his treatise of "the influence of the sun's rays upon fire",[^96] and that "fire is set in motion by the sun-sphere,"[^97] though the reasons for this, accredited to him by Albert, are not specifically mentioned there.[^98]

Finally, to conclude this chapter with a quotation that will lead us directly into the subject of the next chapter, Albert says that as Augustine, so have also Maimonides and other philosophers, who accept the doctrine of creation in time, professed the view, that all things were simultaneously created. "His et eiusmodi rationibus consentit Augustinus, et etiam quidam philosophi ponentes mundum incepisse per creationem tenent viam August., <u>ut RABBI MOYSES</u> et alii quidam."[^99]

Maimonides accepts "the simultaneous Creation of the heavens and the earth,"[^100] without question. "All things were created together," he says, "but were separated from each other successively. Our sages illustrated this by the following simile: We sow various seeds[^101] at the same time; some spring forth after one day, some after two, and some after three days, although all have been sown at the same time."[^102] "I explain, therefore," he concludes, "the first verse in Genesis thus: In creating the principle God created the things above and those below."[^103]
