In order to emphasize the importance of the influence of the Jewish philosopher, Moses Maimonides, exercised particularly through his great philosophical treatise, GUIDE TO THE PERPLEXED, upon the scholastic philosophy of the thirteenth century, this thesis has been undertaken and written. The significance of this fact is, unfortunately, very much overlooked, or, at least, underestimated, and, therefore, not brought out in sufficient prominence,[^1] surely not in the degree relative to its significant bearing upon the whole stream of scholastic thought. It is the express purpose of the writer, therefore, to present ample and incontestable evidence of the weighty import of the influence exerted by Maimonides upon many of the fundamental tenets of scholasticism.

Except for the first chapter in which the general position of Maimonides in scholastic philosophy has been summed up, and the chief doctrines of scholasticism influenced by Maimonides briefly pointed out, the succeeding chapters of this thesis have been devoted to the presentation of indubitable evidence of the powerful influence exerted by the Jewish philos-opher upon Albert the Great, in particular. I have selected the consideration of Albert the Great, in preference to that of other scholastics,—even to that of his more illustrious disciple, Thomas Aquinas,—designedly; because "the real influence of the GUIDE on Christian thought begins with the Dominican Albertus Magnus."[^2] It will also be seen that since his distinguished pupil, Thomas, received the bulk of scholastic doctrine from his learned master, Albert, it would naturally follow that whatever influenced the philosophical doctrine of the one would also go into the molding of the philosophical system of the other. By dwelling, therefore, upon the influence of Maimonides upon Albert, the teacher, the writer feels he is, ipso facto, dwelling upon the bearing of the former upon Thomas Aquinas, his more famous pupil, and the greatest luminary that loomed upon the horizon of the Golden Age of Scholasticism.

If one wishes to properly appreciate the influence of Moses Maimonides upon Albert the Great one must first obtain the correct perspective of the general position which Maimonides occupies in the field of scholastic philosophy.

A very engaging parallel, indeed, it seems to me, can be drawn between the first centuries of the Christian era and the thirteenth century in which Albert lived and worked. It appears as though, in keeping with the first great fact of the faith of Jews and Judaism serving as a preparation for the faith of Christianity as a whole, that, ever after, whenever Christianity was to expand in philosophical depth and meaning, the philosophy of Jews and Judaism should, again and again, serve as a preparation for the further development of Christian philosophy.

"The early Christian philosophy," Professor Leighton writes, "is a synthesis of the Christian religion and Greek philosophy, <u>for which the Jewish-Greek philosophy of Philo paved the way</u>."[^3] Just as Philo (30 B. C. E.—40 C. E.), the Jewish theologian of Alexandria, prepared the way for Platonic Christianity which found its greatest expression in Augustine (354-430), "the greatest and most influential theologian of the Christian Middle Ages and possibly of all Christian centuries",[^4] who was the Plato of Christianity, so did Moses Maimonides (1135-1204), the Jewish philosopher of Cairo, pave the way for Christian Aristotelianism which was brought to quite a high point of development by Albert the Great (1193-1280), but which found its most distinguished exponent in his famous disciple, Thomas Aquinas (1225-1274), who was "the first to formulate a complete system of Christian Aristotelianism."[^5] Just as Greco-Jewish philosophy, by harmonizing the "Zeitphilosophie,"—the philosophy of the time,—namely, Platonism, with the teachings of the Bible—Philo, its chief representative, making this the principal aim of his philosophy,[^6]—just as the Greco-Jewish synthesis prepared the way for the Patristic philosophy of the first centuries of the Christian era culminating in Augustine, so did the Jewish-Aristotelian philosophy of the twelfth century, whose most distinguished representative was Moses Maimonides, termed by scholastics "the greatest of Jewish Aristotelians,"[^7] who sought to harmonize the "Zeitphilosophie," namely, Aristotelianism, with the Bible,—so did the Jewish-Aristotelian synthesis pave the way for the Golden Age of Scholasticism. Jewish Platonism, represented by Philo, its chief exponent, made possible Christian Platonism, whose able spokesman was Augustine. Jewish Aristotelianism, whose cause Maimonides so masterfully defended, made possible Christian Aristotelianism, whose erudite builders were Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas.

Perhaps more important, however, than the harmonizing of the doctrines of Aristotle with those of the Bible was the need for independent criticism of those tenets of Aristotelianism which were irreconcilably opposed to the basic teachings of Revelation. Such, for example, were the Aristotelian doctrine of the eternity of the universe and, what the eternal, fixed, immutable laws of nature, necessitated by this doctrine, consequently imply, the denial of God's free will; the limitation of the knowledge of God to universals only, and, what the impossibility of God's having knowledge of particulars necessarily implies, the denial of individual Providence. These Peripatetic tenets could not be possibly harmonized with the fundamental beliefs of the Word of God which clearly taught that the universe was created by a free agent who was omniscient and whose individual providence extended to his creatures. Here was a definite clash of doctrines, an unmistakeable conflict of tenets.

Small occasion for wonder should there be, therefore, that the Christian Church twice prohibited the reading of Aristotle's works. For, as Turner writes, "The Aristotle that was twice condemned was hostile to Christianity."[^8] In fact, in the very decree in which the prohibition was promulgated it was readily admitted that the condemnatory edict was based upon the fact that it was believed that upon philosophical principles deduced from the laws of the natural order it was impossible both to refute the arguments advanced for the doctrine of the eternity of the universe and to defend the Biblical doctrine of creation.[^9]

What was needed here was an indomitable, courageous spirit, fully equipped with a thorough acquaintance with both the Aristotelian and the Biblical teachings, who could, without departing one iota from the basic philosophical principles of Aristotelianism, and, in fact, using these as his foundation,—who could be yet able to refute the apparently irrefutable arguments advanced by Peripatetics for the doctrine of the eternity of the universe and its ensuing corollaries, and, on the basis of philosophic reflection, to justify the opposing Biblical tenets.

The champion of the cause of the Bible appeared in the person of Moses Maimonides, who, by making a definite delimitation of the respective boundaries of faith and philosophy, saved the day and made it possible for the introduction of Aristotle into theology.

Munk, in his MELANGES, dwells upon this important function of Maimonides' philosophical activity. "In order to bring about," he writes, "if this were possible, a reconciliation between Judiasm and philosophy, there was needed a spirit, who, possessing both of these, should combine calmness and charity with energy and profundity, and who should be capable, by his imposing knowledge and penetrating criticism, to light up the whole domain of religion by the torch of science, and <u>to fix with precision the limits of speculation and faith</u>. The great man who undertook this mission was the illustrious Moses ben Maimum, commonly called Maimonides. To the most profound knowledge of the vast religious literature of the Jews, he joined that of all the secular sciences then accessible in the Arabian world...... Offering thus the means of embracing the whole of the religious system, he was able, if not to entirely reconciliate philosophy and religion, at least to effect a conciliation between them, and, in recognizing the rights of each, to render them capable of mutually controlling themselves and sustaining themselves....From a philosophical point of view, his GUIDE TO THE PERPLEXED rendered them (i. e. the Jews) capable...of exercising through it an <u>incontestable influence</u> upon scholasticism."[^10]

The fixing of the boundaries of speculation and faith and the defense of the latter when it came into unavoidable conflict with the former is also pointed out by Guttmann as characteristic of the philosophy of the Jewish Peripatetic. After calling attention to the fact that Albert the Great, when discussing in his treatises questions involving purely Aristotelian principles, had more occasion to cite the opinions of the Arabic-Aristotelian philosophers than those of the Jewish Aristotelian, he continues thus: "Where, however, a question involved the fixing of the limits of the field of faith from that of philosophy and the defending of the standpoint of the believer in the Bible as opposed to the authority of Aristotle, there Maimonides offers himself as the only writer, accessible to the Western theologian, who attempted a solution of this problem and whom, therefore, Albert the Great also followed in many points."[^11]

Indeed one of the main services of Maimonides' GUIDE to European thought, in general, and to scholasticism, in particular, is its fearless, independent criticism of many important Aristotelian tenets.[^12] Maimonides, in fact, casts ridicule on those "who blindly follow" the Greek philosopher—who "consider it wrong to differ from Aristotle, or to think that he was ignorant or mistaken in anything."[^13] It was this dauntless rejection of conflicting Aristotelian doctrines, unique among the Arabic philosophers of his day who rendered slavish, unquestioning obeisance to the Aristotelian 'oracle', that set Maimonides up as the sole prototype for the Christian theologians of the thirteenth century,—in particular, for Albert the Great and his famed disciple, Thomas Aquinas,—who were thus enabled to Christianize Aristotle as the Fathers of the Church had Christianized Plato.[^14]

Before concluding this chapter with the citation of the individual scholastic philosophers who were, more or less, influenced by Maimonides, it is well to emphasize the fact that, in addition to the important position which Maimonides occupies with respect to his blazing the trail, as it were, in the field of Biblical versus Aristotelian doctrines, the influence of the great Jewish philosopher upon philosophic thought, in general, and upon the scholastic synthesis, in particular, is far greater than is usually allowed. His Biblical exegesis, for example, as expounded in the opening chapters of the GUIDE, was epoch-making, as Professor Bacher has shown.[^15] The "great exponent of reason in faith and toleration in theology,"[^16] Maimonides sought to harmonize belief and reason by admitting "side by side with the literal exegesis of the Bible, a philosophic and allegorical interpretation by means of which the texts take a sense which does not contradict certain scientific truths."[^17] It was for this characteristic trait of Maimonides' whole philosophy, also, that he has been dubbed in scholastic circles the "Jewish scholastic"; because "the principle which inspired all his philosophical activity", namely, that "there can be no contradiction between the truth which God has revealed and the finding of the human mind in science and philosophy", was actually "identical with the fundamental tenet of Scholasticism."[^18] Since reason and revelation are both divine, they must speak the same language. Hence the necessity—according to Maimonides—of a figurative interpretation of the Biblical text to harmonize it with the results of philosophical reflection. This work was indeed invaluable for the scholastic philosophers whose aim it was to make a synthesis of faith and reason, the Bible and philosophy, Revelation and science.

Then, again, it is also worthy of note to mention one of the metaphysical doctrines propounded by Maimonides which won universal favor among, and was unanimously adopted by, the great scholastics of the thirteenth century. This was Maimonides' Doctrine of Negative Attribution, or Principle of Negative Predication, as it is variously called. This doctrine teaches that the only knowledge we can have of God is from His acts, but that we cannot know His essence. Hence, God cannot be defined attributively in any positive way whatsoever, but only negatively.[^19]

A summary of the general position of Maimonides in scholastic philosophy would be incomplete without, at least, a brief citation of the various scholastic philosophers who came under the influence of the principles enunciated in the GUIDE TO THE PERPLEXED, his chief philosophical work, to a greater or lesser extent. It is becoming clearer, the results of recent research show, that the GUIDE was early known through translations.[^20] The original work was written by Maimonides in Arabic entitled DALALAT AL-ḤAÏRIN, and was completed in the year 1190. Several Hebrew versions were soon produced under the title of MOREH NEBUKIM, one of these, written in a charming, if, as some hold, inaccurate, style, composed by Judah al-Charizi.[^21] It was from the latter's Hebrew version of the Arabic original that the first Latin translation was made during the first half of the thirteenth century under the title of DUX NEUTRORUM SIVE DUBIORUM.[^22] It was this Latin version with which the scholastics were familiar and from which they gleaned their knowledge of the philosophical tenets of Maimonides.

Alexander of Hales, the great Franciscan, who died in 1245, the first scholastic, after the introduction of the works of Aristotle, to attempt to Christianize Aristotle, already shows traces of acquaintance with the GUIDE;[^23] while his contemporary, William of Auvergne, was even more deeply influenced by it.[^24] But the real influence of the GUIDE on Aristotelian Christianity begins with Albert the Great. The proof of this is the burden of the succeeding chapters of this thesis.[^25]

As regards Thomas Aquinas, "his dependance on Maimonides," says Guttmann, "is not confined to philosophic details, but, in a certain sense, may be detected in the whole of his theological system."[^26] As Emile Saisset puts it: "Maimonides is the precursor of Saint Thomas Aquinas, and the MOREH NEBUKIM announces and prepares the SUMMA THEOLOGIAE!"[^27] "If the GUIDE of the Jew and the SUMMA of the Christian bear this relation," Yellin and Abrahams, drawing from this close relationship its full implications, go as far as concluding,[^28] "then Maimonides deserves a place among the fathers of the Church"....

The Encyclopedist of the middle ages, Vincent of Beauvais, also makes use of the GUIDE in his SPECULUM MAJUS.[^29] Duns Scotus, too, knew the GUIDE, and held its teachings in esteem.[^30]

Judging from all this and the ample evidence contained in the pages of this thesis it is small wonder that scholastic authorities agree that "Maimonides exerted a <u>very important influence</u> on the scholastic philosophers, especially on Albert the Great, St. Thomas and Duns Scotus,"[^31] and that he unquestionably "exercised a <u>considerable</u> influence....on the Scholastics."[^32] The influence of Moses Maimonides upon human thought, in general, and upon scholastic philosophy, in particular, is, indeed, to quote an expression, "incalculable."[^33] "From Moses unto Moses there arose not one like Moses," is the verdict of posterity.[^34]
