While Albert differs, generally speaking, from Maimonides in his attitude towards the doctrine of angels, the former taking a more mystical, the latter a more rational, view of the matter,[^123] still, in many points of this doctrine, Albert cites Maimonides and shows that he was influenced by the latter.

Thus, for example, Albert cites the opinion of Maimonides—with which he agrees—that that which is recorded about the things done by the angels is to be understood in the light of intellectual vision, but not as though the angels had actually taken on bodily form. Albert quotes from the GUIDE in his SUMMA, saying: "Adhuc <u>RABBI MOYSES in libro, qui dicitur dux neutrorum sive dubiorum, dicit</u>, quod haec, quae leguntur ab angelis facta, non sunt per assumpta corpora facta, sed par visiones intellectuales."[^124]

Maimonides states this in his GUIDE thus: "Angels are incorporeal, and have no permanent bodily form independent of the mind (of him who perceives them), they exist entirely in prophetic vision, and depend on the action of the imaginative power."[^125] He explains this more clearly again when he says: "The term 'angel' signifies nothing but a certain action....Every appearance of an angel is part of a prophetic vision, depending on the capacity of the person that perceives it."[^126]

Albert also refers to Maimonides along with "alii Judaei" for the view that the apparitions of angels are only brought forth for the purpose of enlightening man's spirit. "Ad aliud dicendum", Albert writes in his SUMMA, "quod <u>RABBI MOYSES</u> et aliqui alii Judaei, ut Rabbi Eleazar et Rabbi Josua et Rabbi Johanan[^127] et Isaac,[^128] non referebant apparitiones angelorun nisi ad actus illuminationis, quae fiebat per eos."[^129]

When Albert treats the question whether God could command that which contradicted the edicts of natural law, he holds, with Maimonides and the other "expositores Hebraeorum", that the command dictated to the prophet Hosea to take for himself a prostitute wife (Hosea 1, 2) is to be understood as a vision that appeared for the instruction of the prophet, but not as an actual occurrence. "Dicunt enim", Albert says, "expositores Hebraeorum, ut <u>RABBI MOYSES</u> et Eleazar[^130], quod hoc, quod dictum est in Ozee (1, 2), in imaginaria visione factum est ad instructionem prophetae et non in re perfectum."[^131]

He also assents to the view propounded by Maimonides, that the notion of number is applicable to those beings who are free, or separated, from matter, only in so far as to distinguish among them between cause and effect. This Albert does so as, upon the basis of this opinion, to be able to solve the problem whether among the angels, who are, indeed, also beings separated from matter, it be possible to hold that there exists distinction based in the person. The view of Maimonides is presented briefly by Albert thus: "Adhuc <u>RABBI MOYSES in libro, qui dicitur dux neutrorum, dicit</u>, quod in separatis a materia non est numerus nisi per causam et causatum."[^132]

Maimonides, explaining this point more fully, says: "You must bear in mind that those abstract beings which are neither bodies nor forces dwelling in bodies, and which, in fact, are ideals—are altogether incapable of being represented as a plurality unless some ideals be the cause of the existence of others, and can be distinguished from each other by the specific difference that some are the efficient cause and others the effect."[^133] Again, in his brilliant summary of Aristotelian principles, he states that "Purely spiritual beings, which are neither corporeal nor forces situated in corporeal objects, cannot be counted, except when considered as causes and effects."[^134]

Albert, furthermore, accepts the view represented by Maimonides that the spheres are beings possessing souls.[^135] The consent of the "Judaeus philosophus", he says, makes him subscribe to this opinion with all the more assurance. "Hanc autem opinionem", writes Albert, "elegit sequi Algazel et aliqui alii de sapientibus Arabum, et hoc, quod hic attendimus, accipere est, quod etiam isti consentiunt in hoc, quod coeli sunt animati. In idem autem videtur penitus consentire <u>Judaeus philosophus, qui vocatur RABBI MOYSES</u>, et Judaeus, qui ante ipsum fuit in philosophia magnus, qui vocatur Isaac."[^136]

In another work Albert quotes from the GUIDE <u>word for word</u>: "<u>Item RABBI MOYSES</u>: caeli sunt animalia scilicet apprehensores creatoris. Haec autem est veritas probata etiam ex parte legis, et non secundum corpora motiva (mortua), sicut ignis et terra, sicut putant insipientes, sed sicut dixerunt philosophi, animalia obedientia creatori et laudant ipsum et cantant ei canticum et laudem sublimem. Et hac auctoritas jacet in 2 collatione (ducis) neutrorum."[^137]

This quotation is identical with the following passage of Maimonides: "Scripture supports the theory that the spheres are animate and intellectual, i. e., capable of comprehending things; that they are not, as ignorant persons believe, inanimate masses like fire and earth, but are, as the philosophers assert, endowed with life, and serve their Lord, whom they mightily praise and glorify."[^138] In the chapter preceding the one in which the above passage appears,[^139] Maimonides gives a thorough exposition of "the theory and opinion of Aristotle on these questions",[^140] saying that the latter "believes that the spheres are animated and intellectual beings, capable of fully comprehending the 'principia' of their existence."[^141] Further on he again asserts that "the spheres and the Intelligences are conscious of their actions."[^142]

With regard to the doctrine on prophecy, Albert was profoundly influenced by the opinions of Maimonides. The definition of the various kinds of divination, for example, which Albert denotes as Vision, Prophecy and Dream, is directly borrowed from, and is a compilation of, various passages of the GUIDE. Vision is properly called that which is given to one in a waking condition, says Albert, without claiming that a sensual object of perception should actually exist, and regardless of whether the perceiver experiences at the time loss of consciousness or complete impotency or not. "Dicamus, igitur", Albert writes, "quod visio proprie dicatur, quod aversis sensibilibus et introrsum retractis videtur in vigilia, quocunque modo videntur sive ex quacunque causa, sive patiatur aliquis alienationem sive non, sive etiam patiatur aliquis syncopim, sive non patiatur."[^143] Maimonides explains vision thus: "I will explain the meaning of the term....'vision'....It is something terrible and fearful which the prophet feels while awake, as is distinctly stated by Daniel....Under such circumstances the senses cease to act, and the (Active Intellect) influences the rational faculties, and through them the imaginative faculties, which become perfect and active."[^144]

Prophecy, in its proper sense, continues Albert, comes when man, his spirit having become enraptured, becomes enlightened as regards the future or otherwise occult things, at which one cannot arrive by rational investigation alone. "Prophetia enim", as he puts it, "proprie vocatur, quando homo per raptum intellectus sui illustratur de scientia futurorum vel aliorum occultorum, ad quae deviniri non potest per inquisitionem et rationem."[^145] The passage of the GUIDE upon which this is based is as follows: "Prophecy is, in truth and reality, an emanation sent forth by the Divine Being through the medium of the Active Intellect, in the first instance to man's rational faculty, and then to his imaginative faculty; it is the highest degree and greatest perfection man can attain; it consists in the most perfect development of the imaginative faculty. Prophecy is a faculty that cannot in any way be found in a person, or acquired by man, through a culture of his mental and moral faculties."[^146]

The dream, the third form of divination, has, according to Albert, as opposed to the enlightening of spirit characteristic of prophecy, only to do with the images of phantasy and occurs only in sleep, wherefore prophecy also stands higher than the dream. Therefore, Albert concludes, according to the tradition of the philosophers, the 'somewhat foretelling' dream is the "fallen-off fruit" of prophecy. "Fallen-off fruit" is, indeed, what the unripe fallen-off fruit is called, which still, in some form, gives evidence of the appearance and the taste of the fruit. "Somnium", says Albert, "est in phantasmatibus et in somno solum, propter quod prophetia nobilior est quam somnium....Somnium autem secundum phantasmata est aliquando aliquid significans et aliquando nihil, et per se numquam cum intellectu, propter quod tradiderunt philosophi, quod somnium aliquod futurum praenuntians est casus a prophetia factus. Casus enim vocatur immaturus fructus decidens, qui tamen figuram et saporem fructus etiam aliquo modo praetendit. Similiter autem est de somnio, quod futurum significat vel occultum aliquod...."[^147]

This explanation of the dream is gleaned from the following passage of Maimonides: "Part of the functions of the imaginative faculty is, as you well know, to retain impressions by the senses, to combine them, and chiefly to form images. The principal and highest function is performed when the senses are at rest and pause in their action, for then it receives, to some extent, divine inspiration in the measure that it is predisposed for this influence. This is the nature of those dreams which prove true, and also of prophecy, the difference being one of quantity, not of quality. Thus our Sages say, that dream is the sixtieth part of prophecy;[^148] and no such comparison could be made between two things of different kinds, for we cannot say the perfection of man is so many times the perfection of a horse. In BERESHIT RABBA[^149] the following saying of our Sages occurs, "Dream is the "nobelet" (the unripe fruit) of prophecy." This is an excellent comparison, for the unripe fruit ("nobelet") is really the fruit to some extent, only it has fallen from the tree before it was fully developed and ripe. In a similar manner the action of the imaginative faculty during sleep is the same as at the time when it receives a prophecy, only in the first case it is not fully developed, and has not yet reached its highest degree."[^150]

A comparison of the corresponding passage will also show that that which Albert denotes as "quod tradiderunt philosophi"—a tradition of the philosophers—is really based on the passage culled from the sayings of the Sages in BERESHIT RABBA which Maimonides cites as a corroboration of his view. In his COMMENTARY ON DANIEL Albert accredits this simile of the dream as the "casus a prophetia" to Maimonides, though, this time, he interprets it in a different sense. "Unde <u>RABBI MOYSES dicit</u>", he says expressly, "quod somnium est casus a prophetia, quia prophetia est visio in lumine intelligentiae, somnium autem manifestatio futurorum in imagine, in quo cadit et quasi occumbit lumen intelligentiae."[^151]

Also what Albert says about the natural dispositions, the spiritual as well as the ethical, which prepare the soul for clairvoyant dreams and prophecy, quite agrees with Maimonides. As regards the spiritual preparation, there are three kinds of dispositions to be distinguished. The first class includes the souls of those whose intellect is especially clear and active and who, in consequence of this nature of their intellect, are, in many ways, in accord with the higher Substances; these souls, because of the effusion of the Intelligence of these higher Substances, imbibe part of its light. Such people are by nature best fitted for thinking and for the study of the higher branches of knowledge, wherefore, also, he says, "MOYSES AEGYPTIUS" denotes them as "Wise Men."

"Quibus enim", Albert explains, "est intellectus multum agens et clarus, illi per naturam sui intellectus multum superioribus substantiis congruunt, et cum tota anima sit instrumentum intelligentiae, dicitur imprimere in eum, quotiescunque influentia sit ei sui luminis. Cum autem intelligentia, ut diximus, sit plena formis explicabilibus per motum caeli....accipitur lumen influentiae ab intellectu per modum possibilem sibi et accipitur clarum et manifestum valde, et ideo tales homines optime intelligere nati sunt et ad altissimas scientias, quae sapientiae vocantur, optime et secundum naturam dispositi. Propter quod et <u>MOYSES AEGYPTIUS tales vocavit sapientes</u>."[^152] Albert here refers to the following passage of Maimonides: "In some cases the influence of the (Active) Intellect reaches only the logical and not the imaginative faculty....this is the condition of wise men or philosophers."[^153]

The second class of spiritual preparation includes those whose fantasy and imagination are strongly developed, who are, as a consequence of this disposition, especially accessible to those principles which affect the phantasy, and who confuse, therefore, true with sophisticated wisdom and take special delight in that which is only probable. These distinguish themselves as speakers and statesmen whose calling it is to deal with probability as it presents itself in images.

> "Sunt iterum", Albert continues, "aliqui optimum habentes organum phantasiae et imaginationis....Sed nisi adsit eis lumen intelligentiae, efficientur multiplices in rationibus phantasticis propter nobilitatem imaginationis et multiplicitatem, et ideo isti confundunt sophisticia veras sapientias et innituntur probabilibus, et talia placent eis, et sunt abundantes in rhetoricis et civilibus, quae omnia probalitatem in imaginibus habent."[^154]

Maimonides' corresponding passage is: "But it happens sometimes that the influence only reaches the imaginative faculty on account of the insufficiency of the logical faculty, arising either from a natural defect, or from a neglect in training....They see a strange mixture of true and imaginary things....This is the case with statesmen, lawgivers, diviners, charmers, and men that see true dreams, or do wonderful things by strange means and secret arts, though they are not wise men."[^155]

The third class include those who, in both ways, both as regards the intellect and the imagination, possess an excellent disposition; they are the ones who, as a result of their natural disposition, have true dreams or also visions, at times also predict the clearest prophecies. "Sunt autem", Albert, concluding his exposition of the spiritual prerequisites, says, "tertii peroptimam dispositionem habentes in utrisque, tam in intellectu scilicet, quam etiam in imaginatione et organo, et illi et bona et ordinata valde habent simulacra, et praeparantur semper ad veros et certos intellectus, et illi sunt de habilitate naturae et vere somniantes et vere prae aliis visiones habentes et nonnunquam etiam clarissimas praenuntiantes prophetias."[^156]

Maimonides' corresponding version of this first class is: "If, however, the imaginative faculty is naturally in the most perfect condition, this influence may....reach both his logical and his imaginative faculties: this is the case with prophets."[^157]

Regarding the ethical disposition requisite for prophecy Albert is in complete accord with Maimonides, also citing the latter, that, as a result of the conceptions which the surrender of the exterior impressions and the sensual desires produces in the soul, the imagination and the intellect are so influenced that they are inaccessible to the influence of other causes and especially to the effusion of the higher Intelligences, wherefore, he says, according to the statement of MOYSES AEGYPTIUS, many wise men have declared that the prophets, during the time of sensual desires, of sadness, and of fear, do not prophesy nor do they predict the future according to the truth. The soul must, for quite a long time, through continuous study, through moral living and self-control, be diverted from the effects of the exterior things, and be concentrated upon the inner values; thus the images, produced by the senses and passions, disappear, and divination comes into being.

"Si tamen", explains Albert, "ex hoc supponatur, quod aliis et praecipue exterioribus et corporalibus passionibus detineantur, et ideo timor et concupiscentia venereorum et sollicitudines et dissolutiones gaudiorum vanorum et tristitiae, propterea quod animam detinent, ad se trahunt imaginationem et turbant intellectum, ne moveri possit ab influentia descendente vel alia causa movente, propter quod multi sapientum dixerunt, <u>sicut narrat MOYSES AEGYPTIUS</u>, quod tempore concupiscentiae, tristitiae et timoris prophetae non prophetizant neque vera futura denuntiant. Oportet enim per studium longum et honestes mores et ordinationes affectuum animam ab exterioribus ad interiora revocare et claudere vias, ne tumultus sensibilium, qui est in vigilia, animam extrahat ad exteriora, et si hoc jam multo tempore factum, ita quod jam evanuerint simulacra per sensus et passiones animam pertrahentes ad alia, tunc divinatio artior erit."[^158]

The passage of the GUIDE upon which the above is based is as follows:

> "A person must satisfy the following conditions before he can become a prophet....He must in addition have studied and acquired wisdom, so that his rational faculty passes from a state of potentiality to that of actuality; his intellect must be as developed and as perfect as human intellect can be; his passions pure and equally balanced; all his desires must aim at obtaining a knowledge of the hidden laws and causes that are in force in the Universe; his thoughts must be engaged in lofty matters; his attention directed to the knowledge of God, the consideration of His works, and of that which he must believe in this respect. There must be an absence of the lower desires and appetites, of the seeking after pleasure in eating, drinking, and cohabitation; and, in short, every pleasure connected with the sense of touch. (Aristotle correctly says that this sense is a disgrace to us, since we possess it only in virtue of our being animals....). It is further necessary to suppress every thought or desire for unreal power and dominion; that is to say, for victory, increase of followers, acquisition of honor, and service from the people without any ulterior object.... A man who satisfies these conditions, whilst his fully developed imagination is in action, influenced by the Active Intellect according to his mental training,—such a person will undoubtedly perceive nothing but things very extraordinary and divine, and see nothing but God and His angels. His knowledge will only include that which is real knowledge, and his thought will only be directed to such general principles as would tend to improve the social relations between man and man."[^159]

"We have described", Maimonides, finally, sums up, "three kinds of perfection: mental perfection acquired by training, perfection of the natural constitution of the imaginative faculty, and moral perfection produced by the suppression of every thought of bodily desires, and of every kind of foolish or evil ambition. These qualities are, as is well known, possessed by the wise men in different degrees, and the degrees of prophetic faculty vary in accordance with this difference. Faculties of the body are, as you know, at one time weak, wearied, and corrupted, at others in a healthy state. Imagination is certainly one of the faculties of the body. You find, therefore, that prophets are deprived of the faculty of prophesying when they mourn, are angry, or are similarly affected. Our Sages say,[^160] 'Inspiration does not come upon a prophet when he is sad or languid.'"[^161]

It will be readily seen, of course, that the "multi sapientum" mentioned by Albert refer to the above citation of Maimonides from a passage of the Sages in the Talmud.

Unmistakeable, finally, is also the agreement of Albert with Maimonides in that which the former concludes with regards to the various degrees of prophecy, here, however, adding many things, there, relinquishing others, and modifying, in his own way, what he borrowed from his predecessor. A comparison of the exposition of these degrees of prophecy,—eleven, with Maimonides, and thirteen, with Albert,—shows, in many respects, the dependance of the latter upon the views of the former.

The first and the lowest of the thirteen degrees compiled by Albert consists in a certain trust of the soul, through which man wins boldness and assurance to predict and accomplish something. "Aliquando autem", says Albert, "secundum infimum gradum non manifestat se, nisi in quadam animi confidentia, qua homo concipit quandam audaciam et certitudinem aliqua praedicendi et faciendi."[^162]

This will be seen to be based upon the following explanation of Maimonides: "The first degree of prophecy consists in the divine assistance which is given a person, and induces and encourages him to do something good and grand, e. g., to deliver a congregation of good men from the hands of evil-doers....he finds in himself the cause that moves and urges him to this deed....This faculty....aims at encouraging the person who possesses it to action."[^163]

The third degree, says Albert, is found when the effect of art is such that it expresses itself in appropriate metaphors, and these are the dreams which the wise interpret. "Tertius autem gradus huius formae est", Albert further explains, "quod movet adeo expresse, quod per metaphores conventientes exponitur, et ista sunt somnia, quae sapientes interpretantrer."[^164] Maimonides puts it thus: "The third class....the prophet sees an allegory in a dream....and in the prophetic dream itself the allegory is interpreted."[^165]

The fourth degree, holds Albert, is found when the person is not raised above the effect of the imagination, but only dreams things not metaphorical, as though the thing actually occurs. "Quartus autem gradus est", continues Albert, "qui quidem in nullo attolleretur super imaginationis actum, sed tantum somniat non metaphorica, sicut res evenit."[^166] Maimonides, explaining the fourth grade, says: "The prophet hears in a prophetic dream something clearly and distinctly, but does not see the speaker."[^167]

The fifth grade, according to Albert, occurs when the phantasy is set in motion, and it seems to him that someone is standing at his side who explains to him the things that are seen. "Quintus autem gradus est", Albert further explains, "quando jam movet quidem phantasiam et videtur ei assistere aliquis explanans, quod videtur."[^168] Maimonides, in presenting the fifth degree of prophecy, briefly describes it thus: "A person addresses the prophet in a dream."[^169]

The sixth degree is found, Albert says, when, indeed, images are produced, there shining forth from them, however, the effects of Intelligence. He writes: "Sextus autem gradus, quando movent quidem simulacra, sed elucent quidem expresse intelligentiae actus."[^170] Maimonides' version is: "An angel speaks to him in a dream."[^171]

The seventh degree occurs, Albert holds, when in the dream the Intelligences truly and clearly appear without images. He puts it thus: "Septimus autem gradus, quando adhuc in somno vere et expresse apparent intelligentiae sine simulacris."[^172] Maimonides expresses this in this wise: "In a prophetic dream it appears to the prophet as if God spoke to him."[^173]

The eighth degree comes, it is the opinion of Albert, when the heavenly form is so strong that it moves him while awake but deprived of his senses, still showing itself only in obscure images. "Octavus autem gradus est", as Albert puts it, "quando forma caelestis est adeo fortis, quod movet in vigilia aversum a sensibus et retrahentem sensus intra se, sicut superius exposuimus, sed tamen non ostendit se nisi in simulacris obscuris."[^174] Maimonides presents the eighth degree thus: "Something presents itself to the prophet in a prophetic vision; he sees allegorical figures....in a vision by daytime."[^175]

The ninth degree occurs, Albert believes, when to one, while awake and deprived of his senses, there appears a representation of the future world through expressed and easily adaptable forms. "Nonus autem gradus est", Albert continues his enumeration, "qui respondet tertio, quando scilicet vigilanti averso a sensibus occurrit imaginatio futurorum et ex forma caelesti moventi per formas expresas et de facili adaptabiles."[^176] Maimonides briefly describes the ninth degree thus: "The prophet hears words in a prophetic vision."[^177]

The tenth degree comes, according to Albert, when he has express images, in the form of instruction and teaching. As Albert explains it: "Decimus gradus est, sumptus juxta septimum, quoniam et imagines habet expressas....quod est per modum instruentis et docentis."[^178] Maimonides' version of this degree is: "The prophet sees a man that speaks to him in a prophetic vision."[^179]

The twelfth degree is found, says Albert, in the one which agrees with the first degree of prophecy, about which the philosophers have spoken, and it consists therein that by a person, while awake and with his senses averted and turned inwards, the images of future things are seen expressly, just as they occur. "Duodecimus autem est", explains Albert, "qui sumitur juxta quartum, et ille est primus gradus prophetiae, de qua philosophi sunt locuti, et ille est, quando videntur expresse imagines rerum futurarum, sicut eveniunt, in vigilia aversis et interius retractis sensibus, talis enim praenuntiat occulta et futura, et inde est dictum Hermetis, quod talis homo habet spiritum divinum."[^180]

The expression of "talis homo habet spiritum divinum"—such a man has the divine spirit—though denoted by Albert as a "dictum Hermetis"—a saying of Hermes—really originates with Maimonides who, in explaining the first degree of prophecy,—and Albert here says of the twelfth degree that "ille est primus gradus prophetiae"—writes, "This degree of influence is called 'the spirit of the Lord'; and of the person who is under that influence we say that the spirit of the Lord came upon him."[^181]

The thirteenth degree is a complement of prophecy, Albert holds, and consists in this, that the person concerned, through the influence of the Heavenly Being, has grasped the hidden Good so well that he gets from it true knowledge; this is the highest degree of the human soul. In the words of Albert: "Decimus tertius gradus complementum est prophetiae, quando bona occulta per caelestium instinctum sic praeconcipit, quod veram intelligentiam de his habet etiam absque magna sensuum aversione, et iste gradus est summus humane animae."[^182]

Here Albert is clearly thinking of the eleventh degree of Maimonides which consists in this, that the prophet "sees an angel that speaks to him in the vision ....This I hold to be—if we except Moses—the highest degree a prophet can attain."[^183]

It would be well to briefly sum up here the results attained by the researches included in this thesis. There is material, incontrovertible evidence that, first of all, Moses Maimonides occupies a very important position in scholastic philosophy by his preparing the way, through Jewish Aristotelianism, for the Christian Aristotelianism of the thirteenth century, the Golden Age of Scholasticism; secondly, that the Jewish philosopher exercised a profound, unmistakeable influence upon Albert the Great, with regard to the latter's metaphysical doctrines, his doctrine of creationism, and his doctrines on angels and prophecy, as has been amply shown in these pages.
