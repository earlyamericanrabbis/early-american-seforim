*“And I have also established My covenant with them, to give them the land of Canaan”* (Shemos 6:4)

*“Although I have created seventy nations, My covenant have I not established with any save with you alone”.* (Pesikta, Shemos 6:4)

*“Rabbi Syma’yi says: “Wherefrom in the Torah do we infer that there will be resurrection of the dead? From the verse which says: ‘And I have also established My covenant with them to give them the land of Canaan’; to you would have been the proper expression, if it referred to the generation of Moses, but the word used is, to them, meaning generations later, after the dead will be quickened. Here from we know that the Torah indicates the resurrection of the dead”.* (Yalkut Shimony, Bereishis, 176).

Moses had a hard task softening Pharaoh’s heart; but a still harder task to get the children of Israel to believe that the time of their deliverance was at hand. *“And Moses spoke so unto the children of Israel; but they hearkened not unto Moses for impatience of spirit, and for cruel bondage”*. (Shemos 6:10) But God instructed Moses and Aaron to continue their efforts, and not to be disheartened by disappointments at either front. Noth Pharaoh and the children of Israel will have to come to reason. We thus do read: “*And the Lord spoke unto Moses and unto Aaron, and gave them a charge unto the children of Israel, and unto Pharaoh, king of Egypt, to bring the children of Israel out of the land of Egypt.*” (Ibid, 13)

Now, in this Sidrah, there appears part of a chapter which teaches a great deal, but which received but scanty attention in the Agadot as well as in the numerous commentaries. Immediately succeeding the quoted paragraph, the Torah interrupts the narrative and injects twelve paragraphs of genealogy, tracing the heads of the families of the tribes of Reuben and Simeon, and in greater detail that of Levi, concluding with Aaron’s wife and his descendants till Phinehas. Then the text again reverts to the narrative which had been interrupted.

There must be a reason for it— and there is, in fact there are several reasons. The Pesikta Godolah avers, that because Jacob, before his demise, rebuked these three tribes (see Bereishis 49:3-7), they are receiving special mention here to indicate their real worthiness. (See Rashi)

The Pesikta Zutreta avers, that because they were among the Egyptians, they are here pointed out by the names of the heads of their families, to indicate that their neighbors, the Egyptians, were unworthy of being mentioned.

Rashi explains this interruption quite rationally; Because the genealogy of the tribe of Levi had to be given, so as to trace the family tree of Moses and Aaron, the order of the birth of Levi, commencing with Reuben, is here given as a matter of course.

Maimonides says: “*To have commenced the genealogy of Moses with the tribe of Levy would have been misleading, as it would make it appear that Levi was the first born.*”

Maimonides infers from these passages that the entire chain of chronology is linked in this way, that is from Noah to Moses, and from Moses to Daniel. Otherwise he sees no need here to mention the number of years of Levi, Kehath and Amrom.

But with all these reasons there remains one outstanding question: Why was the narrative interrupted with these matters of tracing the descent of Moses and Aaron immediately after the charge concerning Israel and Pharaoh was given to Moses and Aaron?

The prophet Isaiah exclaims:

“*Look unto the rock whence ye were hewn, And to the hole of the pit whence ye were digged. Look unto Abraham your father, and unto Sarah that bore you; For whom he was but one I called him, And I blessed him, And made him many*” (Yeshayahu 51:2)

In the history of Israel numbers were of no consequence. The remnant few mattered all - and that number was ever ready to continue. It is related here that God “*gave them a charge unto the children of Israel, and unto Pharaoh the king of Egypt*”. Now what the charge was “*unto Pharaoh, the king of Egypt*” is mentioned and repeated in the succeeding passages and chapters, till the liberation of Israel; but no details of the “*charge unto the children of Israel*” are mentioned. Neither do we find a single incident in which we may discover a clue to what that “*charge*” was, and when the charge was transmitted to Israel by Moses and Aaron.

In studying the purpose of God, one can discover the amount of thought he is able to carry with the capacity of his brain. But one purpose, that which relates to His special attention to the people of Israel from its very inception, no philosopher can grasp in its ordinary sense. Out of the loins of one man — Abraham — came forth generations of men and women who are called upon to make every known sacrifice in order to be prepared against a day when God would choose them, and make them His own peculiar people. An occupied territory is picked out, and thereon God’s chosen people are told to settle. From that people, settled upon that piece of ground; ensued the world’s religions, the world’s most precious thoughts, the world’s foundation for an everlasting civilization. That cannot be understood with a mere material brain, or looked upon with but natural eyes, or judged on a scale of man-made justice.

The words of the Pesikta: “*Although I have created seventy nations My covenant have I not established with any save with you alone,*” are not ordinary words to flatter a people, to strengthen its morals, to give it courage to carry on, or to make it more faithful to its calling. They are rather words which signify the tremendous responsibility that people had assumed, or that the thrust of an Invisible Hand made it conscious of a fate no other people ever experienced. The marvel of the whole phenomenon is the insignificance of the numerical strength the people of Israel was ever to muster. The still more amazing feature of the whole career of Israel is hidden in the mysteries of what the world is pleased to call as its sacred history. There are more people to day in any given district in China or India, than the total Jewish population ever was in Palestine; and, yet, the whole world, till this day, derives inspiration from every word uttered to, or by, the leaders of thought among them. The greatness of the Jewish people, however, was that it never for a moment considered quantity.

A cat and a lioness were insulting one another. Said the cat to the lioness: “What kind of a mother are you? How many young at one time can you bring”? Answered the lioness: “True, you can have half a dozen or more at one time, but they are all cats: I am proud to have one at a time, but each one is a lion.”

Of the seventy nations, outside of Israel, who can boast of an Isaiah, an Amos, a David or a Solomon?

Can the world produce a second Bible?

Every denomination, among the hundreds of millions of Christians, or among the hundreds of millions of the Islam kingdoms, trace their spiritual treasure to, and are proud to connect their souls with, the fathers of Judaism.

This is really what God charged Moses and Aaron to tell the people of Israel in Egypt. They all refused to believe Moses, and his message; they were ready to do away with his mission to Pharaoh; they were afraid; they almost had abandoned all they had inherited from their forefathers.

Moses and Aaron took a census, enumerated the heads of the families of Reuben, Simeon and Levi; they traced the descent of Moses and Aaron, and refused to enumerate any further. That was clear notice to all who desired to remain in the bondage of Egypt that they may do so. That was the charge. That was the reason why the narrative was interrupted by the genealogical record. From that minute onward Moses and Aaron devoted their task to soften the heart of Pharaoh as well as to incite him to harden it so that his punishment would fit his crime so much the closer. Moses and Aaron, moreover, revealed to Israel the secret of eternity, of a life without being at all subject to dissolution, of a career without an interruption, saying:

“*And I have established My covenant with them. To give them the land of Canaan.*”

God made known to Israel that His covenant was established not alone with the slaves of Egypt, but with their posterity as well. There will be more troubles, more hardships, more exile, but there will come a day of resurrection, of renaissance.

Wisely did Rabbi Syma’yi say: “*Herefrom we know that the Torah indicates the resurrection of the dead.*”

In almost every century Israel was considered dead; and as often he was resurrected.
