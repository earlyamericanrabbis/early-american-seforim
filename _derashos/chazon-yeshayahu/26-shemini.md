*“And Moses and Aaron went into the tent of meeting, and came out, and blessed the people; and the glory of the Lord appeared unto all the people. And there came forth fire from before the Lord, and consumed upon the altar the burnt-offering and the fat; and when the people saw it, they shouted and fell on their faces.”* (Vayikra 9:23-24)

*“Herefrom we learn to know that when Aaron alone had bestowed the priestly benediction upon the people, the Shekinah did not descend. That was painful to Moses, and he went along with Aaron and, when they both prayed, the glory of the Lord appeared unto all the people. Concerning them the Verse speaks, saying: ‘Two are better than one; because they have a good reward, for their labor’ (Ecclesiastes 4.9) and another Verse, moreover, speaks of them saying: ‘Behold, how good and pleasant it is, For brothers to dwell together in unity’ (Tehillim 133.1)*

*The fire which came forth in the days of Moses in the tent of meeting of the tabernacle continued to burn without interruption till another fire came forth in the Temple in the days of Solomon, even as it is said: ‘Now Solomon had made an end of praying, the fire came down from heaven, and consumed the burnt-offering and the sacrifices.”* (II Divrei HaYamim 7:1; Pesikta, Vayikra 9)

*“And Nadab and Abihu, the sons of Aaron, took each of them his censer, and put fire therein, and laid incense thereon, and offered strange fire before the Lord, which He had not commanded them. And there came forth fire from before the Lord, and devoured them, and they died before the Lord?”* (Vayikra 10.1-2)

Both of the brothers were of one mind; they offered strange fire; that is to say, that they failed to obtain permission to officiate at the service. It is an established rule, that although fire descended from heaven, it was nevertheless mandatory to provide fire for every offering. Thus, the act itself was not contrary to law; but they obtained no permission to perform the act. Another Midrashic authority suggests that did not honor their father, that they took no advice from Moses, and that they offered the fire not on time. Rabbi Eliezer says: *‘The sons of Aaron became guilty of a capital offence when they decided a law in the presence of Moses their master; for, whosoever renders a legal decision in the presence of his master is guilty of a capital offence’.” (Pesikta, Vayikra 10)*

Religion, in its general and accepted sense is a means for the self expression of the soul, and embraces every human action which requires intelligence and moral inclination, the heart’s devotion and the mind’s approval. Of course, sentiment, enthusiasm, ecstasy, inspiration and longing are the chief elements in the make-up of religious substance. Man’s knowledge of the hidden mysteries of life and death being limited, fancy, imagination, self-love, superstition, fanaticism, and ignorance, too, incorporated themselves as part of the substance. To separate the shell from the kernel, to purge the dross from the silver, to remove the thorns and thistles from the vast religious field, continued to occupy the best minds of all ages, with varied success. Judaism seems to have been more fortunate in this endeavor than a number of other religious denominations. It never permitted any alien element to take root in its field. Although universal in character, it ever cared more for purity than for extent, for reason, than for conquest. Like unto the ocean, it never retains dead material.

The glory of God fills the universe; let those who can see, go and worship. To urge people to come forward and admit that the sun-dawn brings on a day, and not the crowing of a cock, would be folly carried to its farthest limits.

The peoples of the world were to learn by observing how the children of Israel conduct themselves. A good example is sure to inspire others. The world did not accept Judaism in all its branches, but all civilized nations of the earth did accept the fundamental principles upon which Judaism is based. Of course, strange fires are still being offered in pagan censers; but the time draws nearer to the ultimate goal. The billion and half of pagan peoples today are yet to awaken; the yellow race, the black race, the Indian Buddhist, and the native Australian will be potent factors in the ultimate triumph of religious principles. Science, too, will take a leading part in doing away with polytheism, and other doctrines which conflict with knowledge, and deter civilization. The universal recognition of the doctrine of Monotheism is not far off.

The self-expression of religion appears most striking in the edifices erected in its honor. The clergy, as a class too, reflect upon their respective denominations. Of course, religion is not a temporal government, and cannot inflict, and should never have the power to inflict corporal punishment for violating its laws. Its self-expression cannot appear in any form of physical force. Its very foundation contradicts such compulsion; its aims are not temporal, it offers no material, economic or protective aid.

The high priest alone was not the sole source of the will of the people of Israel. Aaron’s benediction alone did not bring down the *Shekinah*. Moses too, had to give his blessings before the *Shekinah* descended on the tent of meeting. Only when both, the temporal and the spiritual, powers were united in harmony, and in brotherhood “*came forth fire from before the Lord*”. That was a heavenly fire, to indicate for all times that the high priest can never be the ruler of the people. And such is the law in Israel:

“*No high-priest shall be anointed as king*”

Man’s earthly career demands much of the human element in order to enable him to do his duty, both toward God and toward man; if he be under a constant control of the spiritual, the world will not exist; likewise, if he will be dominated by his material needs only, there will pass but few generations before mankind will turn savage. There must be an equal share of both Moses and Aaron. Then only do the words of the Psalmist ring true:

“*Behold how good and pleas and it is, For brothers to dwell together in unity.*”

Throughout the history of the Jewish democracy, as well as during the reign of Saul, David and Solomon the self-same fire which “*came forth from before the Lord*” in the days of Moses and Aaron continued to burn in the Tabernacle, until another fire like it “*came down from Heaven,*” when the Temple was completed and Solomon concluded his prayer of dedication. During all that time the priests attended to their spiritual calling only.

Of course, the Torah embraces all law, civil, criminal, private, and public, national and international, as well as ritual law; but the enforcement of these laws was not turned over to one head. The Sanhedrin had the judicial power to interpret all law; but that which belonged to the priests was never handed over to the king, and vice versa.

But the arrangement agreed upon and initiated by Moses and Aaron, did not meet with the approval of Nadab and Abihu, Aaron’s sons. “*Both of the brothers were of one mind.*” They had conspired to liquidate the plan of the two chiefs. They were extremists, perhaps fanatical; but they intended no blasphemy, and did not act contrary to law, as it was mandatory to bring ordinary fire when offering sacrifices. They wanted to point out to the people that the blessings of Moses was not needed, as well as heavenly fire was not needed. They rebelled against the idea of delegating great powers in the hands of the temporal ruler, which, according to their way of interpreting the law, of right ought to be in the hands of the spiritual ruler. They thought that they were honoring their father by demanding, or rather by attempting to take, more powers for him than Moses was conferring upon him. In this they erred doubly; they dishonored their father, and rendered a decision in law while their master Moses, was still alive, in his presence. They, moreover, brought the offering out of the appointed time. Not in the days of Moses should fire like unto this have been offered; he was more than a ruler over Israel; he was the greatest of all prophets; he was the lawgiver.

That was “*strange fire*” indeed. The fate of Israel’s long career was sealed by the sons of Aaron just then; the division of powers became a bone of contention in their day; they rebelled, and fell victims to their own folly. But so was the tragedy of Israel repeated twice on the same ground — temporal rulership and priestly domination. A conflict of this character has no place in Judaism, and should ever be considered “*strange fire.*”
