*“And thou shalt command the children of Israel, that they bring unto thee pure olive oil beaten for the light, to cause a lamp to burn continually”* *(Shemos 27:20)*.

*“It is written:*

*‘For the commandment is a lamp, and the teaching is light,*

*And reproofs of instruction are the way of life’* *(Mishlei 6:23).*

*Why was the commandment likened to a lamp? Even as from the lamp many lights might be lit, so is the master who gives public instruction; he educates many worthy disciples, and of his own knowledge nothing is diminished, in fact, it is much increased. Our teachers of blessed memory, expressed it wisely: ‘I have received instructions from my teachers; more than from them I received from my class-mates; but from my disciples more than from both combined.’”* (Midrash Agadah, Shemos, 27:20).

The Jewish people from time immemorial, contribute but a very small percentage to the illiteracy of the world. Historically they are the “people of the book.” Practical science, too, was for many generations part of the life of the Jew. Mathematics, physics, medicine, astronomy, navigation, government, finance, international trade, standardization of values, hygiene and sanitation were known to the earliest Hebrews. Education was the warp and wool of the life’s thread of the Jew from his first appearance upon the scene of earthly activities.

There is a system of education entirely Jewish, although it has connecting links with universal education. That system converges completely on the Torah. Here are a number of branches of education which the educated Jew had to know in order to understand the Torah, the Nebiyim, and the Ketubim, and the Talmud:

\(1\) Cosmogony: One cannot comprehend the opening chapters of the book of Genesis without knowing the theory of Cosmogony as propounded by the great sages from the days of Moses till the very last authority on the subject, found in the Talmud.

\(2\) Anthropology: In order to understand the theory of how mammals were created, and the difference between ordinary mammals and man, one must know and understand anthropology, in order not to fall in many errors. Talmudic and Midrashic literature supply the sources of this branch of higher Jewish learning.

\(3\) Psychology: In order to understand the soul of man, to know what the Torah means in making mention of the soul, one must know the entire field of man’s intellectual powers which emanate from his soul. This field of study is rich in material of the highest authenticity in numerous ancient, medieval, and modern works!

\(4\) Astronomy: Before one can comprehend the work of the planetary system of which this massive globe is a part; before one can conceive the dominion of the sun and the moon and the stars, one must know this science. Without it, even parts of ritual *Holakah* cannot be understood.

\(5\) Archaeology: Before one can understand Genesis 4:12, he must have a full conception of what the physical evidence mean to the Torah-believing peoples. The *Agadah* is resourceful in archaeological material.

\(6\) Ethnology: Before the educated Jew can grasp the meaning of parts of the chapters of Genesis 5:36, he must have a liberal conception of this science.

\(7\) Mythology: Before one can undertake the study of Genesis and Exodus in complete form, one must understand the several branches of idolatry, and their respective cults, before he can grasp the reason of singling out the Monotheist family as the sole bearers of God’s truth upon earth.

\(8\) Sociology: Before one can begin the study of the Torah as a moral force, he must know the whole system of this science as it existed in the days of antiquity.

\(9\) Religion: Before one can have a reasonable understanding of Exodus 1:11, the student must have a conception of the doctrines of religion which bind man to God.

\(10\) Jurisprudence: Before one can understand Exodus 11:25, and all other laws of the Torah, he must fully cover this subject.

\(11\) Aesthetic and Mechanical Arts: Before one can understand the study of Exodus 25:27, and parts of the other chapters of that book, he must make a study of the beautiful and of the utilitarian in connection with the Tabernacle, the priestly garments and holy vessel.

\(12\) Architecture: No one can begin to comprehend Exodus, from chapter 25 till the end of the book or the plan of the Tabernacle without a real knowledge of this science.

\(13\) Zoology: No man can understand any part of the chapters 1-12 of the book of Leviticus, unless he had a thorough course in this science.

\(14\) Hygiene: No man can study any part of chapters 12-16 of Leviticus without studying the science of sanitation.

\(15\) Medicine: No man can understand fully the book of Leviticus unless he made a study of the science of medicine in all its branches.

\(16\) Agriculture: No man can understand part of the Levitical laws pertaining to land, without knowing the science of cultivation of the soil.

\(17\) Military science: No one can properly understand the book of Numbers without knowing scientifically the system of military organization, marches, camp-sanitation, and offensive and defensive warfare.

\(18\) Grammar: No one can understand any part of the construction of the Bible unless he fully understands the Hebrew Grammar.

\(19\) Rhetoric: No one can grasp the meaning of most parts of the Bible without a knowledge of this system of reasoning.

\(20\) Logic: No one can make a study of the Bible and hope to achieve the knowledge of any part thereof, without having acquired a thorough knowledge of this art.

\(21\) Ethics: No one can appreciate the narrative and moral parts of the Torah without having a liberal knowledge of this social science.

\(22\) Geology: No one can understand some parts of the Torah without geological knowledge.

\(23\) Geography: No one’s knowledge of the Bible is complete, unless he knows this science.

\(24\) Geometry: No one can begin the study of some parts of the Bible, without a full knowledge of this art.

\(25\) Botany: Some parts of the Bible cannot be rightly understood without a knowledge of this science.

\(26\) Biology: This science is the first necessity for the study of the Bible.

Of course, each of these branches of arts and sciences embraces many tributary elements in education and wisdom; but they all are essential to the comprehension of the full scope of both the written and the oral Torah. A passage, a chapter, and sometimes but a word, will direct one to find the real meaning in one of these branches of human knowledge. In fact the sum total of all knowledge is segregated between the covers of the Book of Books. These branches of art and science were expounded by the Talmudists, and they became parts of the higher Jewish learning.

The interpretation of the proper terminology, and the meaning of what appears to be enigmatical and obscure, the elaboration on every one of these subjects of study are found in the entire structure of the *Talmud*, both in its section of *Halakah* and in its section of *Agadah*.

This is an age of specialists. Life is considered too short, and its demands too many, and its attractions too fascinating, which is the reason why there are but few who care to give a lifetime of study to it. Besides, the general conception of Jewish education is so inaccurate that it is no longer doubted that it all is limited to few ritual laws, and to preaching!

The people of the book, however, never considers itself completely vanquished in the field of education. The Talmudic literature, though considered antiquated by many, is still the spiritual reservoir of the people who saved the Book of Books for the world.

Up until very recently, the Rabbi’s knowledge had to cover that entire range, and know most of it, before he was permitted to go out to instruct the people. Then, whatever knowledge he imparted, he brought to light the hidden treasures of Israel’s store of wisdom, and by that light, the people journeyed on their life’s course.

During the past three or four decades, the Jewish People fell backwards. The Rabbi’s calling which is now a profession, as secular in its application to daily life as that of any other profession, is just the reverse of what was till then: he brings in the synagogues knowledge of outside sources, and their life’s path is lit up with strange lights.

Study, since the days of the Men of the Great Assembly, and since Ben Zakkai and Akiba, was not a burden, was not considered a step to economic independence, to a life of pleasure, to ease and comfort. Present-day conditions brought on a revolution and a revulsion at the same time.

Woe unto a generation which does not understand that, “*The commandment is a lamp, and the teaching is light, and reproofs of instructions are the way of life*”!

Fellow-students, or fellow Rabbis do no longer gather for the purpose of study, instruction, or interchange of ideas; they gather to protect their jobs.

Masters no longer can be interested in gaining knowledge from disciples; the disciples care not for the maximum of study, but for the maximum of pay and minimum of study.

The whole chain of spiritual links is no longer a landmark to indicate the boundary line between the body and the soul; it is kept polished so that its glittering may attract the well-to-do by some promise that everything would be all right, as long as they are willing to supply the daily needs of the pot and pan. Indeed, one must penetrate the passage:

“*And thou shalt command the children of Israel, that they bring unto thee pure olive oil, oil beaten for the light, to cause a lamp to burn continually.*”
