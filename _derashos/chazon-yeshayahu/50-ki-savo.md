*“And it shall be on the day when ye shall pass over the Jordan, unto the land which the Lord thy God giveth thee, that thou shalt set thee up great stones, and plaster them with plaster. And thou shalt write upon them all the words of this law, when thou art passed over; that thou may - est go in unto the land which the Lord thy God giveth thee, a land flowing with milk and honey, as the Lord, the God of thy fathers, hath promised thee.”* (Devarim 27:2-3)

*“The Rabbis learned: How did the people of Israel write the Torah? Rabbi Judah said: They hewed out upon stones, even as it is said: ‘And thou shalt write upon them (the great stones) all the words.’ (Ibid) Said Rabbi Simeon unto him: ‘If it be as you state, how were the nations of that time able to learn the Torah?’ Said he to him: ‘The Holy One, blessed be He! endowed them with extraordinary understanding - they commissioned their scribes to remove the plaster from the stones which were used by them as plates.’”* (Sotah 35b)

*“There were three sets of stones upon which the Torah was written: One which Moses set up in Trans-Jordania, in the land of Moab. (Devarim 1:5); one which Joshua set up in the midst of the Jordan (Yehoshua 4:9); and the third which Joshua set up in Gilgal. (Ibid. 20)”* (Sotah 35b).

Stone worship which was very common among the ancients, was forbidden under the laws of the Torah. *“Neither shalt thou set up a pillar, which the Lord thy God hateth.”* (Devarim 16:22) Nevertheless, the adoration of stone is frequently mentioned in the Bible. Jacob set up a pillar at Beth-El (Bereishis 28:18); Samuel set up the “*Eben-Ezer*” -stone of help; (I Shmuel 7:12) and the Serpent stone - *En-regel* (Ibid 14:33). Jachin and Boaz, the two columns in King Solomon’s Temple, were adornments and not supports: (I Melachim 7:15 et seq.) The erection of *Mazzebot* became one of the evils and strange worship of Israel, against which the prophet Hosea protested vehemently, saying: *‘The more goodly his land was, the more goodly, were his pillars. Their heart is divided; Now shall they bear their guilt. He will break down their altars, He will spoil their pillars.’* (Hosea 10:1-2). The downfall of the people of Israel finally came about by this strange worship of stone. *Mazzebot* were erected in Judah and Israel, and that led them astray and brought about their destruction’ (I Melachim 19.23; II Melachim 17.10). It seems that the Semitic peoples, more than any other of the ancient peoples practiced stone worship.

However, in connection with the Temple built by Solomon, the two columns, Jachin and Boaz, were not part of stone worship, nor even the least significance or reminder thereof. It is perhaps true as Herodotus states (4:44) that the temple Melk art at Tyre contained two sacred stones, but Jachin and Boaz were not either replicas or reminders thereof. The columns were made of copper, not of stone (Yirmiyahu 52:17 et seq.). Almost a whole literature was created and woven around these two columns, with the higher Bible critics at the forefront, “proving”, in their own critical manner that, as the columns were not for the support of the roof or the arches of the Temple, (they were too short to support the roof, and there were no arches above them) they were placed there as representatives of the Stone Worship?

The names Jachin and Boaz, too, were made to serve a vast number of people, each group finding a different meaning in the words. The Vulgate sets them up as two different persons. Gesenius assumes that they were the names of the donors. David Kimchy, perhaps, found the truth when he gives added strength to the translation by stating: “*May the Lord establish - Jachin His Strength - Boaz -, both, in Israel and in his Sanctuary.*” Thus we find beautiful meaning in the beauty of architecture. And, because Stone worshipers had pillars of stone in their temples to give expression to their deities, such as the Arabic Nusb or Ansub, or as the conical stone, to symbolize Astarte, Solomon employed dopper and made the two columns to express a wish and a prayer, which is perfectly permissible under the laws of Judaism, and is not all under the ban of *Hukot Akun* - imitation of idolatry.

But the custom to use stone for the purpose of storing knowledge for posterity, goes far back to the stone age. There is enough evidence now to establish the premise, if not the fact, that what the book is at the present, stone was in the long-forgotten past. Implements and utensils of the stone age tell us volumes of the extent of culture the ancients were able to attain. Inscriptions and carvings inform us of what the ancients were able to do. It therefore but natural that all branches of attainment found expression in stone. In this, the people of Israel, in a later day, were no exception. The Torah was written upon stone, not any more with stone implements but with iron or other metal.

The nations of that day only gained a superficial knowledge of the Torah. Their scribes stole the plaster, the surface of the words, impressed thereon when the soft plaster was plastered upon the stones.

And, because the people of Israel were not a hero-worshipping people, the custom of monuments to perpetuate the glory of individuals, as was among the Greeks, or the Egyptians, never spread to large proportions. In fact, the erections of *Mazzebot* on graves was due not to a desire to immortalize individuals but to prevent priests from being contaminated by stepping over a grave.

A monument in Israel is not a stone; the real greatness and fame of a leader, a scholar, a patriot, a saint or a hero in Israel is not in the cold letters, carved in stone, but in the depths of warm-hearted generations of men and women.
