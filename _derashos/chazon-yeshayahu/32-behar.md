*“Six years thou shalt sow thy field, and six years thou shalt prune thy vineyard, and gather in the produce thereof! But in the seventh year shall be a sabbath of solemn rest for the land, a sabbath unto the Lord.”* (Vayikra 25:3-4)

*“The counting of Sabbatical and Jubilee years did not begin in Eretz Yisrael till after the lapse of fourteen years from the time the land was conquered and divided and allotted to each person his share therein, even as it is said: ‘Six years thou shalt sow thy field, and six years thou shalt prune thy vineyard, which means that no obligation to fulfill this commandment rested upon the people until each one knew that it was his land, his vineyard. Consequently, immediately after the Jordan was crossed, the people were obliged to separate Halah (1/24 of the dough when a private person bakes bread, and 1/28 when a public baker bakes bread) and give it to the priest; not to eat the fruit of a tree during the first three years after planting it (fruit must be burned; liquids destroyed by burial); and not to eat of any crop before the Omer was offered on the second day of Passover, (16th day of Nisan). When the 16th day of Nisan came about the obligation, to offer the Omer, became in force. At the end of fifty days (Shebuot), the obligation to bring two breads along with the offerings of six, one year old, sheep, one ox, and two rams (one loaf the high priest ate, and the other was divided among all priests of all sections) became in force. At the end of fourteen years the obligation to separate tithes and to begin counting the years became in force. At the twenty-first year they were obliged to observe the first sabbatical year, and at the sixty-fourth year they were obliged to observe the first Jubilee year.”* (Pesikta Vayikra 25).

Primitive men had no conception of organization, either in their recreation, or in their work. It is very doubtful whether they had a real conception of time. Division of time into seconds, minutes, hours, days, weeks, months, and years, took place after recorded history was recognized as a necessity for human progress. Before the children of Israel were liberated from Egyptian bondage they were charged concerning the New Moon observance, from which the commandment took on the significance of observing holy time. Moreover, it became mandatory to observe time accurately, so as to be able to know when each of the holidays fell. Immediately after the Exodus, in Marah, the Sabbath was given to the people of Israel as a mandatory precept. Although history reveals the descendants of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob as Egyptian slaves, they had brought with them to Goshen the nucleus of an organized life, upon which the whole structure of Jewish civilization was built.

Whether or not the exactitude of time is a religious virtue, it depended upon the viewpoint of the individual analyzing it, but no one will dispute its material necessity. With the Jewish people it became part of their economic as well as of their religious life. The Torah mentions certain seasons, and emphasizes the importance of reckoning time in this Verse: *“While the earth remaineth, seedtime and harvest, and cold and heat, and summer and winter, and day and night shall not cease”* (Bereishis 8:22). The Torah, speaking to man, employs the language of man. That is, in every instance where the language appears to contradict the scientific truth, it must ever be remembered that the expression used is that of the man who uses that figure of speech. Words such as sunrise, sunset, beneath the earth, and the like is employed in the same manner as man does, whereas, in truth the sun rises not, nor does it set itself, and in truth there is nothing beneath the earth, save space. On this ground the language of the Torah must not be misunderstood; because of it misunderstanding arose, and many scholars erred in presuming that the Torah is a scientific text book, and because it is not, and does not teach in the language of science, its authority is questioned, and its divinity denied by them.

The life of a people is mirrored in its history. Its history is its conduct, its character, and its achievement. All that depends upon what form of civilization a given people is able to adopt, or is capable of creating. The material well-being of a people is of primary importance. The health, the state of mind, the growth, and the happiness of a people is dependant upon that. Organization, discipline, education, morale, and progress cannot thrive upon poverty, want, depression, or congestion.

Both body and soul must have stated periods of rest and recreation. In the human being there is a constant process of waste and repair, of work and rest. To be scientifically correct, the human body undergoes a continuous process of changes, discarding the old and replacing with new, every vital part. Nature’s forces are regulated by time; birth and death are measured by time; the courses of the stars are running on time; the whole cosmos operates on a time schedule which cannot vary even a fraction of a second. Whether time may be a subject of discussion; but whatever measure human ingenuity was able to invent, everything on, above, and beneath the earth functions on a most accurate time-table.

The importance of time, however, appears more plainly in these Verses: “*Even unto the morrow after the seventh week shall ye number fifty days”* (Vayikra 23:16); and: “*But if he have not sufficient means to get it back for himself, then that which he hath sold shall remain in the hand of him who hath bought it until the year of the Jubilee; and in the Jubilee it shall go shall go out, and he shall return unto his possession*” (Ibidum 25:28). In these two passages we discover both the religious and the material connection of time with the well-being of the community at large as well as with the very existence of the individual. Moreover, respecting thereto it must be said that counting of time, as outlined in the Torah, is not mere advice, or optional; it is mandatory; and almost the entire observance of the Torah hinges thereon. From the reading of the *Sh’ma* to the gracing of the New Moon, from the offering of the first fruits to the restoration of liberty to the slave, from the payment of hire to laborer to the redemption of the first-born, from the return of a lost article to the proscription of indebtedness, from the counting of the Omer to the selection of a vice-high-priest, from the purification of the contaminated to the dedication of the Tabernacle, from the binding of the *Tephilin* to the obligatory precepts which women observe - all depend upon time. The minutes between the appearance of the morning star and sundown, the time-limit of twilight, the sudden darkness in a valley, the lingering light upon the hill-top, the duration of the tides, the term of isolation for the leper, the period of mourning for the dead, the time of burial, the fixed days for trials (court calendars) the sessions of the *Sanhedrin*, - all were measured and the proper time fixed, so that each matter had its time and time-limit.

The standard of time set up in accordance with the sunrise and sun set in Jerusalem. The difference in time between the several continents, and the obligation to observe the commandments which are not depended on residence in Eretz Israel, was looked into and provision made for it. This difference in time created the Second Day of each three festivals - Pesah, Shebuot and Sukkot -, and the observance of the Sabbath was made obligatory in every land to its own time.

Man’s life is measured by time - three score and ten years - being the allotted span by the Psalmist and one hundred and twenty years by the Torah. Now this half century of life, which seems to have disappeared as an average for the normal person between the days of antiquity and the days of David is, to say the least, a subject well worth the while to look into.

The Torah’s time-limit is holy time; the Psalmists time-limit is profane time. Science is devoting most of its efforts to increase happiness by bringing out new comforts of life, labor-saving devices, time-saving devices, and much which might be of great benefit to the human race, on the field of hygiene, medicine, surgery and dentistry. Science, in it’s many efforts along humanitarian lines, is really the hand-maid of the Torah - a new expression of ancient or Biblical Judaism. Much of what the real scientists have accomplished is, to the thinking scholar, a true commentary upon many, otherwise inexplicable passages of the Torah. Outside of the pure ritualism in the Torah, almost all other subjects treated therein, either in health, human dealings, human production, and human life, or outside of human conduct, whether in matters affecting history, anthropology, ethnology, government, or sociology - in fact all modern thought which holds scientific value, or appears to glorify the Torah and to re-establish its worth to all mankind.

There is no reason why a man should consider himself old at seventy unless the conditions under which he is forced to live, the calling he selected, and the government he supports, form a combination to break man’s usefulness before he reaches middle-life. But the mean contributor to man’s undue temporariness upon this earth is man himself. A profane life will not produce longevity; that is a result only of a sacred life. A man at the age of a hundred hears old, if his life was shaped after the Torah, should not feel more feeble than the present-day man of fifty. A woman observing the laws of the Torah in everything pertaining to her native duties as wife and mother, should, at the age of a hundred, feel as healthy as the normal woman feels at her normal attainment of a “new life”, which is at the age of between forty-five and fifty-five. Women should contribute a larger percentage of centurial class than men.

Man’s life depends upon climate, food, environment, labor, dwelling-place, and recreation. But man’s make-up, particularly that of a civilized man, is more psychological than physical - his longevity depends more upon his state of mind, than upon any one of his physical requirements. A profane life requires only the material things which are wholesome, which are conducive to keep his body in good health. Let such life be thrown out of its course, let some real worry enter it, let some psychological disturbance affect it, and the disintegration of that life will set in with the rapidity of time. Without a sacred background, at the time of distress, man finds no back-bone to support him and he falls flat to the ground, often times in a gutter.

We ourselves create time; time is within our soul, part of our being, inseparable from such as is our shadow. That is why we are ever conscious of time, ever being reminded. “Time to go to bed”; “time for the whistle to blow”; “time to get a bite”; “time to make a change”; time to take a little vacation”; “time to attend to the teeth”; “time to retire”. The last is the worst. The longevity of inmates in a home for the aged, is not notable; the longevity of retired people is yet to be chronicled and set up as a health-and-life-giving proposition. Any interest in life, any form of activity, of recreation, will help the aged to prolong their lives.

The Torah is intended as an instrument of life for all that is good; it outlines the paths of life and death, and charges the path of life to be chosen. Its directions are clear; all one has to do is to go onward, along the marked line and not deviate from it either to the right or to the left.

For the body, the Torah prescribes everything wholesome; the meat and drink are health-giving; the social intercourse, pleasant; the neighborliness, peaceful; the labor, both productive and invigorating; the several ages between infancy and senility, well mapped out and clearly defined; and the vitality of the race as a whole, secured. This is to be accomplished by every individual, in his own selected field of endeavor; the king, the priest, the scholar, the judge, the general, the soldier, and the commoner are instructed to lead the life suitable for their respective calling and station in life. No phase of life was overlooked; every conceivable need of the body was well provided for; and every preventive measure, made plain.

To achieve all these, the entire range of natural laws had to be utilized, and every phenomenon thereof, brought out in full view; nothing was hidden; everything appears by its proper name. The whole of man’s life upon earth is to consist of holy time. There is to be an ever-present element of sacredness in whatever man does, in whatever man must do. His being upon earth is but an incident in his eternal life. The span of his earthly or material life, is therefore not to be made an object of profanity. Without a time-limit for every one of man’s actions, the span of life would be a continuous chaos. And, while he is on earth, he must not use nature’s forces as if he was determined to get out everything nature is capable to yield for his own pleasure. The field, the forest, the river, the garden, the mine, the wild life, the inhabitants of the sea, in the air, and every animate and inanimate object must not be used as if it all is to be consumed in one generation. To secure the perpetuity of nature’s treasures so that they may last for all time, so that they may give life’s needs to posterity, every individual man or woman must be conscious of the fact that his or her stay here is but temporary. The earth must have a Sabbath; the trees must have a Sabbath; the ox, the ass, too must have a Sabbath; Counting time must affect everything in nature which is life-imparting, which sustains the living.

Life’s holy time, and all that it means for the living, and for all posterity, is hidden in the thoughts behind the precepts concerning fallow of the ground, the counting of the Omer, of the Sabbatical and of the Jubilee years, as well as of the duties which are to be performed as part of the Torah’s admonitions at stated intervals. In fact, the entire life of every human being must be considered holy time.
