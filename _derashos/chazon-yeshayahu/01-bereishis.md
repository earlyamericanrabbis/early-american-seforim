*“And there was evening and there was morning, one day” (Bereishis 1:5). “Said Rabbi Judah Bar Symun: ‘Let there be evening’ is not written herein, but ‘there was evening’; herefrom is conclusive proof that there was reckoning of time before”* (Midrash Rabbi, Bereishis 3; Yalkut Shimeony, 5).

*“Rav Zutra Bar Tobiah, in the name of Rab, said: ‘The world was created by ten different categories of Divine Attributes, namely: Wisdom, Understanding, Reason, Might, Force, Righteousness, Justice, Lovingkindness and Mercy’* (Hagigah, 12a).

*“Ereb—evening—is evidently so-called because it brings on darkness, and because by its approach all forms are indistinguishable, or mixed together; but Boker—morning—is just the opposite of Ereb because one can distinguish forms. The reason that both constitute a day, is due to the Spherical movement of the earth. Therein, too, can be found the Sod—hidden meaning—of the Talmudic Derash (Rosh-ha-Shanah, 31a.; Sanhedrin, 97a) of: ‘Six thousand years is the duration of this world, and one thousand years it will be desolate’. And, after it is said: ‘And God called the light day, it is impossible that the evening too should be called “day”. The meaning of the text, accordingly, is this: there was an evening and also a morning of the first day for, had there been the evening of the first day and the succeeding morning included therein, how can the “second day” be reconciled”?* (Abraham Ibn Ezra’s Commentary, Bereishis 1:5).

*“Said Rav Ketyna: The world will last six thousand years, and one thousand years it will be desolate, even as it is said: ‘And the Lord alone shall be exalted in that day’. The sages have, moreover, said: ‘The day of the Holy One, blessed be He! is a thousand years, even as it said: ‘A Tehillim of song, for the Sabbath Day’—a day which is wholly a Sabbath. And it still more said: ‘For a thousand years in Thy sight are but as yesterday when it is past’* (Rosh Hashana 31a; Sanhedrin, 97a; Yeshayahu 20:17; Tehillim, 92:1; Ibid. 90:4).

The words of the Torah, as long as one studies them carefully, will be found to contain rational meaning. (Eruvin, 54b) Likewise: the language of the Torah is the tongue of men. (Berachos 31a). Often, the words of the Torah are like unto mountains suspended on a single hair (Sifro, Devarim 335). The texts of the Torah are dependents of one another for, that which one text seals the other opens (Midrash Tanhuma, Bamidbar, 23). These rules, in themselves, open the look of mystery and admit rational and simple meaning to otherwise very difficult and inexplicable passages. With these rules as guiding-star, even the three opening chapters should cast no fear upon the scholar—not at all, even if he be a firm believer in the divinity of the Massoritic text of the Torah to its last SYLLABLE. Maimonides says: “*In the beginning God created everything that is above and bellow... Everything was created simultaneously, but each became separated from the other in turn, so that the whole may be likened to the seed who seeds diverse seed (in one field) simultaneously. Some will sprout after the lapse of but one day, others, after two days, and still others, after three days, though the seeding of all was at one and the same time*” (Moreh Nevuchim, 2:30) This contention, although apparently contradicting the text of the Torah, too, is based upon the rule that the Torah employs the speech of human beings, and as such it is easily reconciled how it was possible for the world to be made piecemeal. It was not at all piecemeal. Everything was created at once, only the development of each came in rotation, as can be best expressed in human language. The authority for Maimonides’ contention is Rabbi Akiba, who said: “*The heaven—they and all that belongs to them; and the earth—it and all that is therein*” (Pesikta Zutreta, Baber edition of 1878, Bereishis 1; Midrash Rabba, Bereishis 1; Yalkut Shimony, Bereishis 1:3; Hagigah, 12b. q.v.). Of course, the rotation of each is given in the text, in human language, in the form and in the construction best understood by human beings.

The main difficulty in establishing the story of Creation, particularly the opening three chapters of Genesis, is one word—*Yom*—Day, which is universally translated to mean a measured period of twenty-four hours. Of course, science denies such story. The Fundamentalists, on the other hand, take the literal meaning of the word Yom as true. The ultimate loser in the controversy, is Faith.

Now, the Torah is not, and must not be made to be, a text book of either science or fable. It is a divine Book wherein one may look, and find divine truth and inspiration. Some of the greatest minds in Jewish history believed, and their belief they perpetuated through their immortal books, that the word Yom denotes a twenty-four hour period, measured by human reckoning of time. The great Nachmonides, in his commentary on the Torah (*Pirush ho-Ramban*) maintains that the word Yom must be accepted in that sense only. Maimonides, although he accepted Rabbi Akiba’s opinion that everything above and beneath was created simultaneously, was a fundamentalist. He accepted the doctrine of Rabbi Eliezer that the Creation took place on the first day of Tishri, and as the sun, and the moon, and the stars were created on the fourth day—Wednesday—, he established time-reckoning on the basis of “B.H.R.D.” upon which he built the lunar system of time-reckoning of months, the solar system of time-reckoning of years, and the system of intercallation, as well as the whole science of astronomy known to the ancients, in which the “*Sod ho-Ebur*”—hidden mystery of Intercallation. (Mishnah Torah, Book III. Treatise 8:11-18). The most remarkable of all sciences, we thus find suspended upon the single hair of the meaning of the word Yom!

But, let no scholar take this conclusion literally. Knowing the year to begin with, simple mathematical calculation, plus the science of Astronomy, will establish the same result. Reckoning time from the fourth day of Tishri, five thousand, six hundred and ninety-seven years ago will bring an identical result, without having to take into account either the rigid or elastic interpretation of the word Yom. The same result would be if time-reckoning were to be dated ten millions of years ago. The only question is that of Faith, that is, for example, would one, denying that the word Yom means a twenty-four period of time, and refusing to believe that the world is only 5697 years old, be considered an atheist, or a sinner? In short, is fundamentalism of this type an established principle in Judaism, similar to any of the thirteen articles of Faith enumerated by Maimonides?

Now, there must not be understood, in connection with the time-reckoning, that the entire question of Creation is thereby put to a scientific test. That God was anterior to all that was created, and that the Creation was ex nihilo— is the foundation of all foundations. The Aristotle claim of “eternity of matter”—*Kadmut*—has no place in Judaism. Evolution, as expounded by many scientists, in so far as the theory of “Eternal Matter” is concerned, can not be accepted even on rational ground. Nothing creates itself. The very harmony among the myriads of creatures, their very similarity in some instances, and the grouping of all, compose the best evidence that back of them all is One Intelligence.

Joseph Albo Says: “*For, whosoever believes that there was anteriormatter, but that out of that matter God created by His Will everything that is, although he is not considered an atheist, according to Maimonides, of blessed memory, for he did not include the belief in ex nihilo as a prop among the fundamentals which he enumerates, is nevertheless, a sinner*” (Ikkarim 1:1). Maimonides did not omit to enumerate the belief in creation ex nihilo as a prop, for the very good reason that this belief is the foundation of all foundations, and is included in the commandment. To believe that there is a God. But the question entirely apart from this fundamental belief is: Does time exist? Was it one of the creatures?

“*Rabbi Judah, in the name of Rab says: Ten things were created on the first day, which are: heaven, earth, formless matter, the void of space, light and darkness, air and water, and the measure of night and day*” (Hagigah 12a). Time exists by measure. It is not the measure either a solid or a liquid, or any other substance which one feels with his senses that it does exist. Time exists, and its existence depends solely because of the planetary system by which its measurement is obtained. It is, in fact, a creature of both, cycle of the planets and reasoning power of man. The day of God was, therefore, estimated to be of not less than a thousand years (Supra Par.3). That statement, of course, has no foundation of fact, but, like time itself, it exists in abstract. This, too, readily reconciles the “hidden mystery”—Sod—mentioned by Ibn Ezra (Supra Par.2) Without the revolving planets and their minute perfection and harmonious colabberation, time could not be measured, or even be known to exists even in abstract.

If, for the sake of establishing the this is that time is not an element, either in the belief or in the disbelief of creation ex nihilo the word Yom will be translated to mean a period of time, whether God’s own time, that is to say a thousand years or whether the scientists time, millions of years, what difference would it make to the honest believer and sincere student? It would not destroy a single iota of Faith; it would not undermine religion in the most Orthodox denominations; it would not destroy aught of the most firm pillars of the Sanctuary of God.

Maimonides, in his opinion that the Creation was like unto the seeds of diverse kinds planted in a field simultaneously (Supra Par.4), which in essence is the identical opinion of Rabbi Akiba, laid a foundation upon which, in so far as the text of the opening three chapters of Genesis is concerned, all may agree. Even the scientist can find no fault with that opinion. It has taken different periods of time “*for the seed to sprout*”. As for the variety of species, elements, minerals, forces, energy, beauty and everything else we find in this world, Rav Zutra Bar Tobiah explained it. The world was created with “*Wisdom, Understanding, Reason, Might, Force, Righteousness, Justice, Lovingkindness and Mercy*”.
