*“And it shall come to pass, because ye hearken to these ordinances, and keep and do them, that the Lord thy God shall keep with thee the covenant and the mercy which He swore unto thy fathers”* (Devarim 7:12).

*“Because ye hearken, etc., refers to the things which man steps on with the “sole of his foot”* (Midrash Rabba, Devarim 7).

*“And it shall come to pass, etc. — This is what the Verse speaks of (Ps. 49.6): ‘Wherefore shall I fear in the days of evil, when the iniquity of my supplanters compassed me about? Said David: ‘Lord of the Universe I have no misgivings of the major precepts of the Torah, though they are major; but I am troubled about the minor precepts which I might have foiled to observe because they are minor, whereas didst Thou charge, saying: Be heedful of a light (minor) precept as of a grave (major) one (Pirke Abot II.X). It is because of these fears, lest the minor precepts be violated because they are minor in character, that it is stated herein: And it shall come to pass, because ye hearken to these ordinances etc.”* (Midrash Tanhuma Devarim 7; Yalkut Shimony 846).

The translators, both of the word *Aekeb* and the word *Aekebye*, according to the Rabba, the Tanhuma, and the Yalkut should not have used either the word “*because*” in Devarim 7, or the words “*my supplanters*” in Tehillim 49.6. The word *Aekeb*, both in Biblical Hebrew and in Talmudic literature has the following meanings: Sole of the foot, heel, rear, ultimate outcome, goal, result success, reward, wages, fruit (of labor), consequence of, and, future, world, and follower (example of). The forced word “*because*”, to “*connect*” the sentence, was employed, like many other similar liberties the translators took to add style and room, for reading into the text that which the Hebrew text denies. The full and correct meaning of the opening sentence of this Sidrah is “*And it shall be, as a consequence of your hearkening to these laws, and observe and do them, that the Lord thy God will keep with thee, etc.*” But the Midrashic sages, because the word *Aekob* is employed, and because of all the blessings which follow in such unusual generalities looked for a more satisfying and more explicit nature of “these laws” herein spoken of. And, in a manner mostly found among the treasures of the ancients, the Rabba, the Tanhuma and the Yalkut selected a word-play and - the entire nature of the rewards in store for those who observe certain precepts is changed. This word play is in the opening sentence of this *Parshah*: *Vehayah Aekob Tishmaun* – “*And it shall come to pass if you will hearken to the precepts which one often times stops upon with his heel - and keep and do them, etc.*” A light precept, a minor ordinance, an easy statute, an unimportant custom and there are many people who consider some ordinances as of minor significance in life, some precepts of minor consequence, and some customs as entirely outmoded. How great their error! Indeed, it is wise to select a particular precept and observe it most ardently in order to manifest human limitations for no one can do so with respect to all of the precepts, ordinances, statutes, and rites; but to disregard them because they are not considered vital and of major importance, is equivalent to denying the divine origin of the greater part of the Torah.

King David feared not the transgressions of major commandments, because the very nature of the offense is a preventive against disregarding any of them. But - *Avon Aekobai*- the iniquities which I tread upon by my heels, encompassed me.

No one loses his patience with a friend to the point of striking him but many disregard the minor things which please a friend most, which come at friendships for endurance. No one becomes an abductor with that crime as his first offense. By failing to return a loaned needle, by one’s wish to employ his fellow without giving him his due hire, will lead him to more chambers in the labyrinth of social perversion. Disregard of, and contempt for the multitude of by-laws enacted by every civilized community, though they be concerning little things, will lead to the disregard of the most vital laws which support the social structure.

There is a charge given by the sages to receive every person with a cheerful countenance. It is but a remote offshoot of the fundamental law to love one’s neighbor as one’s own self. Nevertheless, a neighbor will appreciate a friendly greeting much more than the knowledge that his neighbor never deliberately breaks any of the major precepts. Even a rebuke of a friendly neighbor will be accepted graciously and do a lot of good if it be uttered in the proper language, opportune time and soft times.

One’s conduct toward his fellow-man is but a reflection of his conduct in his service of God. It is said: “*And now, Israel, what doth the Lord thy God require of thee, but to fear the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul*” (Devarim 10:13) “*Said Rabbi Haninah: Here from we learn to know that everything in pre-ordained in Heaven save alone the things which come forth as a result of the reverence and fear we must have for God. That alone is left for us to choose*” (Berachos 33b, Megillah 25a). If one fears God and reveres His commandments, he will demean himself toward his fellow-beings with like fear and reverence. Man is his own free agent; to him was left the choice in all matters affecting his conduct. He is free to reach out for regions as high as Heaven or as low as *Ghenna*. He is the maker of his own status, the carver of his own future, and the moulder of his own character. His inner tendencies are expressing themselves through his conduct, which is entirely controlled by his own will power. This will-power in man, however, is largely influenced by environment, extent of education, heredity, and belief. Culture in its finest expression, is the result of a balanced total of man’s tendencies.

*“One cannot find a dear storing food for the winter, a lion carrying a load of provisions, or a fox dispensing food”* (Kiddushin 82). They find their food everywhere, and one animal is not dependant upon another for its sustenance. Not so with mankind. No individual civilized man is entirely independent of his fellow-man. A man, though he be leading the simple style, must have a host of others to help him in his frugality. One bakes his bread, another makes shoes, and still others provide shelter, clothes, and other most simple but most necessary things, in order to enable him to live. What then may society expect in return even from such simple-living individual?

The minimum requirement as the duty of the most humble number of society, to pay in return for what he enjoys of the toil of others, is emphasized by numerous Talmudic authorities, the most outstanding of which are:

\(a\) A man’s mind must always be social (Kesubos 17)

\(b\) A man must follow the custom of the country wherein he lives (Sukkah 38)

\(c\) A man must not act different than the agreed-upon action by his fellows and the majority of men, whether he was a party to the agreement or absented himself from the discussion. (Derech Eretz Rabba 7; Shabbos 118).

\(d\) A man must not be mirthful when others are sad, asleep when others are awake, awake when others are asleep, remain standing when others are sitting down, sitting down when others are standing up. (Bava Metzia 83; Derech Eretz Raba ch. 6; Derech Eretz Zuta ch. 5)

\(e\) A man must not form rumpus organizations (Yevamos 13)

\(f\) A man’s social-mindedness must be extended to include everything society as a whole, in any given community, considers worthy, charitable, necessary for the well-being of the whole population, or for the welfare of the under privileged. (Pesahim, Berachos 30)

\(g\) Man’s arrogance makes him a leper (Megillah 29) Humility alone will make him an outstanding member of society (Vayikra Rabba 1)
(h) When in a quarrel, be first to remain silent, your better breeding will then show up (Kiddushin 71)

\(i\) Man must not insult others, if he desires that others shall not insult him. (Sotah 5)

\(j\) Man must not listen to the evil report about his fellow-man, for if he does, both he and the sycophant carry an identical burden of guilt (Eruvin 15)

\(k\) A man who is receptive to evil tales about his fellow-man deserves to be cast to the dogs (Makkos 23)

\(l\) Man is permitted to hide the truth, if it be not in the nature of testimony for the sake of making peace, or preserving it (Pirke to Rabbi Eliezaer, ch. On Peace)

\(m\) Unjustified prejudices, and hatred without cause, are as grievous and react as hard upon society as do idolatry, murder and adultery combined. (Yoma 9)

\(n\) Cultured men respect one another (Midrash Rabba, Bereishis ch. 48)
(o) Man must ever remember that there are seven kinds of thieves, and that the most dangerous among them is the one who steals the confidences of his fellow-man (Tosefta Bava Kamma ch. 7)

\(p\) Man must never appear in the light of a wicked person, neither before others, nor in his own estimation (Kesuvos 19)

\(q\) A man, under suspicion of a wrong he knows to be false must tell the one who suspects him that he accuses him wrongfully (Berachos 71)

\(r\) A man should select his company from among the cultured people in his community (Shabbos 113)

\(s\) A man’s direct road to oblivion emanates from his heart – if he lodges hatred against his fellow-man therein (Pirke Abot 2:11)

\(t\) A man should not make a habit of calling his neighbor by his first name (Ta’anis 20)

\(u\) A man, in speaking to a fellow-man must face him (Tractate Kalleh Rabbathy; Yoma 4)

\(v\) A man should not depart from a fellow-man without saying “good-bye” to him (Berachos 64)

\(w\) A man who still has two meals of his own, earned by his labor, shall not appeal to the public kitchen (Peah 8:7)

\(x\) A man’s ability to work should be appreciated by him more than if he were of noble descent and inherited a fortune on which to live. (Bereishis Rabba ch. 74)

\(y\) A man partaking of the toil of others without giving his own labor, or equivalent thereof in return, digs his own grave (Pesahim 87)

\(z\) A man shall not “corner up” even by lawful means, any commodity which sustains life (Baba Batra 8)

These examples of ethical conduct are but a few of the “little things”, which are so often disregarded, supplanted, crushed with the heel, that in numerous countries they are no longer regarded as a part of cultured life. It is against these types of transgressions that this Sidrah decries. Moreover, by observing them society brings down a shower of rewards upon the heads of all of its members, as the Verses so plainly indicate. And it was for these kinds of sins that King David feared most.
