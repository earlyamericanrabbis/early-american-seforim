*“And Moses said unto Joshua: Choose us out men, and go out first with Amalek; tomorrow I will stand on the top of the hill with the rod of God in my hand”* (Shemos 17:9)

*“Moses did not say: Choose out men for me but for us; herefrom we infer that he likened the honor of his disciple unto his own. This is as we were taught by the sages; ‘Ever cherish the honor of thy disciple as thine own’ (Pirke Abot, 4.12; Abot De Rabbi Nathan,27). So did Saul say to his lad: ‘Lest my father leave caring for the asses, and become anxious for us’ (I Samuel 9.5) Even because of this consideration for his lad he merited to become the first king of Israel. It is said: ‘Choose us out men’ says Rabbi Joshua; ‘Choose us out strong, God-fearing men, is meant thereby. ‘And go fight with Amalek ‘, says Rabbi Joshua: What Moses told Joshua was ‘Go out from beneath the cloud, and give battle to the Amalek, is meant thereby’”* (Pesikta, Shemos 17:9; Mehilta, Shemos 17; Yalkut Shimony 264).

The first step toward a rational act in the fascinating history of Israel till after the Exodus was taken by Moses, when he ordered Joshua to go out and fight Amalek. It is refreshing to note this. Theretofore, the heavenly orchestra continued to play; no material strings, no earthly instrument, no human voice was heard.

It is related: When the gold-rush was on, in the year 1849, men had left all their loved ones behind in the east, and, fighting buffalo, and Indian, ran on westward to California. Several years elapsed before some settled existence was established. The first non-gold-diggers to reach the new settlement were actors. During a performance a child was brought on the stage to take part in the cast; the child started crying. Men rushed to the stage, chased off the rest of the actors, and hailed the child’s cry with such delight that every one present had wet eyes before many moments had passed. It was the longing of their hearts burst forth; they could not restrain it any longer. It was the human note, long repressed in them, which broke out.

From Abraham till Moses nothing was left to the initiative of the children of Israel; a miracle at every step. Angels, as body-guards, The Almighty Himself as the Man of War. Their going-out and their coming-in, by day and by night, wherever and whenever their life was threatened all that was required of them was: wait and see what God will do. No revolutionary organization of Israelites in Egypt. No human attempt to free themselves. God was the Father, the Guide, the Provider and the Deliverer. Heaven was occupied as it seems to the exclusion of everything else, with the sons of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. The Egyptians were pursuing them. No, not a blow was to be struck, not a single arrow slung, not an insult cast. *“The Lord will fight for you, and ye shall hold your peace”* (Shemos 14:14)

They longed without doubt, to get at some of the pursuing task-masters; it is human to want just that; but it could not be; the Lord was in charge.

How delighted would the oppressed have been if, instead of having Pharaoh and his hosts drowned by the waves of the sea, they had been permitted to, at least, say something to the tyrants before drowning them! But such could not be. That all was work for the Lord. He alone, not even a heavenly messenger, had the privileges of wreaking vengeance upon the Egyptians.

Food became a problem; the unleavened dough they had carried along with them, in haste, did not last very long. That too, was solved by the Lord Himself. *“Behold I will cause to ruin bread from heaven for you”* (Ibidum, 16:4). They wanted meat, and God sent down quail for them; they wanted water and God provided it for them. Miracles, miracles, Miracles!

When they left the wilderness of Sin and encamped in Rephidim, things did not go so smoothly; miracles did not come the minute the people expected it, and lo, and behold! a cry came up from the camp: “*Is the Lord among us, or not*”? (Ibidum 17:7)

A full-sized man raised a cry because his father wanted him to go to work, get himself a job, and earn his own living!

“*Is the Lord among us, or not?*”

That was a very sweet sound. It was the voice of an awakened national consciousness. The child was raised upon miracles; it got accustomed to it; it would not work, since it can get more than his heart’s desire without work.

A grown-up son must not ask his mother to nurse him, merely because she did just that when he was little, helpless, unable to provide for himself.

Thus when Moses said: *“Choose us out men, and go out and fight with Amalek”* — it was the first human voice for human action the people of Israel had heard. The human being was never intended to be angelic, or to continue for all times to live under celestial rule.

Maimonides teaches: “*If one finds some of his tendencies of an extreme nature, for example, if he happens to be a miser, his remedy is to reach out for the other extreme, then he will find the middle path a good and true course to follow.*” (Mishnah Torah 1:2:2) So we trace the career of Israel at his birth as a nation. From the extreme position of being brought up on miracles, we see him confronted by the other extreme. Amalek, the dreadful warrior, offers battle, and all Moses had to offer was “*to stand on the top of the hill with the rod of God in my hand.*” No more were the people told to hold their peace; they were ordered to fight for their lives; to engage in deadly combat with a powerful enemy on an equal footing, as men, giving no quarter and expecting none.

And the people of Israel stood the test; they did not shirk their duty; they had learned discipline; they did not even protest; no record is found of even a single murmur, nor of a single utterance for a return to the Egyptian flesh-pots. Moreover they were trained soldiers, able to inflict deadly blows with the sword on a well organized and seasoned enemy.

And, they fought for the honor of God. Moses held the rod of God in his hand; to them it was the national flag; Moses held it up high, so that Israel’s soldiers might see it and gain courage. *“And it came to pass, when Moses held up his hand, that Israel prevailed; and when he let down his hand, Amalek prevailed. But Moses’ hands were heavy; and they took a stone and put it under him, and he sat thereon; and Aaron and Hur stayed up his hands, the one on the one side and the other on the other side; and his hands were steady until the going down of the sun. And Joshua discomfited Amalek and his people with the edge of the sword”*. (Ibidum 11-13)

For the first time the people of Israel, under the leadership of a skilled general, fought for the Lord; they, moreover, supported the hands of Moses, who, ever since he appeared among them continued to caution them not to raise either hand or voice but to look at God’s miracles. It was now to them, a sensation they longed for. A pent-up feeling let loose!

And, no God, their Father in Heaven looked down with favor upon His children facing death for His sake! They were not the frightened children He saw at the banks of the Red Sea, when Pharaoh overtook them. Instead they were brave soldiers marching to certain victory, to remove a great menace from their path on their onward march to the Promised Land.

And the people, for the first time, had a real human admiration for Moses. He not only put Joshua in command but he even placed him on a level with himself; and when the war was at an end, the credit of victory was given to Joshua, as the Torah testifies, saying: *“And Joshua discomfited Amalek and his people with the edge of the sword.”*

Wisely did the Talmudic interpreters of the Torah as well as the Midrashic authors comment on this historic event by saying: *“What Moses did say to Joshua was really like this: ‘Go out from beneath the cloud, and give battle to Amalek”*.

Therein lies the great secret of the course of human events throughout history; there are times when a nation must depend for everything upon Providence, and remain in peaceful inactivity; and there are times also when a nation must go out from beneath the cloud of glory, and fight for its country, for its culture and for its civilization.

The birth of the Jewish nation was as miraculous, and at the same time, as natural as the birth of a child. When the child grows up he must achieve, he must hew out his position, in a rock if necessary.
