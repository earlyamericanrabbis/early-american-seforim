*“And Joseph was brought down to Egypt” (Bereishis 39:1). “The Verse says: I drew them with cords of a man’, this refers to Joseph, of whom it is said: ‘And they drew and lifted up Joseph out of the pit’; With bands of love, that too, refers to Joseph, of whom it is said: ‘Now Israel loved Joseph’; ‘And I was to them as they that lift up a yoke’; that also refers to Joseph, over whom I lifted up enemies, of whom Potiphar’s wife was first; ‘On their jaws’ too, refers to Joseph, who brought forth from his jaws evil report, even as it is said: ‘And Joseph brought evil report of them unto their father’. But in the end, the Verse says: ‘And I fed them gently’, which refers to Joseph’s feeding of the whole world, even as it is said: ‘And Joseph was governor over the land’”* (Midrash Rabba; Yalkut Shimony, Bereishis, 39; the Verse referred to is in Hosea 11:4).

The Midrashists, in this instance, resorted to word-play, and text-parellelism. Reference is made to the change of heart undergone by the brothers, to have Joseph sold as a slave instead of murdering him; to the love his father had for him; to his suffering on account of jealousy at the hand of his enemies; to the cause of the hatred engendered in his brothers’s hearts against him, and to his ultimate triumph. For all of these a symbolic verse is quoted, saying:

*“I drew them with cords of a man,*

*With bands of love;*

*And I was to them as they that lift up the yoke*

*On their jaws,*

*And I fed them gently.”*

The great drama is thus presented in five acts, namely:

1)  Bringing him up from the pit and selling him to the Midianites;

2)  His father’s partiality towards him, in preference to the rest of the brothers, by presenting him with a coat of many colors;

3)  His enemies in Egypt;

4)  The cause of all his troubles;

5)  His elevation by means of his wisdom.

\(1\) The word “*Vayitnakhlu*”, is given in the English version as, “*And they conspired.*” Rashi, the great Peshstists, translates it, “*And they were filled with false conceit.*” The Ibn Ezra translates it “*And their hidden thought was*”; Mendelsohn translates it, “*And resolved to carry out the evil thought*”; but Jonathan ben Uziol translates it, “*Veitya’tu*”—And they held counsel together. The context favors the latter’s translation. The succeeding verse tells us, “*Come now, therefore, and let us slay him*”, which is indicative that they had deliberations before pronouncing the sentence of death. That doesn’t give the act an appearance of a conspiracy. They must have weighed the nature of his offence, and found him guilty. There is no doubt that “*their hidden thought*” had been to put him to death long before they saw him appear alone, away from his father, in their midst, and there is every reason to surmise that they tried him, that they held counsel together, and found him guilty of a capital crime. The law in Jewish jurisprudence is that if the Sanhedrin expressed an unanimous opinion about a defendant’s guilt in a capital case, no punishment at all is inflicted. Thus when Reuben who had not been at the conference, heard of their decision, he was able to save him, for they all had decided to put him to death, which, in reality, had the appearance not of a trial but of a conspiracy. They did not carry out their decision, to, first, kill him, and then cast him in a pit; they merely cast him in a pit, as Reuben directed; he thereby thought of saving him altogether. But the case was evidently re-opened, because Judah had to argue them out of bloodshed. He had to plead with them to sell him as a slave; for had he not done so, with him and Reuben against a death sentence, they would have had an argument of law, to sustain their judgement, which could no longer be considered a conspiracy. But Judah was the titular king of his brothers, and when he pleaded they hearkened to him, and Joseph was thus saved and sold as a slave instead.

\(2\) The coat of many colors was the little incident, out of which sprang forth the most dramatic episode in the annals of the Patriarchate. Indeed, “*on account of a faulty nail, a shoe was ruined; and because of the faulty shoe, a horse was killed; and because of the fallen horse, a man was slain; and because of the death of the man, a battle was lost; and because of the lost battle, an empire was conquered.*” The love of Jacob for Joseph was not misplaced, but it was provoking to the rest of his sons. Joseph was the first-born of his mother, Rachel; he had ability, and spent his childhood in the environment of the sons of Bilhah and Zilpah; they were proud of the fact and paid him due homage; he commenced to dream of sovereignty, of power, of being a leader and not a follower; he became conscious of his ability, of his favorable position with his father. Then came his dreams of “*shoaves and stars*” and his father’s interpretation thereof; that fired his imagination still more, and it gave him that “*psychological something*” which, though undefined, brings out what is known as “*atmosphere*”; his brothers saw the change in his makeup, and they watched his movements, and paid strict attention to every syllable he uttered. His dreams, coupled with his “*evil report*” about his older brothers to his father, brought on an overwhelming jealousy, a consuming flame, in the hearts of his brothers, who in order to check his ambition had to form a combination against him. The “*coat of many colors*” was the last straw—a princely garment made specially for him by his father. They, therefore, considered the matter as a personal affront which signified to them that their most sanguine fears might become a reality. Joseph would supersede both, the rightful first-born, Rueben and obtain his birthright, and their lawful leader, Judah, and establish himself as king over them. He could not do it alone, but with his father’s unbounded love as an aid, nothing would stop him; His dreams and his “air” spoke more than unmitigated, open rebellion. As a rebel they judged him, and their impulsive decision was to inflict capital punishment upon him. Better judgment, of both Reuben and Judah prevailed. Judah, however, was willing to have him sold as a slave, and as such he would have no chance to have his dreams realized.

\(3\) Blood and breeding will tell. Joseph was neither considered nor treated as a slave; his appearance, his demeanor, his speech and his ability did not produce the impression of a slave upon any one who came into contact with him. According to Talmudic lore Joseph had spent some time among the Ishmaelites, in Median, and there he had met his future wife, who was related to Potiphar, Joseph’s Egyptian master. And, even here, in Egypt, jealousy, between Osnath, the future wife of Joseph who then was on a visit with her kinsman Potiphar, and Potiphar’s wife, brought about his troubles and imprisonment.

\(4\) Joseph was by nature a ruler. The ancient world had not seen his like; he desired system in everything; he appreciated harmony in nature the well established rules and laws in everything nature’s expression had an influence. All contrasts and extremes in nature, he knew, were from one source, and formed one gigantic whole, which, to his way of thinking and belief, emanated from nature’s One God. The ancients had no conception of law of proportion, of real beauty, of harmony, of self-expression in a self-controlled manner. Everything was either subdued by force of might, or running wild. His self-control, his organized brain, his way of bringing order out of chaos, his power to subdue passion, without apparent physical restraint, his commanding personality, became the envy of his captors as it had been the object of hatred by his brothers. His was a nature none of the ancient civilized man or woman had ever seen before, and, therefore, entirely misunderstood by them. Because he was very useful, his life was spared by his Egyptian master; because of the patriarchal system that the father of the family controlled life and death in the family, the brothers had to spare his life. He was a man of destiny, and that which provoked jealousy and hatred in the hearts of others against him, was due to his natural and psychological makeup. It was neither due to his own shortcomings nor to his environment; it was due to those mysterious happenings which bring down upon earth persons who change life, who inaugurate new eras, who better conditions, and eliminate wrong. Joseph was sent to earth to assist in the birth of a nation—the people of Israel.

\(5\) The folklore current among the Jewish people with Joseph as the hero is inexhaustible. No legendary figure of the Greeks or the Romans can compete on this field with the actual, blood-and-flesh son of Jacob and Rachel. Simple folk delight in creating a myriad of mysteries around his prosaic doings in order to save the world from a terrible famine. His economic knowledge became themes for Cabalists as well as for yarn spinners. His long hair, his beautiful complexion, his style of clothes, particularly the “*coat of many colors*”, are till this day fascinating tales seasoned with fairies, angels, cherubim, seraphim, and holy creatures. The very practical Scottish people claim descent from Joseph, through Ephraim, because of their Highlander’s fancy for checkered kilts, which, they say, had its origin with Joseph. The many forms of mystic meaning to every word in connection with the dreams of the bottler and baker as well as that of Pharaoh, the prophetic significance attached to every word of his interpretations, and the unlimited stories in connection with his conduct toward his brothers, before and after they knew who he was, constitute a saga, the like of which neither a Plutarch nor a Boswell ever dared even dream of.

The very phenomenon of such beings upon earth brings home to the ordinary mind that the mysteries of life, and the course of human events, cannot be planned ahead for any considerable length of time. There are always forces at work to upset the plans of the tyrant as well as that of the liberators. Like nature itself, the course of human progress does not continue to run calmly for a long period of time. Storms must break out, often with great violence and destruction. The cause of it all is hidden in the wisdom of God. We who pass on, and leave behind us all and everything to the coming generations, can only watch with interest the effect. Joseph, like so many of the world’s very great, had wisdom bestowed upon him by the All-knowing Providence, which was the cause of his final triumph. For us, and for generations past, and yet to be born, he left the heritage of to effect.
