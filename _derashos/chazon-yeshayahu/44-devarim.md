*“These are the words which Moses spoke unto all Israel (Devarim 1:1) ... Said Rabbi Levi: Three prophets expressed themselves in the word How Moses, Isaiah and Jeremiah... Moses saw Israel in full security, and he said to them: ‘HOW can I alone bear your burden and your strife’? Isaiah saw Israel hastening toward their downfall, and he said to them: ‘HOW is the faithful city become a harlot’? (Isaiah 1); Jeremiah saw Israel in their desolation, and he said to them: ‘HOW doth the city sit solitary’?* (Midrash Rabba, Eicha 1).

*“Said Rabbi Samuel b. Evish in the name of Rab: The Holy One, Blessed is He! has a place called MYSTARIM where He weeps, even as it is said: My soul shall weep in MYSTARIM (sedret) for your pride (Yirmiyahu 13:17) ... teaching us that because Israel’s pride was taken away from him and given to the idolators, the Divine Presence weeps. Rabbi Samuel b. Nahmony said: Because the pride of the Kingdom of Heaven was taken away from them the Divine Presence weeps. Has the Holy One, Blessed is He! the attribute of sadness? Did not Rabbi Pappy say: ‘There is no sadness in the Presence of God, even as it is said: ‘Honor and majesty are before Him strength and beauty are in his sanctuary. (Tehillim 96:6)? In His Inner Sanctuary there is sadness, in His outer sanctuary, there is not. If there is no sadness in the outer sanctuary why is it written: ‘And in that day did the Lord, God of hosts, call to weeping and to lamentation, and to baldness, and to girdling of sackcloth? (Yeshayahu 22:12) That refers to the destruction of the Holy Temple, when even the ministering angels wept, even as it is said: ‘Behold, their valiant ones cry without, the ministering angels weep bitterly’ (Yeshayahu 33:7)”* (Hagigah 5b)

COURAGE

The loss of self-confidence, of courage, of hope, or of the sense of security changes the entire mental make-up of the individual as well as of the nation so affected. The book of Ecclesiastes, pronouncing everything under the own vanity of vanities, is said by some school of philosophy to be a text for, and the basis of pessimism. The Talmud avers that there was a movement to “hide” the book, but Solomon’s conclusion to “*fear God and observe His commandments*” (Koheles 8), saved the book from that fate.

The book of Job, while based upon Idealism, might be cited as an example of meliorism. His wife’s advice, “*Blaspheme God and die*”, (Iyov 3) was only an outburst of passion; whereas, the arguments of his friends sustained the idealism that the world is marching forward and ruled by the force of goodness. But neither the book of Ecclesiastes nor the book of Job approach the subject with either materialistic or pantheistic doctrines, which form the basis of pessimism. Both of these books, one attributed to Moses (Job) and the other to Solomon, are filled with the idealism of the hopeful, which Plato believed to be optimism.

Judaism does not condone pessimism; it disowns predestination; it views life in the future; it finds reward in goodness as such; it recognizes realities and shuns imitations; it promises neither a fixed stoicism nor an empirical pessimism; it disownes the Epicurean fatalism and never admitted the Buddhistic absorption; it never gave sanction to the hopelessness of evolution, nor to the recklessness of the agnostic.

The very attributes of human life, be they sad or joyful, be they full of mirth or filled with tears, be they tragic or happy, testify to its fullness, to its change, to its possibility, to its expression. Pessimism is a negative doctrine, and cannot therefor, influence Judaism, which is a positive form of a universal civilization.

Was the greatest lawgiver of all times, Moses, a pessimist, when he said: *“How can I alone bear your cumbrance and your burden and your strife?”* Was Isaiah a pessimist when he, the greatest prophetic moralist and preacher of all ages, said: *“How is the faithful city become a harlot?”* Was Jeremiah a pessimist when he, the most inspired statesman and patriot of all ages, said “*How doth the city sit solitary?*”

A people, in order to survive and prosper, must practice discipline morality and justice. Self-restraint was not one of the characteristics of the people of Israel in the days of Moses. No people is hastening to its decay faster than a people lacking in discipline. That, too, brings about autocracy which leads to tyranny. Moses was not afraid to carry the burden; he was ready, when his people made the golden calf, to sacrifice his life for them; but he was afraid that, in the days to come, if his people will not have the necessary organized self-restraint, a tyrant will rule over them and will bring about their destruction. He wanted them to take heed. He, therefore, pointed out to them his own inability to carry on unless their discipline improved. It was an admonition that he expressed.

A people, in order to maintain its continuity, must maintain a standard of moral conduct fit for their posterity. In other words, progress must be made in each generation to the end that perfection might be attained; in harmony with the teachings of the Torah.

Isaiah was the spiritual leader of his day. He saw that the men and women of his generation were backsliding. He was afraid of the future. He knew that Jerusalem must be a faithful city in order to survive. He, therefore raised his voice against the improper conduct of his people. It was a chastisement that he pronounced.

A people, in order to maintain and secure its independence, must be patriotic and well-trained in the science of government. Jeremiah, seeing the downfall of Israel’s national life, raised a lamentation. It was the woe of all the people that he expresses.

But Moses, Isaiah, and Jeremiah saw no eternal doom. Pessimism was not part of their creed. Moses, before his demise, said: *“Happy art thou, O Israel,- Who is like unto thee?, a people saved by the Lord”* (Devarim 33:29) Isaiah did not despair, for he said: *“They shall obtain gladness and joy, and sorrow and sighing shall flee away”* (Yeshayahu 35:10). Jeremiah too, finds the fountain of optimism, saying: *“In those days, and in that time, saith the Lord, the iniquity of Israel shall be sought for, and there shall be none, and the sins of Judah, and they shall not be found; for I will pardon them whom I leave as a remnant”* (Yirmiyahu 50:20).

But there is one evil which Moses, Isaiah and Jeremiah could not find a remedy for, an evil that only God, in His secret place called Mysterim, could see - the evil of the loss of self-respect; of the loss of national pride! It became par of the Galuth. Rabbi Samuel b. Iviah, or rather Rab, penetrated the veil and saw it. Rabbi Samuel b. Nahmony saw its double - the loss of pride, of the Torah, of the Kingdom of Heaven, which should be Israel’s own. The Temple was the emblem of both, the pride of national life, and the pride of the Torah! On the loss of these, no one could be optimistic, not even the heavenly hosts. For that loss both Jeremiah (13:17) and Isaiah (22:12) found no help on this earth. Heaven alone could not refrain from calling forth lamentation, weeping and sackcloth. National pride or religious pride must come from the people beneath. Despair, the forlorn hope of the pessimist breaks a people’s morale. Heaven can only weep. The remedy for such evil cannot be found anywhere, save in the courage of the gory people so afflicted.
