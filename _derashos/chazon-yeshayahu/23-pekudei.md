*“For the cloud of the Lord was upon the tabernacle by day, and there was fire therein by night, in the night of all the house of Israel, throughout all their journeys.”* (Shemos 40:38)

*“Holy Writ says: ‘For the cloud of the Lord was upon the tabernacle by day, and there was fire therein by night’; this is to teach us, that the pillar of fire was a continuation of the pillar of cloud, and that the pillar of cloud was a continuation of the pillar of fire. During all of the forty years wandering in the wilderness, Israel had no need for either the light of the sun, or the light of the moon. For, when they saw the cloud taking on the color of white, they knew it was sun dawn. Even so will it be “in the future to come”; as it is said:*

*‘Then the moon shall be confounded, and the sun ashamed; For the Lord of hosts will reign in mount Zion, and in Jerusalem”. (Isaiah 24.23)*

*And it is moreover said:*

*‘The sun shall be no more thy light by day, Neither for brightness shall the moon give light unto thee; But the Lord shall be unto thee an everlasting light, And thy God thy glory”* (Ibidum 60:19)

*“Said Rab (Rabbi Hazekiah in the name of Rabbi Jeremiah): Once upon a time a disciple of Rabbi Simeon ben-Yohayei went abroad for business, and returned with great wealth. When his classmates saw his prosperity they, too, wanted to leave the academy and go abroad. When Rabbi Simeon ben Yohayei found it out, he invited his disciples to accompany him to a certain valley, outside of their district. There he offered a prayer to God, and the valley was filled with golden dinars. Then said he to his disciples: ‘Whosoever desires it, may take of the gold as much as he wants; but, know ye that any one who takes this gold, takes only his own, part of the share of what is stored for him “in the future to come!” Forsooth, the reward for studying the Torah never reaches one in his lifetime but ‘in the future to come’, which gives a special significance to what Solomon in his wisdom said: ‘And she laugheth on the day to come’* (Mishlei 31:25; Pesikta, Shemos 40).

There is a natural limit to every desire, a natural boundary to every passion; but covetousness for wealth has neither limit nor boundary. Everyone knows that gold accompanies no one to the grave; that the most precious thing —life— gold cannot purchase; and, yet, everything in life, including life itself, is staked for the sake of gaining gold. It is not in nature; it is only natural for living creatures to hunt for food, and to look for shelter. These two things are instinctively hunted for, by the entire animal kingdom. Man’s intelligence, his excellency over the lower animals, made many discoveries in nature which enabled him to march on from strength to strength in the field of knowledge and thereby make his span of life fascinating. He was able to achieve greatness by means of his soul. His natural cravings for that which makes his life more pleasing, more comfortable, more enjoyable, prompted him to invent devices which serve his ends.

There are three things which serve the purpose of mankind, namely physical force, knowledge, and wealth.

A great philosopher once asked: “*Why do the wise frequent the doors of the rich, but the rich never call upon the wise?*” “*Because the wise know what they want, and the rich do not,*” the philosopher answered. Knowledge made the physical forces take the place of slaves for mankind; wealth, as society is constituted now, controls both knowledge and physical force.

Rabbi Simon ben Yohayei understood this. He took his disciples to a valley, and showed them wealth. But he also told them that by taking it, they were merely robbing themselves of part of the reward which is theirs, stored up for the future. He imparted to them the knowledge which they had failed to grasp, that by their study they create wealth.

The children of Israel, at their birth as a people, were given everything without having to labor in return for what they got, save to acquire wisdom, to receive instruction from Moses, from Aaron and his sons, and from the Seventy elders, and to apply the knowledge thus gained to practical use in their daily lives.

After they had seen the Tabernacle built, and as they continued to march, every phenomenon they were fortunate to perceive was explained to them. But the Torah never relates an incident, never employs a word, unless it has a permanent value for all times. From the descriptive method employed, it is apparent that special significance was attached to the condition under which the people made their journey through the wilderness, as every position, line of formation, measures of precaution, and means of protection, are minutely detailed.

The pillar of cloud to protect them from the sun, and the pillar of fire to give them light and protect them against the dangers of darkness, both were measures of great necessity. The very fact that the means of protecting them against the ever-present dangerous elements, and enemies, were of a supernatural character, is pointed out, because it serves as a warning against all of Israel’s enemies throughout the generations, showing them that, unlike any other people, a special Providence watches their goings out and their comings in, both by day and by night.

The Midrashic authors, ever on guard lest some word be misconstrued or some passage wrongly interpreted, penetrated the real spirit of both the word and the passage, the text and the context, in order not to leave the subject matter in the dark. Moreover, they delved into the significance of the construction of the narrative as well as of the narrative itself. The marvelous narrative of the book of Exodus is concluded with this passage:

“*For the cloud of the Lord was upon the tabernacle by day, and there was a fire therein by night, in the sight of all the house of Israel, throughout all their journeys.*” That they considered most interesting from an exegetical viewpoint, because the passages immediately preceding this one relates how the Tent of Meeting was protected by this very phenomenon. And, as to the cloud by day and the fire by night, that too had been described immediately after the narrative of the exodus. Why this repetition, and more particularly, why at the conclusion of this most fascinating book?

The very position of this passage prompted the authors of the Midrash to comment thereon: “*This is to teach us, that the pillar of fire was a continuation of the pillar of cloud, and that the pillar of cloud was a continuation of the pillar of fire.*”

Therein the sages summed up the full spiritual substance of the book of Exodus. The book opens with a clouded horizon over Israel; Goshen was no longer free; the children of Israel suffered every indignity morally and every hardship physically; but the “pillar of fire” - the *Amud Eish* - had not been extinguished; the tribe of Levi still remained free, and the house of learning open. That showed them the way to liberty. They then witnessed the Revelation upon Sinai, when the pillar of fire - the ever-burning flame of the Torah, was again predominating. But the continuation of the “*pillar of fire*” was the “*pillar of cloud*”, for, immediately after Sinai, a molten god lane of in the camps of Israel. A tabernacle was ordered built. Every one had a share in it, contributed something to it; there would be the “*Tent of Meeting*” and there would be lodged the Two Tables of Stone - the Torah. This completed, the people continued their march toward their goal. But the march was arranged in such manner as to give significance to every detail in connection therewith.

Now, the people wanted to know the meaning of it all. Particularly since the war with Amalek. They took every miracle as a matter of routine; their food, their clothes, their shoes, their water — everything was given to them miraculously. They had no other means of obtaining it, and custom made it all an everyday occurrence and, therefore, commonplace. But when the Tabernacle was completed, they saw something out of the ordinary routine.

“*So Moses finished the work. Then the cloud covered the Tent of Meeting, and the glory of the Lord filled the Tabernacle. And Moses was not able to enter the Tent of Meeting, because the cloud abode thereon, and the glory of the Lord filled the tabernacle. And whenever the cloud was taken up from over the Tabernacle, the children of Israel went onward, throughout all their journeys. But if the cloud was not taken up, then they journeyed not till the day that it was taken up.*” (Shemos 40:33-38)

This was a new phenomenon to them; they could not comprehend it. But the Midrashic authors explain this miraculous scene most significantly, by saying:

“*During all of the forty years’ wandering in the wilderness, Israel had no need for either the light of the sun, or the light of the moon. For, when they saw the cloud taking on the color of red, they knew that it was sun-set, and when it took on the color of white, they knew it was sun-dawn.*”

This highly philosophical commentary needs no elaboration; in time of danger, of war, of expulsion, of martyrdom, the people of Israel saw in the cloud the sun-set; in time of peace, they saw through the cloud the sun-dawn. Thus it was, and is, “*throughout all their journeys.*”
