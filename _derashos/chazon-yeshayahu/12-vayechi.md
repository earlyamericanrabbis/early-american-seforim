*“And he charged them, and said unto them (Bereishis 49:29). “What did he charge them with? He charged them, saying: Carry my coffin in the formation as you are to march and rest during your journey in the Wilderness under banners, three in the east, three in the west, three in the north and three in the south, so that all the twelve of you will surround my coffin, but every one of you personally”* (Pesikta, Bereishis 49:29; See also Yalkut Shimony, Bereishis, 161, and Rashi, Horiot 6b).

The Midrashic authors deduct from the double expression of, “*And he charged them, and said unto them*”, that what Jacob “*Said*”, the record of which exists in the Sidrah of this week, is not what he “*charged*” them with. But of the charge no record exists in the Sidrah of this week. They, therefore, came to a conclusion that tradition preserved the exact words of that charge. And the traditional record is quoted from the Pesikta Zutreta, which is better known under the title of Midrash Lekah Tov. The first part of the charge, namely, that all of his sons should surround his coffin in the manner their descendants would march and rest on the journey through the Wilderness is both, a command and a prophecy; but the second part of the charge namely, “*Let not your children carry my coffin, but every one of you personally*”, contains an element which is incomprehensible. Why not permit their children to carry the coffin? What harm did Jacob see in such possibility?

Jacob was a true prophet; in addition he was also a keen observer of human nature. There is a vast difference between the two powers; the prophets were not as a rule, worldly. But Jacob, during his troublesome life had to contend with Esau, and with Laban, and again with Esau, before and after he had worldly experience. He took territory from the Amorite, with his sword and with his bow. He had to obtain his father’s blessing by acting the part of Esau. He had many narrow escapes, both from Esau and from Laban. He struggled with an angel, with an unearthly power, and was not vanquished. He had to build and rebuild his home at frequent intervals. He suffered the tragedy of Rachel’s untimely death, and the pain of his sudden separation from Joseph. He entered the land of exile, as the Midrashic authors aver, by means of a golden chain of love–he went to Egypt to see Joseph, knowing that he brings his children into a land of strangers, where they would be afflicted. His experiences, more than that of the other patriarchs, Abraham and Isaac, fitted him for the mission he was called upon to carry on.

In the unwritten charge, which tradition preserved for us in the Midrashic literature, Jacob left the most significant advice in our history. He charged his sons with three distinct duties, each one having in it prop to support the life of the nation.

\(1\) “*Carry my coffin in the formation as you are to march and rest during your journey in the Wilderness, under banners, three in the east, three in the west, three in the north, and three in the south, so that all the twelve of you will surround my coffin*”. In this charge he transmitted to his descendants three distinct sources of strength, which would sustain them through the centuries of slavery in Egypt: (a) That they will be hence from Egypt as an organized army, able to defend themselves against any possible enemy. This was to inform them that their plight will not break their morale, and that they will have able leadership. (b) That they would be organized under individual tribal banners, which conveyed to them the very happy tidings that they would continue to have tribal independence, and live under a system of democracy, which they loved so dearly. It also informed them that the identity of every tribe will be preserved, and that every tribe will have a share in the affairs of the entire nation. (c) That all of the tribes will remain true to his teachings, of which all of them were so justly proud. They were all instructed “*To surround his coffin*”, This was to inform them that the future tribes would remain around his ideals, and that he would continue to be the centre around which all of his descendants will be concentrated:

\(2\) “*Let not your children carry my coffin*”. He pointed out to them a very grave danger, which overtook later generations, after the people of Israel had already been for centuries in the Promised Land. This warning sums up almost the whole scope of the philosophy of Israel’s sacred history. The sons, the young ones, invariably, fought for a position to carry Jacob’s coffin. Sometimes they asserted that they represented the true spirit of Jacob, and sometimes they openly desired to bury the past, to carry the coffin of Jacob and exhibit it to all, that Jacob is dead. We have the assurance of the Talmud and of the Midrash – in fact of the entire story of Israel – that Jacob is not dead, that he never died. Rehoboam, son of Solomon, is the outstanding example of what children sometimes do when they carry Jacob’s coffin. It is an ancient landmark, set up by our father Jacob, that the young must never be permitted to carry the burden of Jacob’s coffin; they must first see what their fathers are doing. *She’al Abicha Veyagadcha* – Ask thy father, and he will tell thee.

\(3\) “*But every one of you personally*.” In this last charge, Jacob summed up the entire career of his descendants as a people among the nations of the world throughout time. It is not good to do things by proxy; it is not commendable to delegate important matters, upon which depends the life of a people, to untried hands. Jacob feared lest his children will learn of the ways of the idolatrous nations, which by themselves alone are not permitted to perform any rite, or carry out any religious duty. It is, till this day, the practice of modern religious groups that laymen alone can do nothing in a religious way. They perform no service unless a priest, or clergyman leads; no church is opened for religious exercises, unless there is a minister to preside. But not so is the idea of Israel concerning such matter. Laymen are as much obligated to perform religious duties as are rabbis, preachers, or readers. Also, government is mainly in the hands of a single ruler, oftentimes a tyrant, who deprives the people of their liberties. Jacob’s conception of religion and government is entirely different; both must be democratic, even under the leadership of a high priest or a king, for both must observe the law. Religion must be free from hierarchy and government from oligarchy. Both are possible only when every individual citizen does his duty, and enjoys the full share of rights and privileges.

Jacob’s coffin, the symbol of Israel’s tradition, of his birth, of his hopes, of his struggles and of his triumphs, must not be entrusted to any one person. “*But every one of you personally*”; neither a proxy, nor a tyranny; neither a hierarchy nor a dictatorship. Government must be a democracy, and religion entirely free from the evil of blind following. Both are obligatory upon every individual, and must be made to be so by means of thorough education. The burden must be carried by all. Jacob is not dead, and his coffin is the symbol of what the living Jacob charged his descendants to do.
