*“And Esau took his wives, and his sons, and his daughters, and all the souls of his house, and his cattle, and all his beasts, and all his possessions, which he had gathered in the land of Canaan; and went into a land away from his brother Jacob”* (Bereishis 36:6).

*“Said Esau to Jacob: ‘Divide in two parts all the wealth that my father left, and I will have the choice of either part, because I am the first-born.’ ‘This wicked man has not yet filled his eyes looking upon wealth’, said Jacob to himself, and decided to divide his father’s estate this way: he made a schedule of all the portable wealth which was left by his father, and pronounced it as one share of the estate; and the cave of Machpelah and the whole of Eretz Yisrael, he pronounced as the other share. That was a hard problem for Esau; and he went to Ishmael, his uncle, to take counsel. Said Ishmael to him: ‘At the present time the Canaanite is in possession of the land and of the sepulchre; all Jacob now has is a hope to inherit the land. Take what was left by your father and Jacob will have nothing.’ Esau followed his uncle’s advice; he took schedule one, and, by agreement, which was also a written treaty of peace, he ceded to Jacob the cave of Machpelah and the whole of Eretz Yisrael. Then said Jacob to him: ‘This is my inheritance, depart from my land even from Canaan’, and he went. Thus it is said: ‘And Esau went into a land away from his brother Jacob’* (Pirke D’Rabbi Eliezer, Yalkut Shimony, Bereishis, 138).

The psychological effect *Derash* makes upon one’s mind is, to say the least, very paradoxical. Jacob, the wanderer, the nomad, the patriarch who, at frequent intervals, changed his residence, and pitched his tents in new quarters over so often, refused portable wealth, and selected as his share a territory which he knew he would not enjoy during his lifetime, and which, in the first instance, was already his by virtue of the promise made to him by Almighty, saying: *“The land whereon thou liest, to thee will I give it, and to thy seed”* (Bereishis, 23.13), and, in the second instance, he already knew that the native population claimed it, and that they outnumbered his household manifold. He, moreover, knew that, in the end he or his children could not gain a foothold in the land without the will of God. Thus, his only chance of getting or keeping what was his, could come about by means of a miracle only, why then did he pay so much for a property which was and would remain his without? Esau’s consent?

Throughout history the wandering people of Israel learned to know the value of portable wealth, and the worthlessness of immovable property. During every expulsion, the Jewish people learnt that lesson. The native population in every instance, became their heirs, in addition to their persecutors. A dread rested upon them for more than three generations after every exodus, and the mere mention of forming a settlement on land was enough to awaken suspicion in them. The rural part of the world and the Jews parted company centuries ago. They are among the urban populations of the world, and there are conspicuous in every country.

Not being considered natives of any country, they are pointed at as aliens even in countries which they had enriched, in which they had invested their all, and in which they had a history centuries old.

Strange, indeed, that even in a free country, although we find Jews owning real estate in cities, hardly three or four per cent of them ever think of buying up some landed estates, some ranches, or some wooded acres, even though the buying be very cheap, and attractive and carry along clear titles and security of modern, civilized governments.

We find Jews in the United States; for example, who own blocks in large cities, the invested capital of which could purchase a million acres of good Montana, or Washington, or Dakota farm, timber, or stock-raising land. That is really not due because the Jews have been so long away from land. On the contrary, wherever Jews are engaged as farmers, if the land is only not of the impossible variety, they have made good. But that is due to an instinctive desire to be protected in times of need.

There seems to be something in the make-up of the Jew, some psychological reason why his instinct invariably leads him to large, populated centers, and to invest his spare capital in industry, commerce, finance, or even speculative stocks and bonds rather than in safe and sane investment of land.

“*Eyn Ona’ah Le-Karkaot*”—No price is too high for land—says the Talmud, and none can claim that he paid one-sixth higher than its market value and thereby break the sale. Land was not intended to be an article of commerce; to buy it and sell it and make or give profit on it. The purchase of land, the right to own land, the title acquired in land, were considered sacred procedures. The only one who acquired right of property without the right of succession, acquisition, or conquest was a soldier who died fighting and he acquired the right to a grave on the spot where he fell dead.

There is a peculiar kind of love one feels for a particular territory. We are all provincial to a fault. We are first for our side of the street; next, for the other side of the street; next, for our precinct; next, for our town; next, for our county; next, for our state; and last, for our country. The degree of love for certain spots upon this planet can only be gauged by the degree of nearness of a native people to given territories. It is within the nature of things to love one’s country wherein one was born. The Jew is no different in that respect from any other person. But, being claimed by no country as a native, he continues, for centuries, to pour out his love and his life for something which was never his.

That is only part of the tragedy of the Jew. The greatest of all tragedies, however, is discovered in the lack of appreciation of values we trace throughout the history of the Diaspora. The Jew always was a traveller. He always was a speculator. He distributed his ready cash among the barons, whose principle it was never to pay interest, and to whose interest it was never to pay the principal, to a Jewish creditor.

Land could have been purchased in Palestine during the past four centuries, in fact since the expulsion of the Jews from Spain. It was the easiest thing for the Jews in Europe to have acquired title to extensive territories in Palestine, particularly during the whole of the 19th century. By leasing it to the Arab population, even at the lowest possible rental, the owners would have had fair return on their invested capital. The tragedy becomes still more apparent, when every opportunity during the first decade of the present century, was overlooked.

Whether we court it or not, the old world, both in theory and in practice, considers the Jew an alien. The many historic lessons should have served as a warning to European Jewry. Imagine the relief for European Jewish war sufferers, if the world’s Jewry had owned land enough to settle half a million of homeless refugees on Palestinian land! Almost \$100,000,000 was collected in America alone for the relief of the war-sufferers. With even a half of that amount, enough land could have been purchased in Palestine to establish there a powerful Jewry, which would have commanded the respect of the Arabs, and made cordial relationship possible.

The wealth of all Jewry can not balance the worth of Palestine, although its property values would not equal the property value of one square mile in lower New York, around the Wall Street section.

A people is rich, when it lives upon its own territory, even though that territory be impounded for a time in the hands of an enemy. A homeless people is ever poor. All the wealth of the Spanish Jewry—where is it now? All the wealth of the Russian Jewry—where is it now?

The same would have happened to Isaac’s wealth, had Jacob selected it instead of Eretz Yisrael. He knew that the land was his; he knew that no claim of Esau’s to it, was just; but he wanted him out of the land. The Yalkut adds still more; “*In return for his signature on a written treaty, Jacob gave to Esau the entire territory*” from Seir to Magdiol, in which the present site of Rome was included” (See Yalkut Shimony, end of paragraph quoted above). Jacob also knew well that he would have to fight for the land, when the time would come to make a permanent settlement there. He, moreover, knew that in the end only the miraculous work of God would succeed in driving the Canaanites out of the land. But the knowledge of having a land, of being able to tell his children, “*We have a country; it is our own*”; of having the hope “*to go back home*”, was worth more than all of the portable wealth Esau could carry.

And, Jacob’s deal was working in his favor the minute he consummated it; he had a right to tell Esau “*to get out*”-and he did get out. Behold the testimony of the Torah: “*And Esau went into a land away from his brother Jacob*”. That was real wealth. The greatest heritage of Israel, in all ages. It is Israel’s wealth even till this day.
