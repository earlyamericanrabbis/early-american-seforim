*“Behold, I set before you this day a blessing and a curse; the blessing, if ye shall hearken unto the commandments of the Lord your God, which I command you this day; and the curse, if ye shall not hearken unto the commandments of the Lord your God.”* (Devarim 11:26-28)

*“Out of the mouth of the Most High proceeded not evil and good”* (Eicha 3.38)

*“Said Rabbi Eliezer: Since the Holy One, blessed be He”, said this thing on Sinai, from that moment onward proceedeth not from Him Evil and Good, for evil cometh to those who do evil and good to those who do good without a pronounced decree by Him”* (Midrash Rabba, Devarim 11)

*“Ye are the children of the Lord your God; ye shall not cut yourselves, nor make any baldness between your eyes for the dead.” (Devarim 14:1) “Rabbi Judah says: ‘Ye are children of the Lord your God, as long as your behaviour is the behaviour of children.’ Rabbi Meir says: ‘Ye are children under all sorts of behaviour.’”* (Kiddushin 36a)

Judaism never could digest false philosophies, any more than the ocean can retain driftwood, or oil can absorb water. Many reasons were advanced to rationalize this phenomena which, in themselves, were reasonable enough as far as the argumentative material of the preacher or philosopher, was concerned; but it failed to alter even one iota of the current of Jewish life, or to bring about a single fundamental change in the concepts of Judaism itself. There are certain ideas which fail to impress themselves upon a Jewish mind. One of these is the belief of some many of the Jewish neighbors in predestination, preordination, and the denial of free choice of the individual. If a man be not a free agent, how can responsibility be traced for either reward or retribution? What fault can then be found with the criminal if he had been predestined at birth, or before, to court crime and was thus forced by a far superior Will to be what society finds him to be? Under such disadvantage, when the devil in the individual was planted by the order of God, what fault can there be found with the individual if he acts as he was ordained to act? Conversely, if an angel was planted in the individual, why glorify the just and give him reward?

Moreover, if this belief be accepted, how can self-realization be accomplished? Or, how can religion influence either the individual or society at large, if there is a fundamental reason for the behaviour of every individual, and that reason being that the Most High ordained the most low to be as they are? Under such condition man’s soul is not more than an instrument of God in the power of either a devil or an angel without the least power of self-expression.

Judaism disowns such fettering and shackling, and teaches that man is his own free agent, and because his tendencies are numerous, the majority of which are evil rather than good, even as the Verse says: *“And that every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually”* (Bereishis 6:5), God endowed him with a soul, well able to discern good from Evil, and instructed him to follow the good. Fatalism, insofar as man’s behaviour is concerned, is not a good defense for a bad character under this system of philosophy.

There are four things concealed from man’s knowledge which top all other mysteries under the sun: (a) life, (b) death, (c) good, (d) evil.

\(a\) How humble and lowly is the origin of each life upon earth! Yet, how mighty life becomes when properly utilized! How helpless the child is at birth! Yet how powerful he may be! All life, all nature, serves man, and man, by his will, can serve all living, and improve all nature! The wonder, the mystery of it all! This mysterious phenomenon is best evidence that all men were created in the image of God. And, since it is so, we can readily see the logic of the precept: *“And to Him shalt thou cleave”* (Devarim 10:20). Of course, it is impossible to cleave to the Shekinah - Divine Presence -, the sages have therefore interpreted this charge to mean: “*Cleave to the wise man and their disciples*” (Kesubos 111b; see also Mishnah Torah, by Maimonides, Bk. One 2:6:2). The wise man, the upright man, his teachings and environment reflect what the image of God in man really is. The words of the Psalmist, who said: *“The heavens are the heavens of the Lord; but the earth hath He given to the children of men”* (Tehillim 115:16), too, become more lucid and logical to the understanding men who see themselves as the Image of God. That is the highest form of self-realization; but the why, and the wherefore must remain a mystery.

\(b\) All life terminates in dissolution; and all nature undergoes a process of constant changes. Animate or inanimate, all are forced to obey this natural law of change. On the surface of things, the inanimate change by such slow degrees that to a given generation the onset of change is hardly perceptible, save alone the striking change brought on by the seasons of a yearly cycle. Not so the animate, particularly man! Death comes to interrupt all of man’s plans. What, then? Is death a terminal where the journey’s end appears and the rest is silence? Is it the goal of mankind? Or is it a beginning of a life without nature’s disadvantages, if such be this life upon earth? “*The highest form - the image of God - where does that go to?*” “*The ox knowest his owner, and the ass his master’s crib*” (Yeshayahu 1:3); does the man know as much about his soul? But the ox knoweth not if he is going to die, nor the ass that his life is limited to his crib and to his burden; man knoweth both, that he is going to die, when he will be forced to leave behind crib and burden alike! In the mystery of death lies the answer to the ever-present question of - what then? Even in this perplexity responsibility to one’s own faith will lead one to self-realization.

\(c\) In a serpent we beheld his entire make-up as soon as he is hatched. He can never be good, nor ever refrain from doing, what in our eyes appears to be evil. His self-expression is assured to him by nature at his birth. He follows the natural laws implanted within him as do all other inferior animate creatures. But when a child is born, who can tell what he will be when he will reach his maturity? Man is a whole world in miniature. Like unto Jacob’s dream-ladder, man is “*set up on the earth, and his head reaches heaven*” Every animal, fowl, fish or creeping thing knows by instinct what is good and what is for him. The elephant will not eat animal food, the lion will not eat straw; the worm will not become ambitious to either swim or fly, nor will the fish strive to build its nest on a tree-top. Viciousness in the panther, domesticity in the kine, leaves on the tree, or wool on the sheep remain witnesses to nature’s unfailing methods and continuity. But what of man’s inner tendencies? Nevertheless, like unto all these, he is in nature subject to the same limitations. What is good for him is the most perplexing problem which his intellect fails to solve. What to choose as good for himself is not sufficient; he must choose what is good for his neighbor too. *“See, I have set before thee this day life and good, and death, the blessing and the curse; therefore, choose life, that thou mayest live, thou and thy seed”* (Devarim 30:15-20): Life and good, life and blessing; death and evil, death and curse, - how may one find the path leading to life and good, life and blessing, and avoid the path leading to death and evil, death and curse? Even in food, the sick and the glutton know not what to choose, and often, fail even to follow a doctor’s direction. How manifold more perplexed becomes the problem to find the good for the soul! But, herefrom we learn to know since God said: “*Therefore choose life*”, that man is his own free agent, and that good and evil is not sent down from heaven but are created by wrong choice of man alone. Man’s solemn responsibility to his own soul must ever be the path leading to self-realization.

\(d\) The serpent said: “*For God doth know that in the day ye eat thereof, then your eyes shall be opened, and ye shall by as God, knowing good and evil*.” (Bereishis 3:5) How many millions of human beings, who did not, and do not know good and evil, stumbled and fell as did Eve in the Garden of Eden! Indeed, “All the labor of man is for his mouth!” (Koheles 6:7) And the question: *“For who knoweth what is good for man in his life?”* (Ibid. 12), remains unanswered to this day. Man considers flattery, tale-bearing against a neighbor, ill-begotten wealth, conquest of an unprotected people, lying himself into the high positions, pleasures of dissipation and lust - good - and most men fall on any one of these - and yet, remind less than that of a philosopher, and a soul less than that of a saint can easily trace the evil beneath all of these. Maimonides, in his Guide of the Perplexed (1:2) points to the standard of good and evil as set up by Almighty and as being an accurate measure or scale to measure or weigh, truth and falsehood. The good God spoke of and the evil God warned against, were to be evenly balanced on the other side of the scale, or in the other measure, by truth and falsehood. But man’s test of good and evil was, and is, established, by his senses - far, far away from the original standard. Who can tell whether that which man considers good is true, and that which he considers evil is false? The contrary is mostly true. Life is considered good and death, evil. Is it true—is life true, is death false? “*Wicked people are judged by the creator of evil, just people, by the creator of good.*” (Berachos 61) Measure for measure, based upon truth. Both, the wicked and the just die—is what follows.[^1]

Solomon, in his wonderful way of extracting truth from a proverb, a symbol, a figure, or a parable confused saying: “*There are three things too wonderful for me, yea four, which I know not: the way of an eagle in the heavens; the way of a serpent upon a rock; the way of a ship in the midst of the sea; and the way of a man with a young woman.*” (Mishlei 30:18-19) Aye, where one analyzes these figures of speech the confessions of Solomon’s lack of knowledge of the ways of these four penetrates far deeper than the surface of the theme conveys. Life and Death, Good and Evil are the Four he did not know. Look upward, there where the eagle finds a way to soar, for the solution to the riddle of Life; look straight upon the way of a ship in the midst of the sea, for the solution of the riddle of Death; look down upon the way of a serpent upon a rock, for the solution of the riddle of Good, and look ahead upon the way of a man with a young woman for the solution of the riddle of Evil.

The good is only what heaven considers good and true and, therefore a blessing, not what the serpent said that it was good, which really was false and, therefore, a curse. The evil is what is false and therefore, a curse which is dotted with victims along the way of a man with a young woman. Life, as intended by Almighty, is good and true, therefore, a blessing. It must be free of all impediments as is the way of an eagle soaring the heavens. Death is but a milepost in the road of immortality, as is the way of a ship in the midst of the sea- half of the journey is over, but it will reach a landing port, unless an evil wind and a false course will bring upon the curse of God and be wrecked.
