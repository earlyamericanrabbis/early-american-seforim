*“And when ye shall come into the land, and shall have planted all manner of trees for food.”* (Vayikra 19:23)

*“The Holy One, blessed by He! said to Israel: ‘Although you will find the land full of good provisions, do not remain idle, and be careful to continue to plant, as the Verse indicates: ‘And shall have planted all manner of trees for food’ (Ibidum). Even as you have found, and are now partaking of what others planted, so must you plant for your children’. Let no man say: ‘I am old, I have but a few years more to live, why should I tire myself out for others, seeing that tomorrow I might die’? Solomon said: ‘He hath made everything beautiful in its time; also He hath set the world in their heart, yet so that man cannot find out the work that God hath done, from the beginning even to the end.’ (Koheles 3:11) The princely philosopher here refers to the major thing which man cannot find out: God withhold from man the time of his death. Had it been otherwise, man would say: ‘Why build, why plant, wherefore do I stand and work for others, seeing that tomorrow I die’? Therefore was the time of his death concealed from him; let him build, if he merits, he himself enjoys it; if not, let others enjoy it and bless him for it. Once upon a time, King Hadrian, while marching with an army to face an enemy in battle, encountered an aged man who was planting fig-trees. Said Hadrian to him: ‘Thou art old, why dost tire thyself out for others?’ And the old man replied: ‘My lord, the King, I am planting; if I will merit it, I myself might yet partake of the fruit thereof, if not my children will.’ After the lapse of three years, Hadrian, on returning from the war, found the old man, standing on the same spot. The old man, remembering the conversation, filled a basket with ripe figs and presented it to the king, and said: ‘My lord, the king, receive I pray thee, this token from thy servant. I am the selfsame old man thou did encounter planting, while on the journey to give battle to thine enemy, to whom thou didst say: ‘Why art thou tiring thyself out for others?’ Behold I have already lived to eat of the fruit thereof, and these in a basket are a present for thee.’ Thereupon the king ordered an attendant to accept the fruit, and charged his chamberlain to fill the basket with gold coin as a reward for his spirit of service. When the old man returned home, he told his wife and his children of his good fortune. Among the listeners was a woman, who hastened home and ordered her husband to take choice fruits as a present to king, and receive a basket full of gold in return, as, said she, he evidently gives gold in return for fruit. The man obeyed his wife, and carried to the king choice fruits. When he reached the king’s presence, he said: ‘I hear that thou art fond of fruit; here is the best I could gather in the orchards and groves hereabouts’. ‘Take the fruit and throw it in his face’, ordered the king. With a swollen face he returned home, not realizing that what the king wanted was thrift, work, foresight and not a gatherer of fruits, to the growth of which he contributed nothing. Said the Holy One, blessed be He! ‘Learn from My ways’, even as it is said: ‘And the Lord God planted a vineyard eastward, of Eden.’* (Bereishis 2:8; Midrash Tanhuma, Vayikra 19:23.)

The only interest in life a person finds is labor. The child remains ignorant, it does not labor to acquire the knowledge of what certain, black marks on white paper mean; the youth remains ignorant, if he does not labor to find out how to do a certain thing which, aided by his labor, acquires value to others; the man remains ignorant, if he does not improve his theoretical knowledge with practical experience, by his own efforts - by his own labor.

The work of the first generation of mankind was most difficult; although their necessities were but few, and their wants still less. Before Adam obtained the first morsel of bread he had to undergo many hardships, and wait with a Job-like patience. When he was placed in the garden of Eden, although everything was ready for him, he was charged “*to till it and keep it.*” Then he was told that he should eat his bread “*in the sweat of his brow*”.

“*When thou eatest the labor of thy hands, Happy shalt thou be, and it shall be well with thee.*” (Tehillim 128:2)

Man cannot live the life of a hermit and be happy; neither can he live all by himself, without the aid, without the labor, of the group to which he is a neighbor. A man would have to spend a lifetime to grow the material, and make his own clothes, or make his implements with which to till the ground in order to raise his food.

A man rises in the morning; he finds, outside his door milk, bread, meat, groceries, and a newspaper. Let him start to figure out how many people were kept busy preparing his breakfast for him. He bathes, puts his clothes on, has his breakfast and runs to his work. How little does he contribute to the well-being of all those who worked to make his clothes, to give the comforts, the food, the home, the information he had just partaken of and was supplied with? The entire brain of civilization is at the back and call of every individual member of organized society. How dependent people are upon one another, how helpless the individual is!

And, it all is the result of labor. The mind of the thinker, the hand of the mechanic, the patience of the inventor, the hoe of the peasant, the pen of the writer, the lesson of the ecclesiast, the counsel of the jurist, the skill of the physician, the charm of the musician, the protection of the government, the art of the actor, the brush of the painter, the chisel of the sculptor, the trowel of the mason, the pick of the miner, the compass of the mariner, the throttle of the engineer, the drawings of the architect, the rod of the parent, the grind of the teacher, the key of the operator, the stick of the aviator, the yardstick of the merchant, the rule of the industrialist - all contribute to the well-being of every member of society. When a close analysis of it all is made, the vision of a gigantic machine, driven by the power of earth, sea and sky, appears upon the horizon of human activity, and ingenuity, and staggers the mind of the philosopher with what the future will serve mankind.

It all appears material; but it is the issue of the spiritual - the he-child of man’s brain, of his soul! But, in order to enjoy all that the world to-day affords, every individual must contribute something to the well-being of his fellow man. There is no room in the world to-day for either drone or the drudge. Every one must produce something, must toil, must pay in return for what he gets. And, there are a number of things needed to keep up the pace of the world’s progress. Hands alone will not work it; feet alone will not move it; wings alone will not carry it; machinery alone will not drive it; money alone will not upbuild it; government alone will not protect it; material alone will not support it. Socialism is but one phase in present-day-life; industrialism is but one more; capitalism is but a small part of the colossus; labor alone is but its engine. But, above all these, it requires a soul! The entire structure of present-day civilization might be likened to a giant; it may move mountains, dry up oceans, swallow forests, and conquer the world; but it needs life to do it with. The body of a lifeless giant is of as little value as that of a maggot.

The children of Israel were first taught how to take care of their soul; they were instructed to be holy, to emulate holiness of God. An idle person, though he be leading a holy life, is as profane as the laborer who works without God in his heart. Neither are fitting in the make-up of life, in the life that God intended human beings to live. If the holy man produces in his field of endeavor, if he reasons out the subject-matter of his calling and gives of its benefits to his fellow-beings, he is a producer; he gives a spirit to the giant; he makes life more fascinating, more in harmony with the make-up of the universe. Art, drama, music, literature, and all other forms of mental work draw inspiration from religion. Religion is a pathfinder, when directed by scholars, by men who refuse to compromise their principles for what they can get in the way of either gold, or office, or dignity, or power. One who gathers the fruit of other men’s labor, one who lets others toil but he enjoys the harvest, is a monkey wrench; if he shares ought in modern life, the machinery surely will go wrong.

Two men formed a partnership in a wood-splitting proposition. They were to split wood on one and the same yard, and share the earnings equally. Now, when one raises the axe, before landing the blow on the stump, the sound ah! escapes from his throat. When the two got their first job, says one to the other: “Now, let me see, the price is not high; no need for both to work and get through the job in but a couple of days. I’ll tell you what: You do the job, and I’ll do the saying of ‘ah’, thereby saving your throat for the Sunday choir.” There was no objection on the part of the partner; he wanted to save his voice; he delighted to be in the choir, and he readily agreed to it. But their employer heard it all, indeed, he saw it all. When the job was finished, he called in both of the working men to get their pay. He had silver coins, and here is how he divided their wages: “Here is the money”, said he, dropping each coin separately on his desk, and pointing to the one who actually did the splitting to take it; “as for you”, he said to the other; listen well to the sound of the coins, conserve the sweet voice thereof; it is yours; you earned it; you contributed voice to the job.”

Such should be the wages of the agitator, of the one who lives on labor of another, of the social leech, of the chronic disturber of social peace, of the fellow who uses the laborer as his tool, and contributes voice to the job.

Neither is idleness conducive to prolong life. A retired person so quickly loses interest in life that he dies; so does an old man in a home for the aged. Cultivating flowers, planting potatoes, raising chickens, and kind of work gives an old man an interest in life, and he lives on. It is very unwise to cease activities because one can afford to live in idleness, or to force idleness upon one because he no longer can keep pace with the swiftly-moving column of progress and earn enough of the present-day wages to live on. Some form of work should be given to the aged.

The old man who planted fig-trees deserved the reward; and the man who merely gathered fruit of other men’s labors, deserved the punishment. No day’s labor was ever in vain; and no day’s idleness can ever be counted in among the days of one’s lifetime. Wisely did the Midrashic sages comment on the Verse; “*The Holy One, blessed be He! said to Israel: ‘Although you will find the land full of good provisions, do not remain idle, and be careful to continue top plant.’”*.
