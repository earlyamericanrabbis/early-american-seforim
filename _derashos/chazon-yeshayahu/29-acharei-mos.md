*“And no that letteth go the goat for Azazel shall wash his clothes and bathe his flesh in water, and afterward he may come in to the camp.”* (Vayikra 16:26)

*Rabbi Joshua of Shiknin, in the name of Rabbi Levi, says: “The Evil One contradicts four commandments, all of which are under the category of statutes, namely: (1) a brother’s wife; (2) garment of mingled stuff; (3) the Azazel goat; (4) the red heifer.*

*(1) It is said: ‘Thou shalt not uncover the nakedness of thy brother’s wife, ‘ and it is again said: ‘her husband’s brother shall go in unto her and take her to him to wife;’ (Vayikra 18:19; Devarim 25:5); while the brother is alive, she is forbidden to him, but if the brother dies childless, he may take her to wife; concerning this commandment it is said: ‘Ye therefore shall keep my commandments.’*

*(2) It is said: ‘Thou shalt not wear a mingled stuff, wool and linen together,’ (Devarim 22:10) yet, in a garment of fringes, one may use wool and linen, and concerning this commandment, too, it is said: ‘Ye shall keep My statutes.’ (Vayikra 19:19)*

*(3) It is said: ‘And he that letteth go the goat shall wash his others’, (Ibidum 16:26) whereas he, by his act, atones for others, and of this too, it is said: ‘And it shall be a statute for ever unto you. ‘(Ibid. — 29)*

*(4) It is said: ‘Of all that have to do with the offering of the red heifer, that they all become unclean (See Bamidbar 19:7—12), whereas the ashes of the heifer itself purifies the contaminated and of it too, it is said: ‘This is the statute of the law which the Lord hath commanded’.”* (Ibidum —2, Yalkut Shimony Vayikra 577)

The Torah employs the language of man, as it was intended that man should understand it. The several categories of commandments in the Torah, were not all given at one and the same time. The celebration of Revelation is not called “*the day of giving of the Torah*”, but the “season” thereof. Moreover, not all of the commandments were given on that season, as there were eight different seasons during which time some of the commandments were given to Adam, Noah, Abraham, Jacob, Judah, in Egypt, and in Marah... These were commandments concerning belief in God, not to practice idolatry, not to kill, not to commit incest, not to eat flesh of a live beast, not to rob, to obey the laws of property rights, circumcision, not to eat the sinew that shrank, to marry a deceased, childless brother’s wife, to consecrate the New Moon, to slaughter the Paschal Lamb, redemption of the first-born, and the Sabbath. The eighth season was on Sinai (See Mateh Moshe 690)[^1] The Torah contains all of the laws necessary to shape the conduct of the human race up on earth— a code of life for the living, rules affecting the daily intercourse between men. In addition thereto there are some laws which have nothing whatever to do, at the first glance, on their character, with either life or the living; they are statutes between men and his God. They are, fundamentally, beyond the comprehension of every-day human reasoning.

These statutes embrace quite a large number of the commandments, affecting mainly Palestinian life, some parts of the service in the Temple, and but very few affecting the daily life’s routine of the faithful Israelites. And yet, the very last one of all of the statutes contains the finest sentiments back of it, when religious faithfulness to God and His ways are taken under a spiritual scrutiny.

It is a simple matter for the human mind to reason out anything at all which is familiar to it. For example; the laws concerning crime. And, yet, that is a far greater mystery to the thinking mind than the ashes of the red heifer, or the agent of the Azazel goat, or the Mingled-stuff garment, or a sister-in-law of a deceased brother. Criminology is one of the greatest problems of a civilized state; psychology fails to solve it; love, or fear, fail to unravel it; punishment and reward do not fathom it. Nevertheless no one’s mind will remain as perplexed when speaking about crime as when these four subjects are brought up. We take crime as a daily phenomenon of social existence; whereas we marvel at the significance of apparent contradictions, or at incomprehensible mandates. The soul, however, refuses to remain indifferent to anything which has to do with the mysteries of either life or death. The why and wherefore of things ever comes back to disturb dormant powers in the human make-up. It seems at times that we do know, or that once upon a time we did know, a reason for everything. Have we forgotten it? When did we learn it? Have we ever really had the knowledge thereof, or is it only imaginary? Perhaps a former existence troubles some neck in our recollection, which cannot be the brain, for the brain we now have never before existed - what then is it that comes to disturb us? Is it a mystery again, awakening in us that curiosity which stops at no obstacle, which fears no darkness, and which knows no boundary-line?

The statutes of the Torah have no common-sense reasons for having been added to the duties of the believer; it is enough that we know it as a God-given commandment. That was the attitude of every great soul which recognized therein the Will of the Creator, which believed that the Torah is divine. The *Chokim* - statutes - can never be readily comprehended as the *Mishpatim* - laws. Evidently there was a divine purpose in making them so. The *Peshat* of the Torah reveals to us nothing further than that.

Now the Torah speaks to us in four different languages; (1) *Peshat* - accepted meaning reasonably and readily understood; (2) *Derash* - not meaning given to the text by means of elucidating it, of taking it together with the context, of explaining it by means of the hermeneutics, by harmonizing thereon; (3) *Remez* - new meaning read into the text by means of added significance to the letters, their numerical and geometrical significance, their symbolic lessons, or their spiritual exposition based upon what degree of knowledge the subjects treat; (4) *Sod* - new meaning revealed by means of Kabalistic, or secret, mystical deduction and induction. In all, it is said, that the Torah might be sermonized upon in seventy different ways; that each word has seventy meanings on its face! And, it is a certainty, that there were and there probably are, scholars who can, by different methods and grades of knowledge interpret the Torah in seventy different ways. However, the P.R.D.S.— *Peshat*, *Remez*, *Dorash*, and *Sod*— were accepted as authentic ways of expounding the Torah.

For the purpose of establishing the laws - *Mishpatim*- of the Torah, no other means than the *Peshat*, and sometime the *Derash* is ever employed. *Remez* and Sod have no place in the several codes. No law, not even affecting ritual, was established upon a Kabalistic ideal; the ordinary accepted meaning of the Torah ever prevailed among the Rabbis of all ages. Of course, there were, and there are, Kabbalists who practice certain additional rites, or who observe certain additional rules and customs which are not based upon any save that of Kabbalistic ground; but they are doing so as a matter of extreme piety, and not because of legal or moral obligations as enunciated by the authors of the several codes.

There is, however, a generally accepted doctrine that, although many of the commandments are not practiced in the diaspora, as they are solely practiced in Palestine, their significance, nevertheless, are not obsolete even in diaspora. The same is true concerning all laws which had been practiced during the Palestinian era by order of the Sanhedrin; the laws are no longer in force, and cannot be until the re-establishment of a Sanhedrin in Palestine, but the significance, the symbol of every law remains a powerful factor in the lives of the people. Thus, every law still retains a significance even in this most modern day.

A trace of symbolism may accomplish a great end. For example; the red heifer; no rational reason therefor was, or is, known to any one - not even Moses knew it, according to Talmudic authorities. But the practice, the offering thereof, is symbolic. It speaks volumes for a faithful people which serves God, which does first and Hearings after. In all time, there are to be offered ten red heifers; one was offered by Moses, one by Esra, seven during the Second Temple, and the tenth and last one, will be offered when the reunion of all Israel in Jerusalem will come to pass. The ashes of every red heifer was divided in three equal parts; one part the priest took; the other part was for the people, and the third part was stored away. What a self-expression of immortality of a people one discovers in the number and the times the ten red heifers were to be sacrificed! Nothing disappeared forever in Israel’s history; nothing was lost sight of; everything carried hope in its bosom.

Mingled-stuff garments must not be used anywhere; in all climes, in all seasons this must be remembered. Only in one instance, in a garment made for fringes - in a *Talit* of white and blue - the colors of Israel’s hope, my wool and linen be woven together! A token that the wool of the animal and the linen of the ground - the honest toil of the peasant - the return to the land, must ever be the goal of Israel!

The preservation of life and potent life in the family was the outstanding aim of the earliest Hebrews. Judah, the son of Jacob, was the first to inaugurate the practice. The pagan world could not grasp the significance thereof, the unworthiness of a brother taking to wife his living brothers disfavored wife, and the unworthiness of rebuilding the house of a deceased brother, because both these laws affect one and the same person. But the people of Israel continue to consider a living brother’s former wife, or the wife of a deceased brother who had children by her, a near relative with which no lawful conjugal relationship can be established. Back of this law Israel can see the entire system of purity of the race; in it, it sees mirrored the patriarchal life of his forefathers, and hopes for a return to a life where purity was complete, obligatory, and safe.

The *Azazel*-goat! A sacrifice for others! It is symbolic of Israel. It is the enigma of all ages!
