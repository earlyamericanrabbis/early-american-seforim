*“And the Lord spoke unto Moses, saying: Command Aaron and his sons, saying: This is the law of burnt-offering”* (Vayikra 6:1-2)

*“This is what the Verse points at saying: ‘For who in thy skies can be compared unto the Lord, Who among the sons of might can be likened unto the Lord”* (Tehillim 89:7)

The Holy One, blessed be He! said: “*If I were desiring a sacrifice for My own sake, would I not charge Michael, the arch-angel, who is near Me to offer them to Me?*” This points at the Psalmists Verse which speaks of those “*who dwell in the skies*”, of the angels whose habitation is in heaven. “*Yet*”, said God, “*of whom do I ask sacrifices?*” So it is concerning the Show-Bread: “*Every Sabbath day he shall set it in order before the Lord continually, it is from the children of Israel, an everlasting covenant*” (Vayikra 24). Peradventure, one will say: I have need for Israel’s sacrifices, for my own sake; tell him: Be it far from the Lord. Wherefore, then, did God command to offer sacrifices? So as to give more reward for Israel’s devotion, even as Isaiah, the prophet said:

*“The Lord was pleased for His righteousness’* *sake, to make the teaching great and glorious.*” (Yeshayahu 42:21; Pesikta, Vayikra 6)

The offerings to God, while the Temple existed in Jerusalem, were selected from five different kinds of clean animals and birds, namely: cloven footed ruminant cattle, goats, sheep, doves, and turtle doves. All forms of sacrifices came under four general groups, namely: burnt-offering, sin-offering; trespass-offering, and peace-offering. The thank-offering is included in the peace-offering. Thereto are added; the offering of an individual, the paschal lamb, and tithes of cattle, and the first-born.

Public offerings are limited only to burnt-offerings and to sin-offerings. The only peace-offering of a public nature is in connection with the Pentecost bread, which consists of two sheep, the whole of which is known as “*slaughtering of the public peace-offering.*” The public never brings a trespass-offering; and never offers a bird as a sacrifice.

The burnt-offering of cattle consists of males only; but no distinction in gender is made when the burnt-offering is a bird, although lack of a limb disqualifies it. Male calves, too, are brought as a burnt-offering.

A sin-offering is taken from clean cattle, both calves and full-grown, male and female.

A trespass-offering is taken from male sheep only, some from the full grown ram variety, and some from the young lamb variety.

A peace-offering is taken from cattle, goats, and sheep, both full grown and tender, male and female; but no birds.

Every public sacrifice must be a male; every public sin-offering is taken from goats and cattle, but not from sheep; and every public burnt-offering is taken from sheep and cattle, but not from goats.

Every individual sin-offering is taken from the female variety of either goats or sheep, but not from cattle, save only three, namely: sin-offering of a ruler, which is a he-goat; sin offering of an amounted priest, and that is an ox; and the sin offering which the high priest offers on the day of Atonement, which too, is an ox, and the offerings of the latter two are burnt.

Every public sin-offering is eaten by the priests save the he-goat, the fellow of which was sent to Azazel, the offerings of idolaters, and the ox sacrificed by the public as an offering of “*innocence*”.

The burnt-offerings, the sin-offerings, the trespass-offerings, and the public peace-offerings are strictly holy; but individual peace-offerings, first-born, tithes, of cattle, and the paschal lamb, semi-holy.

There are four categories of peace-offerings: (1) public peace-offering, which is brought together with bread on Pentecost; (2) peace-offerings of individuals on a celebration of joy or a holiday, which is brought without bread; (3) peace-offering of a nazarite at the conclusion of his days of separation, which is brought bread, and consists of a ram; (4) peace-offering brought either in fulfillment of a vow, or as a present to which bread is added; this offering is called thanksgiving-offering, and the bread - thanksgiving bread.

The interpreters of the Torah, throughout the Mishnaic and Talmudic periods down to Maimonides, made a profound study of the Levitical laws affecting sacrifices, and numerous treatises were constructed by later Rabbinical authors, so that as an academic study, the subject thereof is considered a most important branch of Jewish scholarship. In the Torah itself are 142 commandments in connection with the construction, service and maintenance of the Temple, of which 76 are mandatory and 66 prohibitive. To give one an idea of the range of this branch of study, the Rabbinical laws concerning the Sabbath might be cited as an example. The number of commandments in the Torah concerning the Sabbath is but five, two mandatory and three prohibitive!

The details concerning each commandment in connection with the service of the Temple, particularly with sacrifices, are of such profound character that but few scholars in a generation were spoken of as competent authorities on this subject. Modern Rabbinical schools offer no-course whatever in this branch of higher Jewish learning. Even in the study of the book of Leviticus, the part concerning sacrifices does not occupy any place in the curriculum. Almost one-fourth of the entire ritual law is now, in so far as modern Rabbinism is concerned, obsolete. It is not even considered academically as an important subject, although Kabala is superficially taken up by some modern Rabbis. Perhaps, because mysticism furnishes some theme for widowed ladies, and others, who seek new life for all but wasted bodies and impoverished souls, the study thereof is worth something professionally, if not as a publicity stunt, or as a stepping-stone to a degree in divinity.

Of course this is but one important subject which now is completely out of the minds of both clergy and laity. The radical group of the 19th century pronounced the subject of sacrifices, the mode of service, and every other Levitical law in connection with the Temple, dead for all times. As far as they are concerned that part of our Torah was completely divorced from present-day and from future Judaism.

But Israel’s hope to gain a majority of population in Palestine, when more than 600,000 adult men will live there. Then it will become the duty of the Palestinian Jewry to organize a Sanhedrin. According to the doctrine of Maimonides, based upon Talmudic authority, the members of the Sanhedrin will be selected from among the ordained Rabbis; but the ordination - *Semichah* - will have to be put back in practice by no less an authority to rebuild the Temple, and re-instate the ancient service, including sacrifices, therein.

Nevertheless, the scholar who did devote some of his time to the study of this subject is no to be considered antiquated, or a carrier of dead weight, as every form of sacrifice, defined in the Torah and elucidated in the Talmud mirrors a most modern lesson for the house of Israel.

Let no man believe that God needs sacrifices. In this Maimonides, is right; all sacrifices were but one great course of instruction for Israel, to separate them from the practices of their idolatrous neighbors. The binding of Isaac was an illustration as to how God desires not to have human sacrifices offered to Him. Wisely did the Midrashic authors say:

“*If I were desiring a sacrifice for My own sake, would I not charge Michael, the arch-angel, who is near Me, to offer them to Me?*”

The four kinds of sacrifices, the sin-offering, the burnt-offering, the trespass-offering, and the peace-offering, cover the ground of human shortcomings. No amount of either preaching or charity-giving will actually restore to a man or woman his or her tranquility, or peacefulness of mind, when he or she is conscious of the fact that his or her life was interrupted by sin.

Prayer will do a lot; repentance may change life’s course; but what impresses most, what leaves the heart pure, the hands clean, and the conscience free, is some act entailing actual participation of body and soul. A sacrifice without repentance was not acceptable; neither can repentance with words only be of much help to the sinner. It required both. A sacrifice was required from rich and poor alike, although the values differed in proportion to the material ability of the individual. That system, too, kept the people in constant touch with the priests, the teachers of the Torah, the capital of the country, and the religious laws generally. One had to know what it was sinful to do, before he could bring a sacrifice. Besides, it gave every citizen a lesson in humility and discipline. Imagine a respectable citizen, leading an animal throughout his own neighborhood, where everybody knew that he did something wrong, and goes to atone for it! No one shamed him; no one at all mocked him; he was going to regain his self-respect, to make peace with his Maker. That was a form of sacred discipline, much more than the present-day jail, or penitentiary, or reform school or charity drive, or black-ball, or ostracism. Everybody knew that everybody else is human, with all human failings. No hypocrisy there. Indeed: “*The Lord was pleased, for His righteousness’ sake - To make the teaching great and glorious.*”
