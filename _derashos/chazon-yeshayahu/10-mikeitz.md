*“Now Jacob saw that there was corn in Egypt, and Jacob said unto his sons: ‘ Why do you look upon one another?”* (Bereishis 42:1)

*“Jacob saw travellers bringing corn from Egypt. Derasha: Now Jacob saw that the time had come for the fulfillment of the prophecy of: ‘Know for a surety that thy seed shall be a stranger in a land that is not theirs’. (Bereishis 15:13) And he also saw the distant hope that, ‘Afterward shall they all come out with great substance’. (Ibid.14) And Jacob said to his sons; Why do you look upon one another;” so self-satisfied in the eyes of the Canaanites? The Midrashists say: the expression, Tira’u, the proper term for what he wanted to convey to them, is not employed, but titerau, an additional letter Tav, to signify to them; that the pronouncement of four centuries of exile, made by Almighty God to Abraham, (Ibid), when he was told that ‘thy seed shall be a stranger in a land that is not theirs’, was about to come into effect. Tav is numerically 400 said Jacob to them: Lemah Titerau—’Why do you look one upon another, is it because of the four hundred years’ of exile?”* (Midrash Rabba, Bereishis; Pesikta, Bereishis,42; See also Ta’anis 10b).

Jacob and his sons continued to instruct in the yeshivah founded by Shem and Eber, of which Abraham and Isaac successively were the heads. Jacob, in his days as the head of that school, instructed his children and grandchildren, and his entire household, in the several branches of knowledge then known to the civilized world. In addition, every incident, every vision, every dream, every journey, every camping place, every neighbor, was discussed, analyzed, and criticized in order to get to the kernel of each topic. Every law custom, ritual and dogma which had been incorporated in the religion preached by Abraham, and Isaac and received from them by Jacob and his sons was close - examined, lest it had acquired some foreign substance in the course of the continuous wandering of the patriarchs. Every right acquired by the patriarchs was preserved by each member of the school, so that in case one of them should migrate, or settle somewhere else, he should know his rights in every territory. Moreover, the patriarchs, being prophets, knew and observed the Torah, in all its detailed commandments.

The few families, however, kept together and their chief objective was to found a nation upon their peculiar belief and mode of life.

Jacob’s spirits fell considerably when his sons came and showed him a “*blood-stained coat of many colors,*” and claimed it to be what was left of his son Joseph. The prophetic spirit, too, had left him. He could not see far into the future. But he knew what destiny awaited his children. He knew of the traditional Promise made by Almighty in a vision which his grandfather Abraham saw. He also knew that his wanderings were even then parts of the exile period decreed against Abraham’s descendants. But the *Shekinah* had withheld its glory from him since the day of the tragic news about his most beloved son had reached him, and he could not, by means of prophecy, foresee the exact time when the actual decree of “*Know for surety that thy seed shall be a stranger in a land that is not theirs*” would come to pass.

Being a descendant of a nomadic people and a wanderer himself, he knew the dangers of famine in the several territories in his immediate vicinity. His grandfather, as well as his father had suffered from famine, and had to look for bread in “*lands that were not theirs*”!

In the midst of these contemplations he saw travellers coming and going in long caravans, to and from Egypt, and the caravans were empty going and laden with corn when coming. He then saw, quite naturally, a vision which belonged to generations hence; he saw the treasures of the earth being carried to Egypt. For what purpose? To what end? God guides the footsteps of all mankind. Why, then, were the peoples of the earth going to Egypt, treasure in hand, in exchange for bread? His lowly spirit and lack of prophetic inspiration had withheld the real reason for that, until the famine reached his door, when, like a flash, it dawned upon him. “*Look, my sons, and disciples! The treasures of the world are being carried to Egypt. I see the triumph of my exiled children; I can visualize them now, even as I see that there is corn in Egypt, carrying out these very treasures on their way to liberty, to their land flowing with milk and honey.*” “*Yash Sheber Bmirayim*” There is hope- the true meaning of the word Sheber is hope - in the land of Egypt, for my children!

He saw that his time to enter the land of exile was drawing near; that his children will soon be “*a stranger in a land that is not theirs*”, he identified the “*land*” as Egypt, because of the fact that of all the countries near Canaan, God had bestowed upon her the blessings of plenty.

But Jacob could not discuss this openly; he was too much stricken, with sorrow, first for the loss of his most beloved son, and second, for the knowledge gained that exile was facing him and his children; he, therefore, spoke to his sons in symbols. He added a letter to a word which in turn added a tragic meaning to it. He knew that his sons, true scholars as they were would understand the numerical meaning of that letter, Tay, which stands for four hundred—a symbol of the four hundred years’ exile which was then to overtake them.

The sons of Jacob knew of the fate which awaited them; they had heard it as a tradition from their father at school; they believed it, too; but they could not realize that the stroke was due just then.

Here the Midrashic authors reached a high psychological position. In the word *Titeratu*, Jacob, in addition to the symbol of the four hundred years’ exile, as was said, gave his sons, both an admonition and a rebuke. The admonition consisted in this: The household of Jacob still had food; the wolf was not yet actually at the door; he knew that his sons, too, saw the caravans, and the nature of their journeys was too well apparent to need any hints from him; but to his amazement he noticed an air of indifference on the faces of his sons, because there was still food in the larder. He therefore, admonished them not to wait until there will be actual suffering, or a threat thereof, but to provide for their household in advance.

The sages, in a much later period, declared: “*The life of one who has not provided for at least a full year’s sustenance is suspended in the air.*” But the word has still a deeper meaning: *Lama Titeratu* — Why do you look upon one another as self satisfied in the eyes of the Canaanites? This was a rebuke. But this rebuke contains a great moral lesson to the children of Israel. It is very unwise to appear to be better off than the rest of the population of the country in a time of distress; it is very bad taste to make the other fellow feel that he is poor and needy. It was evil to go around dazzling the eyes of the Canaanites with an air of plenty, or arrogance.

The children of Israel had displayed both, pride and fear; pride, in appearing to have plenty in the eyes of the Canaanites, and fear, because of the approach of exile. Their father wisely told them: Cast away your pride and you will know of no fear!
