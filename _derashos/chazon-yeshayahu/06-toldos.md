*Jacob and Esau*[^1]

*“Behold, thy brother Esau, as touching thee, doth comfort himself, purposing to kill thee. (Bereishis 27:42) The sages said: He already considers thee dead; he even receives the mourner’s consolation over thy demise; yea, he already drinks the cup of condolence after thy burial.”* (Yalkut Shimoni, Bereishis 27:116:1).

*“The voice is the voice of Jacob. (Ibid 22) No accepted prayer was ever delivered unless there was the voice of the seed of Jacob among the worshipers.”* (Gitin 57b)

*“Said a Seducee to Gebyhah Ben Pesys: ‘Woe unto you sinners, who say: The dead will come to life! Seeing that the living die, how is it possible for the dead to live again’? Said Gebyhah to the Seducee: ‘On the contrary, woe unto you sinners, who say that the dead will not come to life! Seeing that they that were not, are, they that were, deduct a minor from a major, surely will be’. Said the Seducee to Gebyhah: art thou including me among the sinners? All I have to do is to rise up and thrust my foot against thy back and straighten thy hunchback (Gebyhah was hunchbacked) for thee! ‘Then,’ said Gebyhah to him, ‘If thou wilt be able to do this for me, thy fame as a surgeon will be spread, and thou wilt receive a great increase in wages.’”* (Sanhedrin 91a).

It is evident that with the birth of Jacob began the Jewish problem. The hunter and hunted; the swordsman and the man of the book; the warrior and the pacifist; the man of might and the man of right; the materialist and the moralist; the vengeful and the meek; Esau and Jacob!

Without having any pretence, Esau tolerated Jacob, who was content with his father’s instruction and his mother’s love. Jacob looked upon the fields, the forests, the rivers, the hills, the dales and the brooks as sources of divine inspiration; Esau looked upon all these as sources of greed and gain; wild life to Jacob was a divine addition to man’s store of knowledge, but to Esau it was an added source of revenue; people to Jacob were the height of Almighty’s Will in the universe, an expression of His Own Self through their souls, but to Esau they were here in large numbers to slave for the few supermen, to applaud the despoiler, to fight the battles of the mighty. The divine purpose of God in creating this world was obvious to Jacob, but to Esau it was but a material world to grab, to drain its sweetness for self-satisfaction and lust.

So with Rome and Jerusalem, the former stood for all that Esau represented, the latter for everything that Jacob aspired to achieve. So with the Beth-ha-Midrash and the armory, the university and the cannon factory, the library and the battlefield! In fact, so is the Book and the Sword! The Book invites all peoples to come and enjoy the blessings of God while here and prepare for a life Eternal hereafter; the Sword destroys the spirit of the many, satisfies the lust of the few and denies all the fat of the earth and the dew of heaven.

The Christian, the Mohammedan, when they do pray, we hear the voice of Jacob speaking through them and by them. Prayer does not spring forth from the mouth of the cannon, the sword of the conqueror, or the self-satisfied materialist.

The greatest blessing of the remnant few is the fact that the followers of Esau, in all succeeding generations, considered the prayer of Jacob a dead issue. Esau considered Jacob dead, because he believed the stroke can be delivered any time. He celebrated many wakes, drank many cups in anticipation of the event, which was considered by him the smallest item of the procedure.

Not alone the followers of Esau, yea, even among Israel itself, there arose from time to time those who believed that Jacob was dead, that all he did need was the last stroke, which could be done at will. The ancient Saducees, for example. They, as the Kuzari avers, did not believe in the World to Come - *Olam Habba*. To them Jewish life was but living in the shadow of death, the end of which was ever present, and sure to come.

And there have been, and unfortunately for them, there still are groups among Israel whose views are no broader than that of the edge of the sword, and their hope not stronger than that of the Saducees.

Then, too, the successors of the Saducees carry on a philosophy of fear. Believing that their end is near, they endeavor to prolong it, to hang on to life, even to a life of degradation, as much as they can. And of those who brave the rage of Esau’s hands they speak as of a thorn, a hunchback, upon the body of the dying Israel. Their fear is accompanied by an ever-present treat, saying that if the voice of Jacob is lifted up Esau’s hand might strike the final blow.

Jacob and Esau, Gebyhag and the Saducee, the Voice and the Hand, the Book and the Sword, how long will the struggle last?

Until the Voice of Jacob will be raised in solemn protest, which will re-echo throughout the world as a prayer, and both Christian and Mohammedan peoples will be moved to join. Such prayer will be accepted.
