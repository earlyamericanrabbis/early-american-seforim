*“And there came one that had escaped, and told Abram the Hebrew (Bereishis 14:13)”. Said Rabbi Judah: “The whole world was on one side, and he was on another side”* (Midrash Rabba, Bereishis 42; Yalkut Shimeony, Bereishis 73)

*“And Abram passed through the land unto the place of Shechem, unto the terebinth of Moreh. And the Canaanite was then in the land. And the Lord appeared unto Abram and said: Unto thy seed will I give this land; and he builded there an altar unto the Lord, who appeared unto him: Abraham did not build an altar until he received the tidings from God that Eretz Yisrael would belong to his children”* (Bereishis 12:6 - 7; Yalkut Shimeony, Bereishis 67)

The outstanding heads of families in the intervening generations between Noah and Abraham were: Shem, Arpachsad, Sholah, Eber, Peleg, Reu, Serug, Nahor, and Abraham. The Hebrew branch of the Semitic race, according to tradition, was founded by Eber (Rabbi Nehemiah, Yalkut Shimeony, Bereishis 73). The English “h” takes the place of the Hebrew “e”. Ebri, singular; Ebrim, plural, denote descendants of Eber.

It is evident that the people of the intervening generations between Noah and Abraham were of a higher intelligence than the people of the intervening generations between Adam and Noah, as we trace progressive steps in the general conduct of the post-deluvian population. The rainbow-symbol, the permission to eat animal meat, the six commandments they were charged to observe, speak, in so far as their spiritual development was concerned, for a much higher level of culture and attainment than during the ante-deluvian era. The people of the generation of Enoch erred exceedingly and established an idolatry entirely unfit for human beings. (See Bereishis 4:26; Maimonides, Book One, 4:1-2) Such misconduct had to be eradicated. The flood did that. Then came the generation of the confusion (Bereishis 11:1-9). “*And*”, says Maimonides, “*on this path the world continued its course of circuitry until the birth of the firmest pillar of the world, Abraham, our father*” (Book One 4: 2).

“*And when Abraham was forty years old, he recognized his Creator. After he came to this knowledge and understanding, he commenced to confute the sons of Ur of the Chaldeas, and to organize disputations with them, saying: ‘This is not a true path that you are following’. And he destroyed their images, and ventured out preaching to the people, and warning them that it is wrong to worship any one else save the God of the universe, and unto Him alone is it proper to bow down, to offer sacrifices, and compound offerings, so that all future generations may recognize Him*” (Ibid). This gives one an idea of the perseverance of Abraham. In the midst of idolatry worshipped in blind fanaticism, he proclaimed the doctrine of Monotheism. Abraham feared no evil. The savage temper of the hosts he had to face during all of his wanderings never discouraged him.

Abraham was all of his life “*across the other side*”. He became known, throughout the countries of his sojourn as “*The Ebri*”—the one who ever continues to be “*on the other side*”.

Some call it “*stiffneckedness*”, others denounce it as suicide, and to some it appears to be a divine miracle, but it remains a fact that Abraham’s ideas became so deeply rooted in the mind and conscience of his seed that what he did singly they do collectively. The perseverance of Abraham and his ultimate victory against the idolatrous masses of his age is more than symbolic, and has been more than all nations combined could be, in so far as the ability of his children to thrive on being in the minority is concerned. Abraham the Ebri individually typified the whole house of Israel collectively.

Long stretches of time pass by before even a part of the human race began to comprehend the ideas proclaimed by Abraham. The Torah mentions a number of names, conspicuous in their time, but never again to be remembered, or even to be mentioned. Abraham came in contact with kings and kingdoms who, too, never again are mentioned in the Torah. Only Abraham’s kindred, including that of Ishmael, become the central figures in the great drama of the ancients, which is continuing to unfold itself before the eyes of the student. It is evident from the very first appearance of Abraham that he did not believe that his efforts would be crowned with success among the peoples of his sojourn. He saw how unripe they were, and how deeply stooped in their practices they had sunk. He desired that his belief, his ideas and his program for a useful life upon earth be perpetuated. And he wanted, above all else, a people to spring forth of his own loins, and a land where his people would set an example to all others.

When God promised a land to his seed, Abraham erected an altar! That was a promise which he longed to hear. He knew then that there would always be an element in the population of the world which would have no fear to fight for a righteous cause even though it be dangerous to do so, even though the right be on the side of only a small minority, with the whole world on the other side. Of such mettle, he knew only his seed would be. They were, they are, and they will be just as he was, steadfast on the one side, even with the whole world on the other side.
