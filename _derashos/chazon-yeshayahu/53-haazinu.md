*“Give ear, ye heavens, and I will speak; and let the earth hear the words of my mouth.” (Devarim 32:1)*

*“Hear, O heavens, and give ear, O earth” (Yeshayahu 1:2).*

*“Because he (Moses) was near heaven and far from earth, he said “Give ear”, to the heavens, and “hear”, to the earth; just the reverse of the language used by Isaiah, who was far from heaven and near the earth.” (Sifre, Devarim 32)*

*“He compassed him about, He cared for him, He kept him as the apple of his eyes” (Devarim 32.10) “He compassed him”, with elders; “He cared for him”, with prophets; and “kept him” from harm of evil spirits.”* (Sifre, Ibid.).

The birth, development and growth of the Jewish people was constantly guided by miracles. Abraham was miraculously saved; his son Isaac, was born to his mother at the age of ninety, and was saved from being sacrificed on Mount Moriah by the timely voice of an angel. Jacob escaped from Esau and Laban by a hair’s breadth, and his sons were delivered from Egypt by wonderful tokens, and thereafter lived forty years in the Wilderness on God’s own bread - *Manna*. The Red Sea broke in twain to open a way for them, and Jericho fell at the sound of the priests’ trumpets. Miracles, miracles, and miracles on every occasion when rational or natural planned escape from danger seemed hopeless. Until they ate bread made of grain of the Promised Land, these direct miracles then ceased, and the Elders, whom Joshua had ordained, took over the spiritual life of the people in their hands.

But there is nothing miraculous about the birth, development and growth of Judaism. The lawgiver, Moses, or its earliest leaders and exponents, Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, endowed with logical and keen minds, could not agree with the superstitious practices, or gross forms of belief in vogue among their neighbors. Moses was a real shepherd, loving his flock, protecting them and suffering the hardships, common among good shepherds, for them. The greatest measure of fullness in Judaism contains most rational, humane and orderly laws, fit for the highest form of civilized life. In fact, the foundation of human civilization is found only in the Book of books— the Torah. The rewards promised in return for obedience to the laws of the Torah, in addition to that which is in store in the “*World to Come,*” is exclusively upon earth. Prosperity, plenty, health, peace, security, seasonal rains, longevity, happiness and joy are but few of the indicated vast number of blessings. “*If ye will hearken*” was the only condition.

The curses pronounced, “*If ye will not hearken*”, both in Leviticus and in Deuteronomy, are testimony that the punishment of the guilty is not procrastinated: Repentance may avert the evil visit of all or any of them; but their presence to mock and haunt the non-believer was registered along with every national catastrophe which overtook the people. Retribution, as indicated in the Torah, touches the sinner’s and evil-doer’s life here upon earth.

For crime and sin the Torah pronounces seven different kinds of punishments: (1) stoning; (2) burning; (3) beheading; (4) strangulation; (5) death by act of God; (6) excision; (7) lashes. The various acts of crime and sins, for which the punishment is stoning total eighteen; burning, nine; beheading, two strangulation, nine; death by an act of God, eleven; excision, twenty-two-total capital offences, seventy-one; lashes, two hundred and seventy-seven, making a grand total of three hundred and forty-eight.

Crucifixion, Lax-Sectio - cutting or carving a debtor to satisfy his creditors, Madonna’s Embrace, and other means of punishment, now or in the past practiced by other peoples, are of Roman, Spanish, English origin - as trial by fire, water and dry morsel are of German, French and Chinese origin. Taking in consideration the procedure in Jewish courts that every offender, before being found guilty, must have been warned before committing the crime, it is evident that punishment was not docked upon as a social act of vengeance. Heaven was brought down to earth, when a life had to be taken, or forever stigmatized. Justice was to be meted out without regard to persons, connections, stations in life, or political expediency. There was not much law, and still less intricacies of law, but there was a full measure of justice.

Punishment in the “World to Come” - retribution - was not turned over to men. The balancing of that scale of justice was left entirely to the judgment of God. The sins of mankind were never intended to be weighed or measured by human-made instruments. There was enough of individual behaviour left in the hands of men; the bigot, the sycophant, the political religionist, the fanatic, the demagogue, the muck-raker, however, had to be eliminated, and insofar as the Torah is concerned, was completely eliminated. The earth was kept in its place, when the things in which heaven alone is concerned was the issue. Indeed, “*the heavens are the heavens of the Lord, but the earth hath He given to the children of men*” (Tehillim 115:16). And to rebuke one’s neighbor was not to be turned into an instrument of torture for, the positive commandment to rebuke (Vayikra 19:17) is given between two negative commandments; not to hate and not to bear sin.

The eradication of sin and of crime, the denunciation of law standards of morality, the evils of war, the exploitation of labor, the curse of ignorance, the wickedness of false dealing, the dangers of prejudice and hatred, the denial of justice, the suppression of righteousness - to decry all evil, was the mission of priests and prophets, of Rabbi and preacher. But the objective to be attained by them was to destroy sin, then the wicked world would cease to be. Even as was the prayer of Kind David: *“Let sin cease out of the earth, and the wicked will be no more.”* (Tehillim 104:35) To utilize the laws of the Torah for the purpose of carrying on a war against individual persons, to ruin them for failing to carry out all of the implied saintliness is to stoop to a level far below the intent of God in giving it to His children upon earth. *“The Torah, in all its detailed admonitions and precepts, was brought down upon earth to bestow and maintain peace among men.”* (Gittin 59b). It surely should not, outside of a hall of justice, be turned into a sword. To take from a man his spiritual life is the equivalent of denying him the hope of repentance. Indeed, “*as long as a man is alive, hope is still in his bosom*” (Yerushalmi Berachos 9:1)

Moses, at the end of his career, knew both heaven and earth, although he was nearer to heaven than any man before or after him. He asked for the “*ear of heaven*” - a whisper was enough; but he knew his earth, too, and demanded even them a full and complete hearing of what he had to say, so that no one would miss a word and misunderstand him. Isaiah, in his vigorous outset, asked the earth to “*give ear*” to him, but requested heaven to Hearken to his words. Moses was the symbol of heaven upon earth - it is possible to make it so by observing the laws of the Torah which he had received upon Sinai from the hand of God. Isaiah was the Symbol of earth. He had seen what sin and corruption had done to it. He denounced both sin and sinner. The earth, he knew, would understand his least whisper. But he loved the people and, unlike Jonah, he did not want the destruction of the sinners, but of sin. He, therefore, prayed to heaven to listen to his every word.

Wisely did the sages of the Sifre say: “*He compassed him with Elders; He cared for him, with prophets, and he kept him, from evil spirits.*” Heaven points the way to mete out justice in the spirit of righteousness. It is up to earth to follow the example.
