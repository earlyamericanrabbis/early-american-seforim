*"Thou shalt in any wise let the dam go, etc. The Sages said: If you have fulfilled the commandment of the nest, you will merit to release a Hebrew servant; from where is this derived? As it is said, 'And when you send him out free from you.' And if you have sustained this commandment, you hasten the coming of King Messiah, concerning whom sending is written; from where is this derived? As it is said, 'That send forth the feet of the ox and the ass'"*

(Midrash Teitzei, Parashah 6)

How wondrous is the hidden idea in these words of our Sages, of blessed memory. How wondrous is the connection that unites the minor commandment—as understood by Chazal—of releasing the nest, with the most sublime aspiration of all humanity: the coming of the Messiah. It is as if within the fulfillment of this minor commandment lies the destiny of humanity, and through it is expressed the aspiration for a complete redemption and eternal happiness. And indeed, if we contemplate well this minor commandment, we will clearly see the thin, yet very strong, threads that connect and unite the minor commandment of releasing the nest with the coming of the Redeemer.

One of the greatest shortcomings of man, a shortcoming that is a stumbling block to the moral development of humanity, is "egoism"—this is man's self-love. This trait, in and of itself, if utilized with measure and rhythm, is highly beneficial for the preservation of the human race; but if one crosses the border, its loss outweighs its reward, and it is liable to lead to very bad consequences.

The well-known principle that "the intellect follows the desire" is highly faithful regarding these words of ours. Since the desire for one's own benefit is exceedingly great and strong, man therefore found within his intellect that his importance is indeed greater than all creatures, and his rights are more numerous than the rest of the world's creations—for all creatures were created solely for him, and the entire universe was created solely for his benefit and pleasure. And thanks to his "intellect," man found sufficient reasons permitting him to utilize his rights and to fulfill his longings and the desires of his soul. One of them is that only man was graced with "intellect," and through his intellect, behold, man succeeded in forging iron chains wherewith to bind all living creatures, to rule over them and dominate them according to his will and desire. By the power of his intellect, man invented a law: "Might rules the world."

By the power of such "logic"—that since man is stronger than other living creatures, the permission was given to him to rule over them—it brought man to a lower level. Man began to tyrannize human beings as well, and permitted himself to conquer into servants and subjects those people who, due to various circumstances, did not succeed in life. And thus two factions came into being in the world, whereby human beings were divided into two levels, into two classes: masters and servants, nobles and people of high stature—and people of low value standing at the bottom of the ladder.

Since then, that form of life has been stamped upon the universe, and history was created full of terrifying images—that history wherein each and every page is dipped in human blood, and each and every line is filled with suffering and harsh, terrible afflictions that the "servants," so to speak, suffer from those who crowned themselves with the title of masters. And sometimes, when their souls grow weary of their toil, the hand of the "servants" will be against their masters. It goes without saying that such conduct is very far from leading the universe to its desired perfection, its correct purpose, and its hoped-for destiny. And the primary shortcoming is that which we mentioned above: the feeling of man's "self-love" in an excessive measure, which brought him to err in his intellect. Instead of bringing benefit to the world, he hinders by his bad deeds the moral development of humanity.

But the truth, or more correctly the "alpha" of truth, is that man upon his birth already found before him a beautiful and perfected world, and he did not bring with him anything more than what was brought by the smallest worm dwelling inside a cleft of a rock, and man has no excess rights over the creeping thing. Man, like the small worm, are creatures of the Holy One, Blessed be He, and there is one right to both of them together to live and exist in the world, and in vain does man boast and think of himself as the crown of creation, that the entire world was created only for his benefit.[^1] \* And if man takes to his heart that all the living and all existence on earth has the right of existence just like man, then he will certainly understand that also to other human beings there is the justification to live, to breathe air, and to conduct themselves like him. And even if they fall below him in their strength, in their wisdom, in their wealth, and in their standing in life, nevertheless he has no permission to rule over them and to take them as servants for himself. And if this idea were to spread and find a hold among the people, then we would be drawing closer to the days of hope for which we aspire and yearn.

And this correct idea Chazal saw in the minor commandment of "releasing the nest," and they expressed it in their words which we brought as the foundation of our words. Our holy Torah came to arouse through this commandment the most delicate feelings of the human soul, and it calls out to us: Son of man! See, I have given you the permission to utilize living creatures for your benefit and pleasure, but please take to your heart and understand that it is not with absolute justice nor with complete uprightness that this matter was permitted to you; "*If a bird's nest chance to be before thee in the way in any tree, or on the ground, whether they be young ones, or eggs, and the dam sitting upon the young, or upon the eggs, thou shalt not take the mother with the young.*" Our holy Torah comes to teach us that even the small young ones have the right of existence no less than man, and though it permitted us to utilize them for our pleasure, nevertheless we must conduct ourselves with them with kindness and mercy, and not to cut them off and bring annihilation upon them. It is not beautiful for a man to satiate his longing and fulfill his desire through the pain and grief of the mother of the young ones, and therefore our holy Torah warns us: "*Thou shalt send away the mother,*" etc., do not dare to destroy the nest entirely. The dam will yet build for herself a new nest and find therein comforts and completions for her lost young ones, and you shall suffice only with the children—"*and the young take to thee*"—and if you conduct yourself thus, then I permit to you their eating.

And to this Chazal directed in their saying: "*If you have fulfilled the commandment of releasing the nest,*" etc.—if you paid attention to the rights of the bird, and if you deepened your understanding to comprehend that it is forbidden to eliminate its right of existence, and you did not destroy its nest—then I promise you that you will merit to release a Hebrew servant; from where is this derived? "*And when you send him out free from you.*" That is to say, if you have already reached such a level to understand that to every living creature there is the right to live just like man, then you will also know to recognize the human rights of your peer—man. And though he is your servant, standing at a low and lesser level than you—whether he was sold to you for his theft by the court, or whether he was sold to you of his own free will—yet in his being your servant, his nest is destroyed, and his young ones—his children—are abandoned by their father. And if your heart felt the pain of the mother of the young ones, then you will certainly and surely feel the pains of the father who was sold as a servant, and you will endeavor to establish his destroyed nest by freeing him from his servitude and sending him out free. If a man sends away from himself that which belongs to him by uprightness and justice, he proves by this that the feeling of "egoism" is limited within him, and his heart is filled with love for all existence. And this was the intention of Chazal in their emphasizing the "*releasing*"—they come by this to teach about man and to show his level that was elevated through "*releasing the nest.*"

And if man reached such perfection to feel the pain of his fellow, even though he stands at a level lesser than him, then "*you hasten Messiah, concerning whom sending is written; from where is this derived?—As it is said, 'That send forth the feet of the ox and the ass.'*" In this we are going and drawing closer to the days of the Messiah, when humanity will then reach such a level that the feeling of love for creatures will not be expressed only by means of "*releasing the nest*" or "*sending a servant out free,*" but rather also "*that send forth the feet of the ox and the ass*"—even those living creatures that stand under the domain of man, he will send them out free, in his knowledge that to all the world's creations there is one right and one law, and it is not for a man to rule over anyone whosoever or to utilize living creatures for his benefit and pleasure.

[^1]: Although our Torah permitted the utilization of living creatures for the needs of man, yet the permission is only temporary and under known conditions; this is a sort of "compromise" with man's desire. But in the future to come, on the hoped-for day, this permission will certainly be nullified and the world will return to its initial era like the days of Adam Ha-Rishon. Here it is worthwhile to mention what I heard in the name of the Rabbi, the Gaon, Reb Aaron Gordon, may he live for many good days, Amen, the Rabbi in Suwałki and currently in Łomża—a moral idea as to why the Torah obligated covering the blood of a bird and a wild beast after their slaughter, which is not the case with a domestic animal: because a domestic animal dwells with human beings and man cares for its sustenance and all its needs, especially in the winter days, which is not the case with a bird or a beast of the field whose sustenance comes to them not from the hands of human beings, and nevertheless man permits himself to take their life; therefore the Torah commanded to cover the spilled blood, to remind man that at the very least he should be ashamed of these actions of his.
