*"It was taught, Rabbi Yehuda says: On Rosh Hashanah they would blow with \[the horns\] of rams, curved ones, etc. In what point do they disagree? This Master thinks: on Rosh Hashanah, the more a person bends his mind, the better it is, etc.; and that Master thinks: on Rosh Hashanah, the more a person straightens his mind, the better it is, etc."*

(Rosh Hashanah 26b)

The idea hidden here in this dispute is that it revolves around that which is agreed upon in philosophical works: that the subject of all psychic actions is the "I" (*Ani*). The "I" is the axis upon which all the contents of consciousness. It is the subject of the entire world of internal phenomena, in which everything is summarized and becomes one—that which the soul performs and that which occurs within it.

And this perfection of the "I" is termed: development into personality, and it leads to the creation of the will and the character for the achievement of a certain specific purpose and a clear goal.

However, the consciousness of the "I" is not equal in every man. There is a man whose "I" attributes his desires and actions to himself; and when he feels and recognizes himself as the subject of his actions, he knows clearly and with certainty that his personality itself is it—that his "I" is that which lived and recorded all these, which led to the creation of the will and character to achieve the goal.

And there is again a man whose consciousness of the "I" is captured by the representation of the object, but he himself is not as the subject of his actions, and also his personality itself is nothing, but rather he knows how to distinguish between true and imaginary advantages in the full light of the concept.

And this is what is meant by: "*This Master thinks: on Rosh Hashanah, the more a person bends his mind, the better it is*". That is to say, that the consciousness of the "I" is captured by the representation of the object, meaning that a man shall not think that he is the subject of his actions, that his personality itself is it—that his "I" is that which lived and recorded all these . . .

And "*This Master thinks: on Rosh Hashanah, the more a person straightens his mind, the better it is*". That is to say, when he himself feels and recognizes himself as the subject of his actions, that he is the axis upon which all the contents of consciousness turn, and only his "I" is that which lived and recorded all these, which led to the creation of the will and character for the achievement of the goal . . . and understand this.
