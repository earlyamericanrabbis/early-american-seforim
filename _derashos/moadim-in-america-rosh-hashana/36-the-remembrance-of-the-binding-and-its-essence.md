*"And the binding of Isaac for his offspring, today in mercy remember."*

(In the Musaf prayer of Rosh Hashanah)

The commentators have already noted here wondrously: behold, the Binding of Isaac is one of the trials of Abraham our Father; if so, for what reason do we not mention his name as well?

However, it can be said the idea in this is according to what is explained in our holy literature: behold, the remembrance of the deeds of the Patriarchs awakens—Heaven forbid—an accusation against the children, because they have not learned from the deeds of their fathers.

But do we not mention the merit of our fathers in prayer? Rather, it can be said that we do so because the Attribute of Mercy has a claim against the aforementioned accusation: for the Patriarchs were not yet commanded regarding the Holy Torah, and behold, the *Yetzer* does not incite as much against one who is not commanded . . . as is known.[^1]

And now it comes out well why, in the blessing of *Zichronot* at its conclusion, we mention only Isaac; for he was not commanded regarding this at all it was Abraham who was commanded to offer him, therefore there is no need to fear the accusation . . .

But regarding Abraham our Father, who was commanded regarding this commandment, and the Evil Inclination fought with him with all its strength, as is known—even so, he stood in his trial. This would—Heaven forbid—give strength to the mouth of the Accuser to accuse us by comparison; therefore we do not mention him.

And this is also the reason for what is said: "*In mercy remember.*" Because in the remembrance of the act of the Binding in its entirety, perhaps—Heaven forbid—the remembrance of Abraham our Father will ascend toward liability for us. Therefore we plead that no side of liability shall be remembered by this, Heaven forbid; only that the remembrance shall be in mercy . . . and understand this.

According to what was stated above, there is seemingly a difficulty to raise: how do we mention the Binding of Isaac in prayer? Behold, on the contrary, it is a disadvantage for us because we do not live up to it, that one of our fathers surrendered his soul before the Holy One, Blessed be He, and yet our deeds are spoiled?

However, the idea in this is according to what is explained in the commentators regarding the version of the prayer: "*And the binding of Isaac for his offspring — remember*". This implies that it refers also to Esau.[^2]

If so, it is correct that we mention it. Since all inhabitants of the world are judged on this day, as is known, we therefore say: "*And the binding of Isaac for his offspring — remember.*" This implies that even Esau is included. Consequently, on this day when we return in repentance, the reminder is for our good, but for Esau, it is to his disadvantage—because even on this day he remains like Esau . . . automatically, the Attribute of Mercy will be awakened upon us . . . and understand this.

Since we have spoken of the mention of the Binding in prayer, it is worth mentioning what the commentators ask regarding what was brought above: "*And the binding of Isaac for his offspring — remember,*" which implies it refers also to Esau. The *Magen Avraham* said there: "*If one said 'to his offspring,' and Esau is included, he is mistaken. For we have learned: One who says 'Konam forbidden from the seed of Abraham' is permitted to the other nations, as it is said: 'For in Isaac shall your seed be called' (Bereishis 21:12),*" etc. See there.

Seemingly, what loss is there if Esau also merits through the merit of the Binding? Do we not pray for the entire world on Rosh Hashanah?[^3]

However, it can be said the idea in this is according to what is explained in our holy literature: behold, the matter of the Binding is also one of the inventions foundational mechanisms that perpetuates the existence of our people to stand in the trials of the Holy One, Blessed be He . . . like the commandment of circumcision . . .

As world experience indicates to us: at the time when they attacked Israel to abandon their God—whereby they brought them up on pyres of fire and altars of blood with the throwing of iron . . . even so, they did not abandon the lamp of the Lord, and did not surrender before all their oppressors. Rather, they fought with a sublime courage of spirit! With a clear and wonderful spirit! Refusing to admit that there is a God besides Him; rather, they found— *"...further courage of spirit, to deny and despise the gods of their tormentors.*" And is this not because of this 'invention'—that the people of Israel have been accustomed from their mother's womb to spill their blood for the Lord?[^4]

If so, what connection does Esau have to this? Behold, he is the one who will attack Israel to abandon their God . . . How can he merit such a holy thing that symbolizes their self-sacrifice for the Lord? . . . and understand this.

According to what was stated above, it is further worth noting here what the commentators wondered about: how the Holy One, Blessed be He, commanded Abraham to sacrifice Isaac his son as an offering, as it is written: (Bereishis 22:2) "*And He said: Take now your son, your only son... and offer him there for a burnt offering.*"

Behold, as soon as he slaughtered him, Abraham would become an *onan*, and how could he engage with his offering? After all, an *onan* is disqualified for *Avodah*?

Rather, they offer various resolutions for this:

1.  One is because Abraham was a *Kohen Gadol*, and a High Priest may offer even while an *onan*, as is known (See Rambam, Laws of Entry to the Sanctuary 2:15).

2.  Furthermore, although Isaac was bound on Mount Moriah, at the time of the binding, he Abraham had the status of a *bamah*, and an *onan* is permitted to perform service at a *bamah* (See also *Chatam Sofer*, Orach Chaim, 208).

And again, the entire reason that an *onan* is disqualified for service and for eating holy things is because the consumption of holy items requires great joy, in the manner that kings eat (See in Rambam, *Mishneh LaMelekh* there).

However, in the case of Abraham our Father, when he agreed to sacrifice his son as a divine command, there was no room for the sorrow of mourning, for he certainly did this with great joy for the sake of the Blessed Name, as is brought in the commentators regarding what is mentioned in the Midrash (*Midrash Pinchas*): "*When Isaac was bound upon the altar, Abraham was worried about the loss of his blood, and not worried about the loss of his days*." That is to say, he was worried about his blood—that it might be disqualified—for Isaac was a firstborn, and the blood of a firstborn and the blood of a burnt offering that were mixed are disqualified for sprinkling, as is known. But over the loss of his days he was not worried, for this thing he did with joy . . . therefore it was permitted for him to perform his service . . .

And further, behold according to the instruction of our Sages of blessed memory: (Zevachim 99a) that an *onan* does not sacrifice, etc., it is because of distraction of the mind. The commentators explained: that the mind is severed from him and he becomes confused, since he sees his trouble before him.

But in the case of "*a High Priest who is greater than his brothers*"—who according to the words of our Sages of blessed memory (Midrash Tanchuma, Emor 4) was great also in wisdom—it is not relevant to say that he would distract his mind and become confused . . . therefore the law of *onan* does not apply to him.

Likewise, also regarding Abraham our Father, who was also a High Priest, it is not relevant to say such a thing . . . therefore also the law of *onan* did not apply to him . . . and understand this.
