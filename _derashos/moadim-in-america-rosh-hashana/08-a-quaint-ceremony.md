ROSH-HASHONOH EVE

Esteemed Congregation:

"<span dir="rtl">מנהג אבותינו תורה חיא</span>".

"*Every custom, every precept observed and practised by our ancestors contains a moral doctrine.*"[^1]

A quaint ceremony marks the advent of this our solemn festival in those Jewish houses where the observance of old- time rites are still dear and highly regarded. After the Kiddush, the proclamation of the sanctity of the day, im- mediately after offering the <span dir="rtl">המוציא</span>, the blessing over the bread, the head of the family dips some Challe or fruit into honey and distributes some of it to all the members of his house, offering up the following prayer:

"*May it be Thy will, O Lord, to grant us a happy and sweet year!*"[^2]

The act expresses in a vivid and impressive form the desire which inevitably governs our heart and mind, which are confronted with the unknown changes and chances of the new year. This wish finds frequent expression in our prayers, notably in the sentence of the touching prayer:

"*Oh, our Father, our King, grant us a happy new year*",

which we utter twice a day during the Ten Days of Penitence.

It is a very natural and sensible petition. Happiness is certainly the desire of every human being. We ask for it most earnestly because the past years, with all their troubles and sufferings, still cast their dark shadows of misery upon us, and the new year, with its uncertainties, fills us with apprehension and solicitude and with a mental condition of anxiety.

Yes, it is all very natural. I would not have this prayer for happiness eliminated from our festival services, though it may seem a somewhat immoderate utterance with which to greet this sacred period which is to give the soul more of an opportunity. For happiness is not only the chief desire of man but also the purpose of our Heavenly Father. It is by happiness that we live and by happy living that we serve Him best. We read in last week's portion: Moses, speaking in the name of God, says the following:[^3]

"*I have set before thee life and death, the blessing and the curse; therefore, choose thou life, in order that thou mayest live, both thou and thy seed.*"

"*The dead cannot praise God*"[^4] calls out the Psalmist. No, nor can the miserable, whom misery inevitably stuns and maims, serve the Lord.

"*Three things cause man to act against his own convictions and against the commandments of the Lord: idol worship, temporary insanity, and poverty.*"[^5]

We cannot praise the Lord or worship Him when we are pressed by poverty and needs.

Happiness, then, let us pray for it, and yet not for happiness unqualified. We must interpret our terms and interpret them correctly. It is not wrong to ask for earthly boons, for the pleasures of the senses, for the realization of worthy hopes and ambitions; but is surely improper to ask for them alone. We shall pray for happiness in the moral sense, if we are not to put ourselves out of place by imploring our Heavenly Father in a selfish spirit.

The New Year's day (or the days of Penitence) shall inspire our yearning for the real joys of life, joys which are worthy of our prayers and the energy of our soul.

My dear friends, let us give to the phrase "A Happy New Year!" a higher meaning. Let us mean by it a prosperous time, indeed, a year of blessedness, so that all of us may be capable of exercising our human duties in righteousness and loving-kindness. Let us pray:

"*Satisfy us with thy goodness and gladden us with thy salvation, and our heart will be purified and serve you in truth.*"[^6]

That, my dear friends, is the characteristic and the con- sistent doctrine of Judaism.

And now one more remark. Let us remember what our sages said:[^7]

"*Who is happy? It is the one that is contented with his share.*"

The lives of others, which we see on their surface only, and whose joyousness may be deceptive, are often made standards of prosperity and happiness. Yet we may have far more reason for contentment than those whom we envy. So let us be wise. Let us pray to the Lord that He may grant us health, delight, and understanding so that we may well be satisfied with our share. Praying for ourselves, we must also think of our entire, race and of mankind as a whole and say:[^8]

"*Help Thy people, Israel, and bless Thy heritage, and feed them and exalt them unto all eternity!*"

Grant us, O God, an everlasting peace! And send us soon the righteous redeemer.

Amen
