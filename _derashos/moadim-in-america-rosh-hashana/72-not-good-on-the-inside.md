The story is told of a little boy who found an electric bulb on the street and came running to show it to his mother. The mother took one look at it, shook it a bit, and said, “ No, Son. Throw it away; it is not good.” The boy kept persisting, “Mommy, it is not broken, look at it; it is good.” “True”, said the Mother, “but not on the inside; it is good only on the outside.”

We know many such people, and come in contact with many such individuals, whose outward appearance bespeaks kindness and humanity, but who, upon closer inspection reveal that they, too, are not good on the inside.

The Rosh Hashonah observance which is always a stirring, inspiring experience, can easily fall into the same category if we are not careful. Outwardly, when we see a crowded Synagogue, filled by an awesome anticipation, the moving spirit of assurance and sense of spiritual uplifting can be highly misleading. This, too, can become just an external manifestation lacking internal affirmation. We must realize that religion is not a part time job but a full time experience, and that it should have meaning for all the days of our lives.

There are many reasons why the Biblical portion selected on Rosh Hashonah centers around the life of Abraham. It is not difficult to understand why, since the Patriarch, in his own life, provided a moving demonstration of our belief that religion should penetrate every life and have a lasting impact on every avenue of human endeavor. He was, indeed, a charitable man, a generous person, a kind-hearted individual, and would not have been completely satisfied with a smug contentment that they have a good Jewish heart.

Abraham did not stop at that. He was first and foremost a Jew who observed, initiated, and introduced the ritual aspects of our religion, whether it be by prayer, by the Abrahamic covenant, by the Jewishness of the home, or by one of the most exalted aspects of religion, which is empathy for fellow human beings.

All of these and more are read on Rosh Hashonah mornings in order to absorb into our being the ever important message that religion is a full time job. It acts as a measure in regulating the conduct between man and man, and its discipline teaches us behavior between man and God.

This all-engrossing and all-encompassing dominion is religious life. Religion is not only evidence here in the Scriptual reading of Rosh Hashonah, but also in the re-statement of our Oral Law, where we find that the very first Mishna combines both the Mitzvahs between man and his fellows, and man and his Creator.

The Law, the first Law registered in the Talmud, is about the reading of the Shema, the great example of our faith, the acknowledgement of the supremacy of God, and the proclamation that he is One above all. When the Mishna wants to find an example that would denote the time of the evening Shema, the sages tell us:

<span dir="rtl">משהכהנים נכנסים לאכול בתרומתם</span>

Indicating that there are certain areas of responsibility, of sharing our possessions, which go hand in hand with prayer; or as the Talmud further states, reminding us that when we proclaim our faith in God nd bow our heads before authority, we must a the same time put a hand toward the poor and needy who have but salt and bread with which to slake the pangs of their hunger.

<span dir="rtl">העני נכנס לאכול פתו במלח</span>

If we are good on the inside and outside of our lives, then the Rosh Hashonah spirit must be carried into our daily life and followed up. Every experience, whether it is a completely religious act like prayer, or a mundane matter like business…for an honest weight and a correct measure are as much part of religion as a recital of prayers and the observance of Shabbos…should be illuminated by the spirit of Rosh Hashonah. Helping the needy and truly sharing our possessions is no less a religious command than putting a Mazuzah on the door or bringing a newborn boy into the Covenant of Abraham.

Rosh Hashonah, by its very name, implies only a beginning and should teach us that its spirit must be felt in every expression of life, in every action that we undertake. With religion in our lives, we should be and can be good on the inside and outside, as we journey thru the years of our lives.

The blowing of the Shofar, the experience of Rosh Hashonah, the impact of this Holiday, and the impetus of all its rituals, is really only a Chochmo…the wise plan, the fine blueprint, but not Jewish life in toto. The latter will only follow if the pattern so beautifully developed on this sacred day becomes the practice of our daily endeavors and the motivating force behind our every day action.
