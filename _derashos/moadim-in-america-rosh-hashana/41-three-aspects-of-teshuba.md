A Rosh Ha-Shonah Sermon

Very few people are genuinely happy. Behind a mask of cheerfulness, most of us conceal various kinds of trouble: worries, fears, hatreds, suspicions, dissatisfactions and griefs.

Genuine moments of happiness joy, love confidence and self-confidence are very rare, indeed.

One man looks healthy, but he knows that he is not. Another is weighted down with heavy economic problems.

One man is deeply concerned about the future of his children. Another man's heart is constantly weeping for the loss of a dear one.

The extermination of 6,000,000 of our brethren has left all of us Jews in a permanently oppressed mood. Not even the establishment of the Jewish State could erase the horrible visions of gas chambers, mass graves and crematoria.

Anyone, anywhere in the world, who reads the newspapers or listens to the radio, is depressed by the fear of a worldwide atomic war.

The purpose of Rosh ha-Shonah is to make all these depressing thoughts, these mental and psychological burdens, vanish from our souls.

By means of Teshubah, traditions tells us, we are inscribed <span dir="rtl">בספרן של צדיקים לאלתר לחיים טובים</span> "*into the Book of the Righteous, immediately, of Good Life.*"

How does Teshubah accomplish this miraculous transformation from sadness to joy, from misery to happiness?

The term Teshubah has a three-fold significance.

In the first place, Teshubah is derived from the root "SHUB," to return. Teshubah therefore, means that we are to go back in our minds over our past lives.

The Biblical name of our holiday, <span dir="rtl">יום הזכרון</span> Yom haZikoron, "The Day of Memory" also indicates that this day is to be dedicated by us to the memory of the past.

You may ask: What possible good can we accomplish by thinking about events and deeds that can no longer be changed? Why go over the things of yesterday?

The answer is that human life consists of a series of causes and effects. Whatever you are today, you are because of things that happened yesterday. What- ever happens today is the result of things that occurred yesterday. Your character today has been shaped by the events of yesterday.

This is true of individuals and of nations. In the Land of Israel there is today, for the first time in 1878 years, a Jewish State. How did this miracle happen? Because for all these years we Jews have not forgotten Jerusalem; because 50 years ago, Theodore Herzl organized political Zionism; and because hundreds of thousands of Halutzim, with their sweat and blood and the risk of their lives revived and rebuilt the Land of Israel. What we are witnessing in Israel today is the direct result of our actions of yesterday.

Another example. In America today, large numbers of Jews are fleeing from Judaism. Hundreds of thousands of Jews deny their Jewishness and have no contact with things Jewish. They do not know what the Jewish religion and Jewish culture represent, and have no desire to learn about them. How did this state of affairs come about? What prevents our young Jews from living as Jews in free America?

The answer is that yesterday-in the past few decades-American Jews concentrated their efforts on their material advancement, paying little or no heed to the spiritual needs of our youth. We are now reaping the fruits of the seeds we had sowed yesterday.

The same principle of cause and effect applies to individuals.

If you want to understand why you have certain habits of thought and action, you must allow your memory to take you back to yesterday, perhaps as far back as your childhood.

What makes you, for example, come to the synagogue on Rosh ha-Shonah and on Yom Kippur? The fact that when you were a child your father or mother tremblingly led you to the Shul, and there you were deeply impressed by the pious atmosphere and the sincere mode of the prayers. As a result, today, no power on earth can keep you away from the synagogue on these "Days of Awe."

The Torah prescribes a wonderful method of improving our characters. Most of us labor under the illusion that our characters are fixed and unchangeable We often admit that we have certain faults, shortcomings, undesirable habits and selfish dispositions. We insist, however, that these are innate qualities. We persuade ourselves that our anti-social and anti-religious attitudes were born with us and that we cannot possibly part with them.

To these people Rosh ha-Shonah calls: do Teshubah! go back in your minds to the cause of your first deviations from the right path. By recalling, on this Yom ha-Zikoron, why, in the first place, your formed a foolish and erroneous habit, you will realize that your evil quality was not born with you. As a result, the casting away of your wrong ideas and actions, will become a lighter task.

I knew a man who committed suicide when he lost his wealth. He had told me that as a child, he often suffered humiliation on account of poverty. He decided, therefore, to dedicate his life to the pursuit of money. Without wealth, he was sure, he could have no self respect. He could enjoy life only if he had more money and a more luxurious home than most people.

Napoleon conceived his ambition to become emperor when as a youth he suffered from an inferiority complex. He was short of stature and he planned to gain self-respect by becoming master over his fellow human beings.

If these brilliant people would have devoted their talents to the service of their fellowmen, as scientists, physicians, or teachers, they would have developed a deeper self-satisfaction. Their names, too, would have become a blessing to all people and to future generations.

The Rabbis express this thought in a remarkable statement[^1]: <span dir="rtl">כל שרוח עצמו נוחה הימנו סימן יפה לו, וכל שאין רוח עצמו נוחה הימנו סימן רע לו</span> “*He who is genuinely satisfied that his actions are noble and useful, enjoys life; otherwise, he is miserable.*"

Psychologists tell us that much of our unhappiness is due to a feeling of guilt. When we are aware that we are not always doing what we ought to do, that our lives are not as noble as they ought to be, we are, in the words of the Rabbis <span dir="rtl">מלאים חרטה</span> ,"*full of regrets.*" We reproach ourselves for not being as good as others are.

By means of Teshubah we remove this burden of guilt. Our consciences become clear: we have reconciled ourselves to God.

The second connotation of Teshubah is stated by the prophet Hosea: <span dir="rtl">שובה ישראל עד ד' אלהיך</span> "Return O Israel, to the Lord, they God."

In turning to God, we may gain, in some measure, rays of Divine Wisdom.

<span dir="rtl">גדולה תשובה שמקרבת את האדם לשכינה</span>

"*The greatness of Teshubah*," Maimonides teaches[^2], "*lies in that it brings man nearer to the presence of God.*"

Once we realize that the Universe has a merciful Creator and Ruler, we will worry less about the imminent danger of global destruction.

<span dir="rtl">אל תדאג על העולם שאינו שלך</span>

"*Do not be anxious*," say the Sages to the Talmud[^3], "*about a world which does not belong to you.*"

The implication is that the Master of the Universe may be relied upon to care for it.

The Psalmist[^4] had a firm faith. He sang joyously: <span dir="rtl">אף תכון תבל בל תמוט</span> "*He established the world, that it cannot be moved.*"

Confidence in God leads to confidence in ourselves. We were created, the Torah assures us, <span dir="rtl">בצלם אלקים</span> "*in the image of God*": we were endowed with wisdom, intelligence and ability to solve our problems and to overcome obstacles. God is a <span dir="rtl">מלך חפץ בחיים</span> "*a King who desires us to live,*" and he has implanted in us a "will to live."

The human race will ultimately grope its way out of greed and egotism and ignorance, and, like the Halutzim in the Land of Israel, build a society of justice, freedom and brotherhood.

Turning to God brings us also consolation in time of grief and mourning. The Jerusalemite Talmud[^5] relates a touching incident. A certain rabbi lost his dearly beloved, brilliant, young son. When the mourning period was over, the Father braced himself and resolved, "*Henceforth,*" he said, "*the love that I had for my son, I will give to God.*"

The saintly rabbi accomplished two things. The love evoked by his son did not go to waste but was channelized into the service of God and man. Beyond that, the good deeds he performed with his new zeal served to perpetuate the name of his be- loved son.

In addition to removing our feelings of guilt and of inferiority, and in addition to giving us confidence and consolation, Teshubah has a third significance: it helps us in making plans for the future. For Teshubah has the meaning and the connotation of: to reply, to defeat an error and to establish the light of Truth.

The Rabbis meant to teach us this aspect of Teshubah when they called this holiday: Rosh haShonah, the beginning of the year. In other words, we are to face the future and we have to make <span dir="rtl">קבלה על העתיד</span> "*a blue print for our conduct in the future.*"

The spiritual weakness of American Jewry stems from the fact that we are too much concerned with the present and too little or not at all, concerned, with the future.

Parents who allow their children to work on Saturday, are interested in the present. They are loving the future. They are losing the devotion of their children to Judaism.

Children who do not get a Hebrew education may enjoy a few hours of play for the moment. In the future their ignorance of Judaism will plague their souls with bitter conflicts.

People who do not create in their homes a Jewish atmosphere by neither celebrating our holidays, nor practicing our customs, may gain momentarily the saving of a little exertion. In the end, however, their children will grow up without knowing of the joys of Jewish life.

Without feeling that we are shaping the future, our lives have no significance. The satisfaction we receive from inspiring our children and grandchildren to be loyal and learned Jews, gives us an added zest for living.

In transmitting the ideals of our fathers to children, we become the agents of an eternal idea, and thus take our places in the scheme of eternity.

In stimulating us to look backward to the past, upward to God and forward to the future, Teshubah transforms us into happy and courageous men and women.

Teshubah, according to Jewish tradition, is a cooperative affair. <span dir="rtl">הפורש מן הצבור</span> "*He who segregates himself from the community, cannot perform Teshubah.*"[^6] ) ( Our prayers for Teshubah are expressed in the plural to indicate our unity and interdependance.

Through our organized efforts, we American Jews, have done much to save the lives of our brethren in Europe. Acting together, too, we succeeded in building up the Land of Israel.

Working harmoniously and cooperatively, we will be able also to build up a better generation of American Jews. Our synagogues and educational institutions can be made to flourish and to inspire our children to be loyal, creative and proud Jews.
