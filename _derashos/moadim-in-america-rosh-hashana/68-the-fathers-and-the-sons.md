A Discourse for Rosh Hashanah

*“And you shall blow with the trumpets over your burnt-offerings, and over the sacrifices of your peace-offerings, and they shall be to you for a memorial before your God: I am Hashem your God” (Bamidbar 10:10). Yalkut Shimoni, Remez 724, and in the Sifrei, Parashas Beha'aloscha, Os 42: Rabbi Natan says, “‘And you shall blow with the trumpets’—this refers to the Shofaros; ‘and they shall be to you for a memorial’—this refers to the Zichronos; ‘I am Hashem your God’—this refers to the Malchuyos.”*

In Mishlei 7:18–20:

*“Come, let us take our fill of love until the morning; let us solace ourselves with loves. For the man is not at home; he is gone a long journey; he has taken the bundle of money with him...”*

In Sanhedrin, page 96a (cf. 96b):

*When they heard the prophets prophesying the destruction of Jerusalem, they sent word to Nebuchadnezzar, "Go forth and come." He said, "I am afraid of the Holy One, Blessed be He." They sent to him, "'For the man is not at home,' and 'man' refers to none other than the Holy One, Blessed be He, as it is said, 'Hashem is a man of war.'" He sent back to them, "He is close by and will return." They sent to him, "'He is gone a long journey.'" He sent to him, "There are righteous ones among them who will protect them." They sent to him, "'He has taken the bundle of money with him,' and 'money' refers to none other than the righteous." He sent to them, "It is possible that Israel will perform repentance and be forgiven." They sent to him, "He has already fixed a set time for them for their punishment before which repentance will not help, as it is said, 'He will come home on the Keseh day,' and Keseh refers to none other than a fixed time, as it is said, 'Blow the Shofar on the new moon, at the covered time (bakeseh) on our solemn feast day' (Tehillim 81:4)."*

In the *Akeidas Yitzchak*, Parashas Emor, Sha'ar 67, page 80, the author interprets all of this allegorically regarding the incitement of the *yeitzer hara* upon man. For the *yeitzer hara* entices man with all types of enticements—sometimes in its own form as the *yeitzer hara*, and sometimes in the guise of the *yeitzer hatov*. As Chazal said (Shabbos 63a): “*‘Rejoice, O young man, in your youth, and let your heart cheer you in the days of your youth, and walk in the ways of your heart, and in the sight of your eyes’—Rav Huna said: Up to this point are the words of the yeitzer hara; from this point forward: ‘but know that for all these things God will bring you into judgment’—these are the words of the yeitzer hatov.*”

And a wondrous moral insight flashes forth from this passage: Know that the argument of the *yeitzer hara* is potent against a person, saying, "Far be it from you to depart from this world without being encompassed and crowned in all majesty and pleasantness; it is better for your heart to choose the statement, *'Rejoice, O young man, in your youth.*' And when you reach the days of old age and hoary hair, then you shall sanctify your time as holy to Hashem, to abstain from all the delights of the world, and then you will be an unblemished burnt-offering." However, afterward in truth, when the person does reach the days of old age and hoary hair, then the *yeitzer hara* comes to him in the guise and words of the *yeitzer hatov*, and will not allow him to return from his evil path.

To what is this matter comparable? To a poor man who came to a certain town, and a scoundrel met him, greeted him, and asked about his situation. The poor man bittered his soul before him, saying, "By my master, I have eaten nothing, neither today nor yesterday, and I have no place of rest to rest even a little from my sorrow and vexation." The scoundrel said to him, "Come with me to my house, and I will give you a bed, a table, a chair, and a lamp." The poor man went with him, and the scoundrel brought him into a large inn, and told the innkeeper to give this guest everything he desired to eat and drink. The poor man imagined in his soul that the entire inn and everything within it was the property of the man who had met him.

After a few days, after the poor man had gathered his belongings and put them into his sack and travel bag to go on his way, the innkeeper grabbed his sack and his travel bag, and demanded that he pay him what he owed for his food, because that man had spoken falsely when he claimed to be the proprietor. The innkeeper took his bag as collateral. The poor man was exceedingly terrified and went outside, and behold, the aforementioned deceiver came toward him, having changed his garments, and therefore the poor man did not recognize him. The poor man related to him that which had occurred to him.

The scoundrel said to the poor man, "Behold, you have acted like one of the fools! How could you listen to the words of a malicious, arrogant scoundrel whose mouth is full of entrapment? But hear my advice: now, since your bundle and travel bag are already in the hand of the innkeeper, go, eat and drink at his establishment at least for a few more days, for your bag will not return to you anyway."

This is precisely the way of the *yeitzer hara*. In the days of youth, it smooths its words to him to commit transgressions—to enjoy the delights of this world for free. Afterward, in the days of old age and hoary hair, when the person senses that it was not for free, but rather he must inevitably pay for what he enjoyed, then the *yeitzer hara* comes in the guise of the *yeitzer hatov* and makes repentance heavy upon him. It greatly exaggerates the punishment to him, saying, "To amend is impossible for you in any manner; at any rate, since your bag is no longer in your hand, therefore, whatever your hand finds to enjoy of this world as long as you are still here in the inn, snatch and eat." And this is the meaning of: "Up to this point are the words of the *yeitzer hara*; from this point forward are the words of the *yeitzer hatov*." That is to say, the *yeitzer hara* disguises itself and speaks words appropriate to the *yeitzer hatov*, for it is to the *yeitzer hatov* that it is appropriate to say, "but know that for all these things God will bring you into judgment." Yet the *yeitzer hara* goes ahead and speaks these words, and its entire intention is that the person should despair of repentance, and should not set his heart to amend that which he ruined.

Let us return to the beginning of our words. The *yeitzer hara* entices man and says to him, "Come, let us take our fill of love until the morning; let us solace ourselves with loves." For this world is comparable to night—"the wicked shall walk in darkness." And it says to him, "Snatch from this world all the days of your fleeting life." And due to our many sins, the work of Satan has succeeded in our days, for God is not before their eyes, and the thoughts of many of us are only for vanity—"for the man is not at home." This refers to the Holy One, Blessed be He. Formerly, the Shechinah dwelt within our homes, as it is written, "*And let them make Me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them*" (Shemos 25:8). As Chazal expounded: It does not say "*within it*", but rather "*among them*" (*besocham*)—within the homes of Israel. And so formerly, the entire house of Israel was a place for the resting of the Shechinah.

In the kitchen, the Shechinah dwelt, because the Jewish housewife was meticulous in matters of the koshering of meat and the salting of meat, and she was careful to separate between dairy utensils and meat utensils. And in the dining room, the statement of the prophet was fulfilled: "*This is the table that is before Hashem*" (Yechezkel 41:22). The meal was seasoned with the washing of hands, the blessing of *Hamotzi* before the meal, and *Birkas Hamazon* after it, and they gave of their bread to the poor. There was no house where poor Torah scholars did not eat—those who sat before Hashem day and night in the study halls to meditate upon the Torah of Hashem.

And the bedroom was the Holy of Holies. The children did not give slumber to their eyelids before their mother or father recited *Kriat Shema* with them, at the very least the verse of *Shema Yisrael* and "*Into Your hand I commit my spirit.*"

And in a specific room stood a dedicated bookcase. For the master of the house who was a Torah scholar, they had volumes of Shas, Mishnayos, and other types of books—books of *Mussar* and so forth. And even for the unlearned folk, the craftsmen, they had Machzorim, Chumashim, also translated into Yiddish, volumes of *Ein Yaakov*, *Menoras HaMaor*, *Shevet Mussar*, *Kav HaYashar*. And in their leisure times, they established their learning on Shabbos and Yom Tov; every day between Mincha and Maariv, and in the evening before sleep, they recited *Kriat Shema* upon their beds, reading and reading so that they would sleep out of words of Torah, and their beds were pure and complete. To put it briefly: the house of Israel formerly was a miniature sanctuary—a dwelling place for the Shechinah in the full sense of the word, and, as it were, "*the Man*" was in His home—in the house of Israel.

But today, when they wage war with the Holy One, Blessed be He, as it were, to cast Him down from His throne—"*Hashem is a man of war*"—there has been fulfilled in us, "*for the man is not at home.*" For they have driven Him out, as it were, from the house of Israel through forbidden foods, and the Name of Hashem is not heard upon their mouths. At the time of eating, with uncovered heads, they push away the feet of the Shechinah, and their children are the children of strangers. Mezuzos are not seen upon the doorposts of their homes, and the books of the Torah of Hashem are neither seen nor found in their homes. Satan, the *yeitzer hara*, is the "*man in their home*" at present.

And even in the synagogue, which is a miniature sanctuary and is called the House of God—as Chazal expounded: "*Yet have I been to them as a little sanctuary*" (Yechezkel 11:16)—these are the synagogues and study halls in the exile (Megillah 29a)—even here Satan comes to stand in our midst to disrupt us from our service, the holy service, with foreign thoughts and offensive speech that will not be accepted. Formerly in Israel, the synagogues and study halls were places from which they would draw the Holy Spirit, and they took from there the Torah of the Living God; for the synagogues were a place of study for school children in Scripture, and the study halls for Mishnah and Talmud, as in the statement of Chazal (Berachos 8a): “‘*Hashem loves the gates of Zion more than all the dwellings of Jacob’—Hashem loves the gates that are distinguished in Halachah more than synagogues and study halls*.”[^1]

The synagogues and study halls were truly a semblance of the Beis HaMikdash formerly in Israel. In the Beis HaMikdash were treasured precious concepts and sublime teachings that could be of benefit to every person of Israel. And a sublime idea in a poetic description is brought in the statement of Chazal (Yoma 21b; Succah 28b):

"*At the hour when Solomon built the Beis HaMikdash, he planted in it all kinds of fruit trees, and they would produce their fruits in their season. And when the wind blew upon them, their fruits would fall off, and from them was the livelihood for the Priesthood.*"

Behold, one who stands in an orchard full of trees bearing sweet fruits which have already completed their ripening—yet solely because they stand at the tops of the high trees, his hand cannot reach to take them, and he cannot benefit from them. Only when the wind blows upon them does it shake them loose, and they fall to the earth, and then he benefits from them. If so, the action of the wind upon them causes that man should benefit from them. Therefore, when the Sage wished to state that within the *Beis HaMikdash* were matters enveloped with sublime and highly valuable concepts, yet as long as they did not contemplate them, they did not know of them and did not attain them—and only among those in whom the spiritual movement was awakened, which is the very action of the *ruach*, did they then see and understand the preciousness of their value and the greatness of their effect, and then the teachings had an effect upon them, and the Kohanim would be elevated in their spiritual level through these teachings.

Therefore, when he wished to depict all this in a poetic description, he clothed this idea in an allegorical garb and said: "*At the hour when Solomon built the Beis HaMikdash, he planted in it all kinds of golden dainties,*" because he enclosed there many sublime concepts and precious teachings, which are called in the language of poetry by the name "golden dainties." And it is to be known that "*and they would produce their fruits in their season*" intends that these teachings were fully matured, yet nonetheless, there were many times when many people did not benefit from them because they did not know or sense them at all, and therefore they did not benefit from them. And this was only due to the absence of the blowing of the wind, for at a time when the wind of their understanding is not awakened, they will not attain them—precisely like a time when the fruits are at the tops of the trees, that as long as the wind does not blow upon them, it is not in the power of man to attain them.

"*And when the wind blew upon them, their fruits would fall off*"—at the time when the power of the spirit was awakened within man and he began to contemplate them, then the spirit acted upon them so that they fell off, because through this spirit he attained them. And in this poetic spirit, one may designate this by the term "*falling off,*" which depicts the matter of the attainment of their teachings to man as though they fell from their place and arrived thereby to the man, just as with the fruits of the tree that stand at the top of the trees, which through the wind fall off and man can benefit from them. "*And from them was the livelihood for the Priesthood,*" because the attainment of these sublime teachings enabled them to reach a sublime level, and from this spiritual matter they were nourished and sustained with spiritual nourishment and moral livelihood.

And this was formerly in Israel. But not in those days and at this time, and not in this desolate land. And here we can say that even regarding the holy place about which it was said, "*Here will I dwell, for I have desired it; this is my resting-place for ever*" (Tehillim 132:14)—even from here the Shechinah has been exiled, to the extent that "*the Man is not at home.*" Even in the house of prayer designated for Him, "*the Man*"—this is the Holy One, Blessed be He—does not rule.

And the reason for this, "*that the Man is not at home,*" is because we have seen many men who "in their youth" were godfearing, complete, and serving Hashem, but toward their old age, his wives and his children turned his heart away from after Hashem. For since he did not raise them upon the knees of the Torah and the fear of Heaven, the result is that they guide him, against his will, upon the path of wickedness. For he is ashamed and humiliated before them to do what is good in the eyes of God, because the young people will mock him.

And about this it is said, "*For they are a very forward generation, children in whom is no faithfulness*" (Devarim 32:20). For the straightforward way is that the fathers should nurture and raise the children to Torah and to destiny, and to train them in the fear of Hashem. But if the fathers are lazy in guarding this charge of theirs, and do not teach Torah to their offspring, then an upside-down world is seen: instead of the fathers raising the children, the children nurture and train the fathers, and entice them and seduce them to turn away from after Hashem their God, Whom they served from their youth. And this is what is meant by "*For they are a very forward generation,*" for the wheel has turned—instead of fathers raising children, the children instruct the fathers for evil. And the cause and corrupt source for this is "*children in whom is no faithfulness,*" because they were not raised upon the breasts of faith from their youth through the training of the fathers; therefore has this come about.

And about this it is said, "*A seed of evil-doers, children that deal corruptly*" (Yeshayahu 1:4). For not only is their corruption within themselves—a blemish within them—but they also corrupt their fathers. Just as we see with our own eyes, that many times men who were raised upon the knees of the Torah and the fear of Heaven from their youth, and hold fast to the covenant of the Torah—yet after they raised their children and they became men who filled their bellies with foreign wisdoms and drank the cup of the poison of heresy, then the fathers also turned backward. They abandon slowly, slowly the way of Hashem in which they had walked forever, and this is through their children who rebel against the word of Hashem. And the fathers also eat sour grapes, whereby the teeth of the sons are set on edge.

For initially, the fathers remain silent when they see that their children are doing deeds that ought not to be done, saying, "*As long as there is peace and truth in my days; I cannot stir up strife constantly and make enemies of the people of his household.*" And in the end, it is these youths, emptied of mitzvos, who mock and laugh at the old father for holding fast to the Torah of Hashem, until ultimately he too is drawn after them for evil, as experience witnesses due to our many sins in these days of ours. Therefore, the children "*deal corruptly*" with their fathers.

And with this, we will explain the meaning of the variant versions of the Siddur text: "*A man should always be godfearing in secret 'and in public' (u'vagalui),*" and there is another version: "*in secret 'as in public*' (*chavagalui*)." The difference between the two versions depends on the state of the generation. In an upright generation that walks in the path of holiness, and each one is assisted by his fellow through the performance of mitzvos by virtue of one looking at his friend—which is that each one assists his fellow and rebukes his companion to strengthen faltering knees in faith and in beliefs—then the prayer needs to be regarding "*in secret,*" where there is no fear of human judgment or fear of shame, that it should be "*as in public.*"

But in a generation that is wise in its own eyes—a generation how lofty are its eyes to despise its old mother and her Torah, and to mock those who guard the religion and its ordinances—then even the most upright man among people, on his own account, will sometimes not stand the test of being insulted due to his service in holiness. He will be ashamed and humiliated to pray in front of his fellow who plays the philosopher against prayer in general, and whose own wisdom has stood by him for some time to make his meal sweet to his palate before the morning prayer. And the upright man, who grows weary of bearing the scorn and mockery of the self-proclaimed philosopher, will enter into an inner room and close the door behind him, and pray in dread and fear (fear of flesh and blood) lest his voice be heard and he become a mockery. And sometimes it happens that he will be in the house of the mocker and will find a justification for himself, saying, "*The Merciful One exempts one who is under duress,*" for through prayer his honor will be profaned, and the honor of people is great. And thus he exits outside of his boundary—the boundary of religion—due to the fear of shame (woe to that shame!).

Regarding this, the text instructs: "*A man should always be godfearing in secret 'and in public'—as in secret, so in public, he should not be ashamed before human beings who mock him in the service of the Beis (meaning, God; standard phrasing used by the Rama in his gloss to Orach Chaim 1:1)*”. And about this they said of blessed memory in *Avos*: "Be fierce as a leopard... to do the will of your Father Who is in Heaven." And let him remember the deed of Daniel, who prayed with self-sacrifice every day in public (as the verse describes, "*and his windows being open*" Daniel 6:11), and let him realize that he will not find more wisdom in his mocking fellow than in Daniel, who gave his life for prayer.

In a time of peril, in a generation that plays the philosopher, asking "What is this service to you?", it is a great war for the most upright man, against whom many fight and oppose. And then one requires great strengths to pass between the waves of the arrogant waters. And a person is in the greatest danger if the war is close to him in his own home—if the son asks, "What is this service to you?" And even though the Torah commanded to answer such sons according to their foolishness, nevertheless, it is a war. And who knows if tonight the old father will not also fall slain at the feet of this war? Many are the casualties this war has cast down, and its slain are mighty—more than all the wars that have occurred between people who differ in their opinions. For the mercy of the father upon the son is abundant, and he cannot declare him wicked when he argues with him. He will desire to find merit for him little by little, moving from the lenient to the stringent, and he will find no rest for himself in Gan Eden if his son will be in Gehinnom.

And what shall the father do if the son refuses to listen to him, and does not desire to change his way and exit from the bonfire of Gehinnom? At the very least, will he not toil with all his strength to minimize the bonfire, until with many thoughts and calculations he extinguishes the fire of Gehinnom entirely? And he will cool it down so much until he does not recognize the place of Gehinnom at all, and automatically the radiance of the light of Gan Eden is also weakened. Then, new opinions will steal into his heart: if there is no Gehinnom and Gan Eden, why should he too toil in vain? This is the outcome of the war between the father and his son—that if the hands of the father are bound with ropes of love to his son, then besides the fact that they will not triumph, he will furthermore fall slain at his feet without anyone to raise him.

However, just as the danger is great in this war, so is the rescue great before one comes to war. And that is, if he acts like the generation of the past: that at the time when the son is raised upon the knees of the Torah and the fear of Heaven according to his path and his training, and also when he ages and grows great he does not depart from it, then the abundant mercy of the father who desires the success of his son will lead him in the good path upon which the son walks, and he finds his success in the understanding of his heart in truth and simplicity. And we can apply to this the poetic phrase of Chazal: "*A son brings merit to a father*" (Sanhedrin 104a)—meaning, the desire of the person for the success of his son will lead him in the good path upon which the son walks, to do that which is good and upright himself as well.

And with this, one can state the intention of the verse, "*that you may tell in the ears of your son*" (Shemos 10:2). For through this, wherein the father tells his son the work of Hashem—to fear before Him and to cleave to Him—he too will walk in the upright path together with his son. For which reason a person must exert effort with his children in the fear of Hashem and His commandments, for not for their sake alone, but also for his own sake and his own flesh does he exert effort. And with this, one can explain the verse: "*Correct your son, and he will give you rest; yea, he will give delight unto your soul*" (Mishlei 29:17).

But we do not do so at this time, for we call upon ourselves: "*And, behold, you are risen up in your fathers' stead, an increase of sinful men*" (Bamidbar 32:14).

For if the fathers raise their children in a path that is not good from the days of youth to the days of adolescence and from there to the days of marriage, they do not have the power to do so much evil until they grow up. This is not the case if the children train the fathers for evil, Heaven forbid: since they are already adult men and individuals, then immediately and at once there are present here sinful men possessing strength, and who are wise to do evil. And this is what it says to the aforementioned corrupting sons: If you rise up in your fathers' stead to fulfill that which they did not do—to raise you for good—you instead turn your fathers for evil. Then you multiply and raise not youths, who have no power to harm, but rather sinful men great in years.

And behold, even though we have sinned in this, that we did not raise our children to Torah, at all events we can amend in part if we stand, at least, in our position—not to be ashamed before the mockers, and all the winds in the world do not move us from our pure place. But there has been fulfilled in us, due to our many sins, "*for the man is not at home*"—the Man does not rule in his home to do according to his good will; rather, his wife and his children rule over him so that he nullifies his good will before their evil will.

And the reason for all this is "*for he has taken the bundle of money with him*"—their entire objective is only money, and gold they have made into a master; it is their lord that rules over everything they have. And in particular, we can say that the Man—this is the Holy One, Blessed be He—is not in his home. When is it that the Man is not in his home? This is during the time when the Jewish man walks on a journey to a distant place, as it says, "*Praiseworthy are the upright in the way, who walk in the Torah of Hashem*" (Tehillim 119:1). For all roads are under the presumption of danger, because wayfarers sin on the roads, doing all abominations which Hashem hates; and then, due to our many sins, the Man—this is the Holy One, Blessed be He—is not in the home of a person when they walk on a journey to a distant place. And the reason is because only a bundle of money has he taken in his hand, and only for money and wealth do they long.

"*He sent to them, 'There are righteous ones among them who will protect them.' They sent to him, 'He has taken the bundle of money with him,' and 'money' refers to none other than the righteous.*" And in truth, by the help of Hashem, may He be blessed, Israel is not a widower—there are even at such a time righteous ones, foundations of the world. However, the distinction between now and the days of old is that in the earlier generations it was said: "*Your temple is like a split pomegranate*" (Shir HaShirim 4:3)—even the empty ones among you are as full of mitzvos as a pomegranate, and about them it was said, "*And your people shall be all righteous*" (Yeshayahu 60:21), because each and every one of Israel merited to cleave to mitzvos, and also to be guarded from transgressions, even though each and every one was not a chassid. But for the present, "*instead of brass I will bring gold, and instead of iron I will bring silver*."

And the distinction between silver and gold is that in truth, gold is more important than silver, but gold is not a coin except for the great, because they do not make small coins from it due to the greatness of its value. This is not the case with silver, from which they can make even small coins, of lesser value, which are within the reach of every person's hand. And this is what is meant by "*He has taken the bundle of money*"—these are the righteous ones who are compared to silver, meaning to say, the average righteous ones, such that each and every man can attain such righteousness; this has been taken from us.

But in truth, even though we have sinned throughout the entire year, nevertheless Hashem said: "*Seek Hashem while He may be found, call upon Him while He is near*" (Yeshayahu 55:6)—these are the Ten Days of Repentance, when the Holy One, Blessed be He, accepts our repentance with mercy. And this is what is meant: even though "*the Man is not at home*" and "*he is gone a long journey*"—that Hashem is far from him throughout the entire year—nevertheless, "*He will come home on the Keseh day.*" This is the day of *Keseh* (Rosh Hashanah)—"*on our solemn feast day*"—on the day when He will set into judgment all the creatures of the worlds. On that day, He will come to His home and we will crown Him over us.

However, due to our many sins, the wicked, even at the very entrance of Gehinnom, do not return in repentance. And about this, David the King, peace be upon him, spoke as one who laments:

“*Surely men of low degree are vanity, and men of high degree are a lie; if they be laid in the balances, they are together lighter than vanity*” (Tehillim 62:10).

And in the *Midrash Rabbah*, Parashas Emor, they expounded this verse concerning Rosh Hashanah. And for our path, we will say: "*Surely men of low degree are vanity, and men of high degree are a lie*"—throughout the entire year they fill their days with vanity, but if at the very least on Rosh Hashanah they would return in repentance, it would be a good thing. Yet it is not so, for even on this day, which is "in the balances to ascend"—wherein weighed and balanced are all our deeds and our thoughts, and certainly it was incumbent upon us to tremble from the fear of the awesome Day of Judgment—nevertheless, even on that day and at that hour, "*they are together lighter than vanity,*" because their design is only vanity and emptiness.

And so Solomon, peace be upon him, said (in Koheles 3:16): “*And moreover I saw under the sun, in the place of justice*”—this is this day when we stand for judgment—“*that wickedness was there; and in the place of righteousness, that wickedness was there.*” The place wherein it was our obligation to justify our deeds, there they are still wicked. And the cause for this is that as long as the sick person senses his illness, then there is hope that he will be healed by means of a bandage and a cure; this is not the case when the power of sensation has already been lost from him, for then there is no remedy for his wound.

So it is with us, due to our many sins: we have already lost the power of sensation. And we have already applied the poetic phrase from the words of David the King, peace be upon him (Tehillim 142:6–7): “*I cried unto You, O Hashem; I said: You are my refuge, my portion in the land of the living. Attend unto my cry; for I am brought very low.*”

And the parable for this: An individual came to an expert physician that he might heal his hand, for it had dried up and become paralyzed to the extent that he could not use it. The physician sat and listened to his words, holding a staff in his hand, and he raised the staff over the hand of this man. The man began to laugh, because he thought that the physician was playing with him. Then the physician said to him, "Know for yourself that you may travel back to your house; you have nothing to hope for regarding your healing, for a rising cure you do not have." The man wept and prostrated himself before him, saying, "Why did you heal so-and-so from this illness?" The physician said to him, "But know that when that certain individual came before me, I raised my staff over the back of his hand, and he cried out, 'Ah!' Then I tested and understood that there is still life in the sinews of the hand and its arteries, and he feels the pain; it was in my power to heal him and restore him to his strength. Not so you: when I struck your hand with the staff, I saw you laughing, as if I am playing with you. Whereby without a doubt, you no longer have any life or sensation of anything in this hand of yours, and what can the physician do in this case?"

The moral lesson is that in the early days, when afflictions came upon us, we felt a very great pain, and we would run and hasten to pour out our prayer in weeping and supplications before the One Who hears prayer, and our cry was to awaken upon us mercies and kindnesses by which the world is sustained and saved. Not so now, for we already do not feel the punishment and the afflictions, and we consider them as natural occurrences, to the extent that even laughter is applicable to them.

And for this, the verse complains in the name of the Congregation of Israel and says: "*I cried unto You, O Hashem*"—meaning, when I cried out, and at the time when I prostrated myself, I cried out over the afflictions and was affected by them, then "*I said: You are my refuge, my portion in the land of the living.*" Meaning to say, this was a sign for good for me, that there is within me life and good sensation, and easily can I be healed, and my portion will yet be in the land of the living. But now, "*Attend unto my cry*"—meaning to say, hear now that I am singing \[; behold, from this it has become clarified to me that "*I am brought very low,*" and there are no longer senses within me to feel any pain. And if so, Heaven forbid, there would no longer be hope for us.

Therefore comes the Shofar to remind and awaken us, as in the statement of the Rambam, of blessed memory (Hilchos Teshuvah 3:4): "*Awake, you sleepers, from your sleep!*" And this is the intention of our statement: "*'And you shall blow with the trumpets'—this refers to the Shofaros,*" for it awakens and rouses us from our slumber through this. "*'And they shall be to you for a memorial'—this refers to the Zichronos,*" so that "*we shall remember*" that we are sick and require a healing. And through this, "*'I am Hashem your God'—this refers to the Malchuyos,*" that we should crown Him over us to serve Him with a complete heart.

And this is what we pray: "*Heal us, Hashem, and we shall be healed; save us, and we shall be saved.*" And the intention is that we have a double illness: 1) that we are sick and require a healing, and 2) also that we do not know that we are sick, and yet we require a healing.

And it is known that there is a distinction between the synonymous terms *yesha* and *ezer*. And so it is written in the book *Redifa Maya*: the root of *yesha* and *ezer*—*ozer* applies to a person who participates with the one being helped, or with someone else, in some assistance; and in the vernacular, *ozer* is translated as *mithelfer*. *Moshia* applies to the savior who alone performs the work of salvation, and there is no helper with him; and in the vernacular, *moshia* is translated as *aroizhelfer*.

And so are also the words of the Maharsha in Chiddushei Aggados (Sanhedrin 95a): "*This is that which is written: 'But Abishai the son of Zeruiah succored him ' (II Shmuel 21:17).*" And his language there is that the word *ezer* in every place applies when the one being helped also rescues himself, except that he cannot rescue himself entirely except with help; thus far his words.

And the truth is that even in a case where it is not within the power of the one being helped to assist even in part, if the one helping him does not help alone but rather in partnership with some entity, this too is called *ezer*. But *teshuah* applies to the *moshia* alone, without partnership. And for this reason, the verse stated in the name of Hashem: "*and beside Me there is no savior (moshia*)" (Hosea 13:4), and it did not say "*beside Me there is no helper* (*ozer*)," for it is not so; but on the contrary, for the primary term of help was established for the actions of man: "*They help every one his neighbor*" (Yeshayahu 41:6). For man is political by nature, because all are partnered in the action of help, which is through body or through money, and there is no distinction between them except between much and little. But that a man should save his fellow without any participation has no reality. For the wealthy man helps the poor man in his time of distress by means of the partnership of possessions; and the physician by means of the partnership of drugs and medical instruments; and the king by means of the partnership of his people. And therefore, without the Holy One, Blessed be He, there is no savior in its absolute truth.

However, *moshia* is sometimes stated also regarding a human being who saves, relative to the one being saved; and that is in a place where the one being helped does not find himself helped even in part—then the one helping him is designated by the term *moshia*, and it is only by way of metaphor. And therefore the verse stated: "*Put not your trust in princes, nor in the son of man, in whom there is no help* " (Tehillim 146:3), and it did not say in whom there is no assistance. And that is because King David, peace be upon him, inclined the ear of the one who trusts in man that it is foolishness and silliness to believe that the man upon whom he trusts has the power in his hand to do good with him because he is a wealthy man, a master of properties. For his reliance is the reliance of a broken reed, since man has no *teshuah* "*for himself*"—meaning to say, that he should save himself without any object assisting him, and there is no reality for man except *ezrah*, and that is through the medium of his possessions. And how, if so, can one trust upon this? Perhaps robbers of the night will come upon him; behold, they will steal until they have enough and will leave nothing in his hand. And this is an additional reasoning over that which it says afterward: "*His spirit departs,*" etc.

Let us return to our subject. We pray: "*Heal us, Hashem*"—and what will be the healing? "*And we shall be healed*"—that it should be our will to be healed. For in truth, we do not feel our illness and we do not do anything for our healing, like the sick person who does not feel his illness. Therefore, "*save us*" (*hoshi'einu*) without our assistance, "*and we shall be saved*" (*ve'nivashe'ah*), as in the statement of the verse: "*and beside Me there is no savior.*"

And after we pray the blessing of Healing with this intention—that He should purify our heart and remove the veil wrapped over our face—we properly juxtapose to it the blessing of the Years: "*Bless for us this year and all its kinds of produce for good.*" For there is produce—bread and grain—for the body, and there is also sustenance from Heaven, which are mitzvos and good deeds for the soul. And for this we pray: "*Bless for us this year 'and all' its kinds of produce*" specifically. Not only produce of bread from the earth, but also from Heaven may the Holy One, Blessed be He, bestow bounty upon us—Amen.
