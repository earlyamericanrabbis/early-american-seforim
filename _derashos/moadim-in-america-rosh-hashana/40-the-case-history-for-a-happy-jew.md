Rosh Ha-shonah Sermon

If we were to select one phrase from the Rosh Hashonah prayer book, the <span dir="rtl">מחזור</span>, that would best express what most people in the congregation earnestly pray for, that phrase would be <span dir="rtl">חיים טובים</span> "good life."

What is it that people call good life? Judging by the daily activities of the Average American Jew, his ideas of what comprises good life are two things: 1) to earn a good living or even good money; 2) to have a great deal of pleasure, usually physical pleasure. Give the average American Jew these two things, good profits and a good time, and he is satisfied. He will pay dues to the Congregation, to the Zionist organization, to the B'nai Brith. He may also contribute to the United Jewish Campaign and to other charities. He will go to services on the High Holidays. All this, however, has nothing to do with his conception of good life. These are duties, but they in no way are allowed to interfere with the real business of living.

Today, in taking stock of our lives, we ought to investigate and inquire whether the pursuit of money and pleasure is sufficient to make life complete and satisfying.

It seems to me that the life of the American Jew has three shortcomings. The first thing that strikes us in learning the daily life of the average American Jew is that he is not really enjoying life. The average American Jew is bored to death with life. Outside of his work and his diversion, life to him is empty. We live because the organism of the body is functioning. I believe that you will agree with me that if, God forbid, every inhabitant in the U.S. would have to state the purpose of his life, in order to continue to live, the population of the U.S. as a whole would be considerably reduced. Although these observations are equally true of the general population of the United States, we, here, are concerned with curing ourselves.

An index to the boredom of American Jews, is the amount of time they consume in killing time. The American Jew is an expert in killing time. In order to kill time the American Jew plays cards, or engages in drinking, or in watching games and shows. If he is not doing any of these things, the average American Jew is bored stiff.

If we will look a little deeper into the mind of the average American Jew, we will note a certain nervousness. This nervousness is often the result of jealousy and resentment. He is jealous and envious of people who have more money or pleasure than he has.

He is resentful because he thinks that others are jealous of whatever wealth and pleasure he has. The third shortcoming in the life of the average American Jew is a little more complex. We all honor and respect someone who has given something to humanity. The memories of George Washington, Abraham Lincoln, Franklin Roosevelt and Theodore Herzl are dear to us because they devoted themselves selfishly to their fellow human beings. Unfortunately, however, we think that we live in a kind of world where we must be egotists. In our hearts we know that we would like to take as much as possible and to give as little as possible. Consequently, how can we have respect for our own selves? As a result, many people suffer inwardly, because they entertain for their own selves a secret contempt. Since we love ourselves, we try subconsciously to justify our selfishness. We develop a defensive mechanism to soothe our conscience. We pretend that we believe in a philosophy of individualism. We claim that every man should be for himself. How many times have you heard people say: "I achieved what I have because I worked. Let everybody be for himself. If the poor fellows has no brains, or no luck or no education" and we might sometimes add, "if he is more scrupulous in business that's his tough luck."

This attitude of so-called individualism, involves us into something very unpleasant. This type of individualism leads to hatred; hatred for those who are less fortunate—for the poor, for the weak, for the unsuccessful. It leads us also to hate those whom we consider, justifiably or not, as our competitors.

Every psychologist knows that a person who hates is miserable. He is torturing himself mentally. Only the person who loves is happy. The more people we love, the happier we are.

This leads us to our main question: How does one find happiness? What is the secret of <span dir="rtl">חיים טובים</span> “good life?”

The rabbis never talked about happiness. They discussed only duty and responsibility to God and to man. Yet we read in various parts of Rabbinic literature, of one person whom people of all types, Jews and non-Jews, found as being always cheerful and happy. That man was R. Judah, the son of Rabbi Ilai. Let me refer to one such passage in the Midrash[^1]:

<span dir="rtl">גוי אחד ראה את ר' יהודה ב"ר אלעאי חמתיה אפוי נהרין. אמר</span> : <span dir="rtl">הדין גברא הדא מתלת מלין אית ביה, או שתוי חמר או מוזיף ברביתא או מרבי חזירי. (ולהלן על ר' אבוה אמרו, דאשכח סימא כלו' אוצר)</span>. <span dir="rtl">שמע ר' יהודה ב"ר אלעאי, א"ל: תיפח. רוחיה דההוא גברא, דחדא מן תלתיהון לית בי. לא מוזיף ברביתא אנא... ולא מרבי חזירי אנא</span>.. <span dir="rtl">ולא שתוי חמר אנא, דאפילו ארבע כסיה דאנא שתי בלילא פסחא, אזיק לרישי מן פסחא לעצרתא</span>. <span dir="rtl">א"ל ועל מה אפך נהירין</span> ? <span dir="rtl">א"ל אורייתא היא דמנהרא אפוי דכתיב חכמת אדם תאיר פניו</span>.

“*An idolater once saw R. Judah b. R. Ilai, and noticing that his face shone, exclaimed, “This man is one of three things: He is either intoxicated, or a usurer or he inherited a great fortune. R. Judah b. R. Illai heard the remark and said: I am none of these things. Money I have none. I only drink wine for kiddush and havdolah; and the four cups of wine I drink on the night of Passover give me a headache from Pesach to Shevuos. “Why then, is your face so bright?” the pagan asked, and the Rabbi answered, “My study of the Torah brightens my face,” as it is written: ‘a Man’s wisdom makes his face to shine.’* ”

R. Judah’s answer gives a clue to the manner in which you and I can find <span dir="rtl">חיים טובים</span> genuine happiness.

It is true that any <span dir="rtl">בעל מלאכה</span> anyone who earns his livelihood by the labor of his hands in a trade which he loves, is undoubtedly happy. The Psalmist said <span dir="rtl">יגיע כפיך כי תאכל אשריך וטוב לך</span>. If you eat of the labor of your hands, you shall be happy and it shall be well with you. But working at a trade, or in business or in a profession requires only the application of our hands and brains. The soul, our real personality, is untouched. Earning a living a practical affair. Our idealism remains hungry.

Of the study of the Torah we are told that “whether one <span dir="rtl">אחד המרבה ואחד הממעיט ובלבד שיכוין לבו לשמים</span> knows more or less, what matters is that he should concentrate his heart to heaven." As a result of studying the Torah, we learn the art of <span dir="rtl">לכוין לבו לשמים</span> of looking at the world from the point of view of God-as if we were looking down upon the earth from heaven. Looking down from heaven, all men and all nations seem equal. Looking down from heaven, it seems ridiculous for any man or nation to claim, "I am superior over the rest of the world." Looking down from heaven, it seems illogical that the Arabs should have millions of unsettled and uncultivated land, while the Jewish people should wander around homelessly. It seems outrageous that a man sitting in London, titling himself Foreign Minister, should dispose of the lives of thousands of fellow human beings.

Looking from heaven, all people are in the same boat. If there is no security for the Jews, there will be no security for anyone.

Another thing we learn from the Torah is,**'**<span dir="rtl">אהבת ד</span> to serve God with love. We develop a new kind of pleasure, the pleasure of doing good. A man who enjoys doing good, enjoys life. He feels that he has a purpose in living. A man who has a purpose in life is happy.

If you will allow me to reminisce, I shall relate an unforgettable incident, that will illustrate another advantage of the study of the Torah.

One night at two o'clock, on the Pacific Ocean, our ship was torpedoed. We got into little boats and we were to climb on another ship. The ladder, made of rope, was about three to five stories high. I had never climbed a high ladder. This time, however, it was a matter of life and death. To make an understatement, I was extremely uncomfortable. Every second was eternity. Suddenly, I found myself repeating a Mishnah that I had studied in cheder at the age of seven. For five or six minutes, as I was climbing the rope, I was reciting a Mishnah in the usual Talmudic tone, over and over again, I almost forgot that I was climbing a high rope in mid-ocean. I imagined that I was, instead, a little boy studying Mishnah Bava Metzia in cheder.

At that moment, the study of the Torah served me as an escape from a terrible reality. You and I are bound to meet in life with situations which we find difficult to accept. All the world knows that reading familiar passages from religious literature, as the Psalm and the Talmud, tides us over such moments of sorrow and catastrophy.

Do you see now why <span dir="rtl">ותלמוד תורה כנגד כלם</span> study of the Torah is the greatest duty of all? Do you realize now why every Jew is supposed to be a student? And why <span dir="rtl">והגית בו יומם ולילה</span> why every Jew must spend some part of each day and evening in reading or contemplating words of Torah.

It is interesting to note that Rabbi Judah b. R. Ilai, who was the happiest man in his generation was also a poor man. It is related that one winter day at an important prayer service, he was absent from the synagogue. When it became known that the reason for his absence was that he did not have a coat, the president of the Sanhedrin, Rabbi Simeon ben Gamlaiel, sent him a coat as a gift. R. Judah, however, refused the gift. He lived only on what he earned.

Happiness does not depend on the size of one's bank account. Self-respect gives one more satisfaction and happiness than all the comforts and luxury in the world.

Of R. Judah it is related that even though he was not the most brilliant scholar of his age, he was [^2]<span dir="rtl">ראש המדברים בכל מקום</span> elected to be "the spokesman on all occasions." Why? It seems to me the reason was that he studied under various teachers. In his youth he sat the feet of the conservative R. Eliezer and R. Tarfon, who refused to change one iota of the tradition. Later he became a disciple of the creative R. Akiba; who exercised great independence in the interpretation of the Law.

In this manner, R. Judah acquired an appreciation of, and a respect for, various points of view. The man with a single tracked mind will get into many unnecessary conflicts. A broadminded, open-minded man will iron out difficulties and increase peace and happiness among his fellow men.

Another trait recorded of R. Judah was that he was extremely modest. What is the meaning of modesty? A certain Jewish soldier used to irritate his fellows by constantly boasting of how clever he and the other members of his family were, how much money they made, and how fortunate they were in evading dangerous chores in the army. I explained to the gentile soldiers who complained about him that this fellow was not proud. On the contrary, he lacked self-respect. Since he did not know of any reason why others should respect him, he told his neighbors about his shrewdness and his wealth. He thought he was thus supplying them with reasons as to why they should respect him.

When the Rabbis tell us <span dir="rtl">מאד מאד הוי שפל רוח</span> "*Be exceedingly modest*," they mean: Make your life useful and meaningful so that you should naturally develop inner satisfaction and self-respect. When a person has self-respect, he is modest. He does not feel the need of publicizing his virtues, real or imaginary.

R. Judah lived during the Hadrianic persecutions, in 135 C.E. when every Rabbi in Palestine was killed by the Romans. R. Judah was one of the five disciples of R. Akiba who survived. When the Emperor Hadrian died and the Jews were allowed to study the Torah, these five disciples sent a message to all the communities in Galilee[^3]: <span dir="rtl">כל מי שלמד יבא וילמד וכל מי שלא למד יבא וילמוד</span> **“***All those who had studied should now make themselves useful and become teachers. All those who had not studied should come and learn*." In this manner, they established schools all over Palestine and reorganize the Sanhedrin.

The facts that he had a share in reviving Judaism must have added to the happiness of R. Judah.

In our time again, the centers of Judaism in Europe have been destroyed. The Torah is again in danger of being forgotten.

You and I may add to our happiness by contributing our share to the revival of Judaism in this country. We can add another source of happiness to our lives: the joy of giving-giving of our time, money and interest to a great cause.

On Rosh Hashonah, we pray for two things: for <span dir="rtl">חיים טובים</span> and for <span dir="rtl">תשובה</span> good life and repentance. The two are inseparable. We can have a good life, we can have peace with ourselves and our neighbors, only when we lead our lives under the guidance of the Torah.

[^2]: Berachot 61; Shabbat 33, Menachot 103
[^3]: Shir HaShirim Rabbah 2:13
