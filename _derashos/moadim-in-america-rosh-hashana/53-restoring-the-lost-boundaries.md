Second Day Rosh Hashono

IN THE HAFTORO FOR THE day,¹ the prophet Yirmiyahu portrays the homeless children of Israel by means of an image that has moved every generation. By means of prophetic vision, he peers into the future and sees them being marched off by the conqueror’s guards into exile. On the highway they pass Rachel’s Tomb, ever venerated as a shrine. As they pass “Kever Rachel,” a voice is heard: it is the voice of mother Rachel weeping for the sad fate of her children. In response to her weeping, a heavenly voice is heard which consoles her and the wanderers with the promise: “Veshovu vonim li-gevulom: And your children will return to their borders,” or boundaries.

As I contemplated the meaning of these words for our own day, enlightenment came to me from an unexpected source. It was a picture from Israel showing a map on the main building of a new colony which the postman had just delivered. The map was designed to show the number of countries represented by the builders of this colony. Topping it was the banner heading: veshovu vonim li-gevulom.

These prophetic words, it should be added were not limited in their meaning to the physical return of our people to their own borders. The words of the prophet brought confidence in times of despair, when the misguided and confused cut themselves off from their people, deserted their faith, and joined the ranks of our antagonists. It was then that our people found in these words a source of comfort, assuring them as they did that ultimately “the children will return to their own borders”, religious, cultural, and social.

The question of boundaries in the spiritual and moral sense is again being raised. From within and without we are being challenged to justify the setting up of boundaries within a democracy. Why must we be different—why do we have to send our children to a Jewish school, why do we have to buy our meat at a Jewish meat market? Why erect boundaries around our lives? These questions touch on the very foundations of our faith, and upon their solution depends the very destiny of our people.

The problem of boundaries which separate one group from the other is not limited to ourselves alone. The movie Lost Boundaries dramatized an aspect of this problem as it affects the members of the colored race. The story concerns a young doctor in a small New England town who in a short time endeared himself to all the townspeople because he was a dependable friend, whom neither fire nor high water could keep from helping those that needed his help. Everything went well until the war broke out and the doctor volunteered for the navy where he was first accepted with high rating, but later rejected when his Negro ancestry was discovered. The most pitiable victims were the children of this doctor, a boy and a girl who were never told of their Negro origin. When the truth did come out there was a real revolution in the heart and soul of these children.

The picture which is based on the actual history of such a case, strikes you as with a sledge hammer—indeed, it could and did happen here. But as a Jew you come away not alone with a feeling of raging indignation that this could happen, and if boundaries are erected against the Negro, they could and are being put up against the Jew as well; you also feel that a mistake, a horrible one, was committed by the parents who had been concealing vital facts from their children. And the parents admit it. The sins of the parents who failed to tell their children about their ancestry were visited upon their children in the form of spiritual anguish and torment of soul.

It is useless to act as if there were no boundaries. It is not smart to fly in the face of facts. Parents who gloat over the fact that their children are growing up in total ignorance of Judaism, and thereby escaping the burdens of Jew ish life, are paving the way for disillusionment, frustration, and shock in their children, and in extreme cases, also of self-hatred. To educate a child Jewishly is an investment in mental hygiene, and inoculates the child against nervous disorders and upsets.

Many of us cling to notions of America which are totally false. This America which is a land of the free is also a land of boundaries. There are fraternal lodges, clubs, societies, and of course, religious organizations, which include some and definitely exclude others. Why is it then that some of us are so nervous about the activities within our own boundaries? We have among us such who tremble and shake when they hear about a Yeshiva or parochial school, as if such institutions were nests of subversive activity inimical to America! We strengthen America and its institutions by upholding the schools and seminaries which seek to disseminate wider and deeper knowledge of our faith and culture. America which drew its first inspiration from the Bible is given new vigor when young people are saturated with its teachings.

Let us return to our boundaries, not only because others crowd us out of theirs, but because to be a Jew and maintain Judaism is good for us and good for the world. Instinctively we all share in our people’s will to live. Mother Rachel, symbol of the Jewish people weeps for her chil dren who desert her. In order to keep alive we must maintain our boundaries, not so much to exclude outsiders, but to protect those within the fold. This is one of the purposes served by Kashruth, as Chief Rabbi Isaac Herzog, during his recent visit to America said, in addressing the convention of the Rabbinical Council of America: “Assimilation begins in the kitchen.” The Talmud Torah makes life within the boundaries significant, while the synagogue endows it with the strength and fervor derived from a vital faith.

Life within the boundaries is life at its noblest and best, because it means abiding by the pattern and principles of a divine code, the Torah. Today we realize that the words of the prophet, “Veshovu vonim li-gevulom” were not mere rhetoric. Their fulfillment in our own day in the ingathering of the exiles within the borders of Israel attest to their divine origin. The other aspect of the prophecy, that of the spiritual return of our people in all lands to their own spiritual boundaries will be fulfilled just as surely.

Divrei Mordechai
