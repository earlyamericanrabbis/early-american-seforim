Religion is usually expressed as the need of man to commune with his Maker, and to feel the closeness and nearness of his Creator who is omnipresent and omnipotent for all time to come.

On Rosh Hashonah, as we pinder a little more deeply, a little more fervently, and as we follow the reading from the Holy Scriptures, It is evident that not only does man want and need his maker, but in a like measure, God wants man, and wants him to possess his world, to conquer it, and conduct himself that his actions here on earth will give credence and meaning for the purpose of creation, substance and content to the divine design for human actions.

There were times in history when God wanted men and found him in the things He sought. When Abraham appeared on the threshold of history, he was the answer to the great divine searching for the nearly perfect mortal period as our Rabbis so fittingly interpreted the Prophet’s questions:

<span dir="rtl">מי העיר ממזרח צדק יקראהו לרגלו, מי העיר ממזרח זה אברהם שבא ממזרח להאיר את העולם</span>

Abraham appeared, in his constant seeking, his ever ready devotion, his ceaseless offerings, and in his all-encompassing thought, he fulfilled and personified the attributes and characteristics which made him acceptable in the eyes of the Almighty.

There were other times in our people's history when the other prophets spoke of God's desire to find man, and find him for a specific purpose, only to be told by the Novi, that the search was fruitless and the plea unanswered. In the midst of the Babylonian Exile, and in the valley of the shadow of tears which accompanied the first portion of that historic experience, Yechezkel exclaimed:

<span dir="rtl">ואבקש בהם איש גודר־גדר ועמד בפרץ לפני בעד הארץ לבלתי שחתה ולא מצאתי</span>

Quotation mark the word of the Lord came to me and I sought for a man amongst them who should build up the wall and stand in the breach before me for the land, that I should not destroy it; But I found none.” A very sad, a very disheartening, and a very discouraging picture.

Here we have two contrasts of God seeking men, 1 resulting in success beyond compare with the arrival of Abraham; And the other search to protect the city of God, but alas no protector was found. It is right to question, in the view of our present state of world affairs, whether God indeed will find the man and men he seeks, and whether you and I and all those who might per adventure fit the description will be ready and anxious to respond to this heavenly search.

Many might say that the currently fashionable religious revival is the answer to God's search, and we should accept the resurgence of religious interests as just the situation which fits the Prophet’s plea. One does not have to delve very deeply into our current situation to discover that what we have is indeed a renewed and revived interest in things Jewish; Whether one could call it religious revival is highly doubtful. A religious revival that expressed itself in a longing and yearning for God. A religious revival would find expression in the declaration of Amos, the humble shepherd of the hills, “Behold, days will be coming,” sayeth the Lord, “when I will send a great famine to the land, not a hunger for bread nor a thirst for water, but a hunger to hear the word of God.”

Is there such a hunger today? Are people streaming impatiently to adult institutions, classes and groups, to head the words of our great teachers, and to influence their thinking and lives by these immortal precepts? Even when such information is sought, even when curiosity is expressed and interest demonstrated, there cannot be and will not be what is considered a truly religious revival until that which at first is regarded as *information* becomes gradually instead, *transformation*.

Jewish learning was never sought for, intrinsically, for its own sake, but merely as a means of preparing for the better life and the more perfect human society. Just recently, a survey by one of our leading magazines addressed to outstanding business men, resulted in the noteworthy and very distressing development that a preponderant majority of people interviewed, while professing a great admiration for religion, and while offering ever increasing sums for its support, boldly admitted that this new-found interest in religion made no impact upon their daily lives and had no influence whatever upon their business mores and ethics.

Tomorrow’s religion cannot be defined in a sanguine aura unless it will signify that man becomes a co-partner of the Divine, and will give himself, as Abraham so convincingly did, for the ideals which religion interprets and for the purposes which it promulgates. Tomorrow’s religion will not call for nor accept our current interpretation, whereby religion is merely a method to use as a tool for man’s advantage, as a therapeutic method by which the stresses of our age are lessened. Tomorrow’s religion should be the vehicle by which we can show our desire to serve God and our desire to observe his laws, rather than the situation to which we have degenerated, where conversely,,,,, we make God and His Torah serve our ends.

A critic recently defined the newly rising structures that dot American suburbia as an expression of an architectural revival and a way of life whereby man spends five days of the week in search of a livehood, one day at least in search of relaxation, and one hour of one day in search of a religion… but this religion he wants, unlike his cocktails, diluted. These may be sharp words, but this is the feeling that one receives. People, who escape the tensions and frictions, the problems and realities of crowded streets and congested tenements, want to close their eyes to the realities and to the problems of the age we live in.

Religion, as the most expressive factor and dominating force in our lives, wants us to be ever aware of these danger spots, of these blind spots, with which our society must grapple. Tomorrow’s religion, to be true to its name, will not dare turn away and close its eyes to those complex and disturbing elements in our society, but will instead pray with the Poet,

> *“Open my ears to the music and let me thrill to Spring’s first flutes and drums, But never let me dare forget the bitter ballad of the alums.”*

Tomorrow’s religion indeed will be best expressed by the knowledge of God’s search for man, and the knowledge of man’s answer that he is ready, if it is to be worthy of that name.

Recently, I read the report of a social worker who described the mental state and psychological attitude of children who came from broken homes, and were placed with understanding and sympathetic people in private homes, contrary to the custom of institutionalizing them. From time to time, the mothers would come, particularly on weekends, to visit these children, and one little girl told the social working, “When my mommy comes she takes me to the store and buys me things. My mommy would by me anything I ask for, but it is *her* that I want.”

We cannot hope to express tomorrow’s religion and we cannot hope to approximate the ideals and hopes of mankind that have permeated our consciousness since the time of Abraham, by reducing our religion to a series of placating purchases of fine masses of stone, of building awe-inspiring structures, of raising imposing edifices to the glory that is God. Rather, we can best express our religion of tomorrow, if by our thoughts, our deeds and our desires, we plant in our fellow men’s hearts a hope for a better world, a hope for a world filled with the pristine beauty of the beautiful word. A hope for a world where man will answer God’s call by serving his fellow creatures in empathy and consideration, by returning to his fellow man his own respect for himself and a knowledge of his innate worth. Tomorrow’s religion will serve tomorrow’s man cleansed of all that makes him unworthy and insignificant. Tomorrow’s religion will belong to the man of the future, whose vision will truly see a world of honesty and compassion, whose world will be peopled by men worthy of the name Man.
