In the process of educating American Jewry towards the assumption of its obligations and responsibilities in these days of historic import, it is well that we remind ourselves that in spite of the admittedly high standard of giving which is being established throughout the country most of us are still far removed from the standard of sacrificial giving and in its truer sense we don’t know the meaning of sacrifice. Surely the average American Jew would indignantly deny this allegation but we need only to point to the experience of a large city where the following incident took place:

The leaders of the community wanted to place six Jewish war orphans, brands plucked from the fire, who were brought from overseas with the hope of giving them homes that would be in a measure make them forget the sorrows and tragedies of their most unhappy youths. Of the six thousand Jewish families in this particular locality, not one came forward to open its doors and unlock its heart to such a homeless and painridden little creature. Our proud feelings and boastful assertion that we are humane, charitable, and devoted to the cause of our people suffer a frightful shock when the whole truth is registered, the truth which affirms that whilst we are willing in a measure to share our possessions with the less fortunate of our brethren we are in no way prepared to make sacrifices in the full meaning of that word in the face of the overwhelming tragedy that enveloped our people. Certainly the concept of sacrifice must include more than charitable contributions. If ever the Biblical command ‘*Adam kee yakriv mekem*’ was to be taken literally, the time is now, for in making an offering we are called upon by the demands of the hour that the sacrificial offering shall in a deeper sense come fromUS, that it should entail a sacrifice of our time, efforts, comfort, luxury, and privacy. Only with this method of giving will we have exhausted the term sacrifice in its truest connotation, a method of self-sacrifice which has been demonstrated by some inspired few in most of our communities but which still is an untried expedient as regards the large segments of the American Jewish community.

In shining contrast is the example set by the Jewish community of Palestine, which clearly illustrates the limit to which people of vision will go to obtain their ideals and reap their hallowed objective. The resolute band of pioneers that came from the Holy Land to the shambles of Europe to guide the pitiful remnant homeward, the insistent demand of the Yishuv that the gates of Palestine be opened to European Jewry, their espousal of the cause of suffering Israel in defiance of the former mandatory government have brought them well nigh to the brink of disaster at times. Yet, in spite f it all, their undaunted spirit was not broken and they continued to fight every inch of the way for those were tossed on the turbulent seas of homelessness caused by a wicked tempest of cruel barbarism. It is not too far fetched to say that the results so far attained justify the spirit of self-sacrifice that they so completely displayed and with which they must inspire us if we too, want to be judged worthy of the greatest gain registered by our people in recent months.

Coupled with the heroes of Israel who fashioned a flower garden from the desert and a loving homeland from the barren dunes, neglected for centuries, are those immortal sons of American Jewry who offered themselves for the service and safety of our sorely tried brethren. The men on the Exodus, 1947, and the countless others on numerous other Haganah ships are the poets of the Lord who have written large the epic of their times shove the dying embers of the world dust.

II

The Biblical reading assigned for today is centered around the fateful march of our patriarchs to Mount Moriah. Scripture quotes Isaac as he turned to his heavy-hearted father and inquisitively queried; *hineh v’ho-aitsim, v’ayeh ha-she l’Olah*. The Rabbis of the Midrash quaintly observe that when the author of the Passover Hagadah referred to one of the four sons as a wise and an understanding child, he had the patriarch Isaac in mind at this critical juncture of his career. Indeed to the author of this Midrashic gem it was clear, *chacham-zeh Yitzchak she-omar v’ayeh ha’she l’olah*.

It will not be too far fetched to suggest that the meaning of this question, the significance of the thought behind these simple words is cloely tied up with our problem as proposed in this discussion. Isaac cautioned that there are times when the fire and wood, the material contribution and physical substance are not enough, that there are times in human experience when the compelling need of the hour carries us far beyond the customary mode of sacrifice and the demands of the circumstances are such that their fulfillment will come about only through active participation with our hearts, with our souls, and with our entire being.

The obligations confronting us currently will not be discharged with financial contributions alone, however generous they may be. To satisfy even in the least the urgency of our responsibilities we will need give ever more time to the understanding of Jewish problems and to acquaint ourselves with the basic elements of Jewish culture. We still need to dedicate ourselves with earnestness towards the furtherance and cultivation of Jewish interests and ideals in the same measure as our more devoted brethren give of themselves for all things for all things sacred to us.

Lest one survey the wide program of activities in Jewish fields of endeavor and consider the same a satisfactory example of an active and dynamic communal program we must emphasize that there has been altogether too much emphasis in organized American Jewish life on activities that bear the stamp of negation and escape. Too much attention has been paid to negative attainments an too little on positive values. Important as the fight against anti-Semitism is, the winning of that fight will not solve the problem of Jewish survival. To insure this attainment we will perforce need greater concentration on constructive values in Jewish education, religious endeavor, and communal responsibilities.

III

This program of intensified self giving may appear as somewhat too demanding to the average observer but to meet the great challenge of our privileged times demands historic decisions. Especially we, Jews of this blessed land of freedom, have every reason and every cause to dedicate ourselves joyfully to the tasks that confront us. Truly we are in the position of the humble disciple of the venerated saint of Bardicheve, who was asked by a trusted pupil to teach him the ways whereby he may discipline himself to accept the will of God in sorrow as well as in joy, thus to fulfill Talmudic dictum

<span dir="rtl">כשם שמברכין על הטוב כך מברכין על הרעה</span>

He was directed to the poorest Jew in town, a humble water-carrier who lived on the outskirts of the village in a most impoverished state and who, nevertheless, was a most cheerful individual, always ready to thank the Almighty for his grace and goodness. When the humble water-carrier was asked to teach the secret of his philosophical joy to the pious disciple, the former exclaimed with amazement: “There must be a mistake somewhere, the Tzaddick could not have send you to me to learn how to thank God in sorrow, for God was always good to me and I have always thanked him amidst joy.”

Sisters and brothers, God has been good to us, our homes and countrywide were spared the ravages of war, our sons and daughters have, by and large, returned, and we are the beneficiaries of a material prosperity at least for the present. Is it too much to ask that we thank God amidst hoy? – To thank Him with a heart that overflows with gratitude? Let us indeed demonstrate our thanks with a holy resolve to rise to the heights demanded of us by destiny and by the performance of such deeds and actions that will stands the test of time and the judgment of history so that when our response to the need of this generation will be weighed on the scales of the future, those while will then judge us will rise with reverence and call us blessed.
