Discourse for the First Day of Selichos

*“Rabbi Chanina ben Chachinai says: One who awakens at night, or who travels on the road alone, and turns his heart to idleness—behold, this person is liable for his life.”* (Pirkei Avos).

According to its plain meaning, the Tanna is not dealing with three separate matters wherein a person becomes liable for his life, but rather with two; and that which he says, "*and turns his heart to idleness,*" refers back to both of them. And this is what he means to say: One who awakens "at night"—at a time when everyone is stretched out upon their beds, and there is no one to disturb him from his service, the work of Heaven—and similarly, at a time when he travels on the road "alone," and there is no one to confuse his thoughts with idle chatter, and despite this, he turns his heart to idleness—behold, this person is liable for his life.

However, it is to be understood that yet another intention is hidden within the words of the Tanna. For "one who awakens at night" means that he awakens from his sleep—the sleep of foolishness and the slumber of laziness—after he has aged, just as the wisest of all men said (Koheles 12:1), "*And remember... until the sun is not darkened.*" Thus, the days of old age are the path of the setting sun and are compared to night. And Chazal said (Avodah Zarah 19a), "*Praiseworthy is the man who fears Hashem—praiseworthy is a man, and not a woman?*" Rather, praiseworthy is one who performs repentance while he is still a "man" in his strength.

And the same applies if a person repents after many troubles have surrounded and encircled him, darkening the sun of his light—his repentance is also difficult. As Chazal said (Sanhedrin 44b) on the verse, "*Will your wealth be equal to it without distress?*"—a person should always precede prayer to distress. And in both cases, it appears to me that they share one common reason. For when a person reaches old age, or when troubles suddenly come upon him, then the body is weakened; and what benefit is there in the repentance of the soul and its desire to draw close to God, since the body that sinned has already passed away, gone, and is no more? To what is this comparable? To one individual who sinned, and a second individual returns and confesses on his behalf.

With this, we can understand that which Hashem said to the prophet (Yirmiyahu 11:14), "*And you, do not pray on behalf of this nation, and do not lift up on their behalf song or prayer, for I am not listening at the time they call out to Me on account of their wickedness.*" This is astonishing at first glance, for according to the style of the statement, it should have said, "*I am not listening at the time you call out to Me,*" just as He actually stated above (7:16). However, according to our words, it is correct that Hashem said to him that according to the judgment of fairness, it is not fitting to accept his request on account of the sins of others, for "he who sinned" is obligated to perform repentance. And He brought him a great proof from this: "*for I am not listening at the time they call out to Me on account of their wickedness*"—meaning to say, even if they themselves are the ones calling out, if they only called out at the time of their wickedness, meaning while they are in distress, I do not listen. And the reason is because it is as if it were a different body, since his standing and situation have changed, and he is not the same one who sinned and was guilty. If so, how much more so is it improper to hear your prayer, which is literally the prayer of a different body.

And this is what Chazal meant when they said: Praiseworthy is the man who performs repentance while he is a man—which means in his strength and power, and not after the earthly forces have ceased from him. And the reason is that while he is full of strength and robust within it, then the sinning body returns to Hashem and he is healed; however, after the physical senses have ceased, repentance is of no avail, because the body lies like a silent lump of matter, and the repentance is only for the soul alone. Yet a person needs to return just as he sinned—in the combination of both of them together, as Chazal said (in Perek Chelek, Sanhedrin 91a) on the verse, "*He shall call to the heavens above*"—this refers to the soul, etc.

Repentance that was "*at night*"—meaning to say, in the days of old age when his strength has weakened—to what is this comparable? To a certain city in which there were two banquet houses. In one, the proprietor was a man who feared Hashem and was a great, outstanding Torah scholar, who sat in a company of Torah learners who feared Hashem and thought of His Name; and when they would grow weary from their learning, they would go to him to rest a little, drink a little beverage there, eat a little, and without a doubt with the washing of hands and with a blessing, as is proper, and afterward they would return to the occupation of the Torah. But the proprietor of the second banquet house was an evil and wicked man, and to him gathered only evil and wicked men who occupied themselves constantly with fulfilling the pleasures of their bodies and the despicable desires of this world.

And there was a certain man whose regular way was always to eat and drink and become drunk, and from the abundance of drunkenness, he squandered all his money there. After this, he took all the silver and gold that he had in his house and brought it there, and he destroyed this as well. And when he became deeply impoverished, and he no longer had anything to sell, and the habit had become a second nature to him, and he was already compelled to drink to drunkenness, he brought an old, worn garment—a rag of clothing that he found in his house—and requested that he give him food and drink in exchange for this. But the owner of the tavern pushed him out of his house with humiliations and blows to the cheek. What did this drunkard do? He took the rags of clothing and went to the second banquet house. The proprietor of this banquet house received him with a pleasant countenance and said to him: "It would have been proper that I not let you into my house, for all your property, your silver, and your gold you destroyed in an evil matter by the scoundrel and villain of the first tavern, and what have you brought to me? Rags of clothing."

Let us return to the moral lesson. Wisdom cries out in the street to call out to those who travel the path: "*Return to my rebuke.*" And the righteous constantly spend all their days in Torah and Divine service, and this is their entire pleasure, and especially in the days of youth, for then they are in the health of their bodies and the strength of their souls. The second banquet house is full of the tribulations of time, "*a foolish woman, boisterous, simple, and knowing nothing*" (Mishlei 9:13). And the wicked, who occupy themselves constantly with matters of vanity, squander all their property and destroy all the good things of time that were within them of the ability to benefit their souls in this world and in the World to Come—everything goes to destruction, vanity, and emptiness, and there remains for them no attainment of good traits. And thus, they constantly spend all the days of youth in various vanities.

But at the time of old age, when their strength fails, and it is not that they willingly desire to return to Hashem, but rather all the talents of their body and its strength have already been handed over to the *yeitzer hara*—and about them it is written, "*Because I called out and there was no answer,*" etc.—it would have been proper not to accept them anymore. Wisdom says: "*The soul that sins, it shall die.*" Prophecy says: "*Evil shall pursue sinners.*" However, our merciful Father, in order that "*no castaway be banished from Him*" (II Shmuel 14:14), responded: "*Let him do repentance and he shall be atoned for.*" And this is what is written, "*And you will moan at your end, when your flesh and your body are consumed*" (Mishlei 5:11), etc. And this is why we pray, "*Do not cast us away in the time of old age; when our strength fails, do not forsake us.*" Please accept our repentance to You in the days of old age, even though we spent our strengths in the days of youth on vanity and burdens. And one might think: this is not the one who sinned, for the sinner was a youth, strong as a cedar and mighty as an oak, whereas the one returning to You is a man smitten and plagued, bent and bowed down; the one who sinned against You was the body, and the one returning to You is the soul alone. Yet, despite all this, Merciful and Gracious God, do not cast us away and do not forsake us. And although it is known that from His kindness, may He be blessed, He also accepts prayer in a time of distress as a voluntary gift, as Rabbi Shimon ben Yochai said (at the end of Tractate Kiddushin), and even if one was completely wicked all his days and performed repentance at the end, they do not mention his wickedness to him—nevertheless, this is not from the perspective of the line of His strict justice, may He be blessed, but rather from the perspective of kindness.

With this, a precious and wonderful concept will be clarified in the explanation of the Masorah on the word <span dir="rtl">ומהר</span> ("*and tomorrow*"):

- "*And tomorrow*" *I will do according to the word of the king* (Esther 5:8).

- "*And tomorrow*" *I will give, when it is with you* (Mishlei 3:28).

- "*And tomorrow*" *you and your sons will be with me* (I Shmuel 28:19).

- "*And tomorrow*" *He will be angry against all the congregation of Israel* (Yehoshua 22:18).

- *Tarry here also today, "and tomorrow" I will send you away* (II Shmuel 11:12).

- *And sanctify them today "and tomorrow," and let them wash their clothes* (Shemos 19:10).

Chazal warned us intensely not to waste even a single hour or a single moment of the time of life, just as we find in the Gemara (Shabbos 153a): "*Repent one day before your death.*" The disciples of Rabbi Eliezer asked him: "Does a person know on which day he will die?" He replied to them: "Let him repent today, lest he die tomorrow, and it will turn out that all his days are spent in repentance." And the intention of Rabbi Eliezer in this statement was only to clarify to them his advice so that it would be accepted upon the heart of the disciples, so that they should not say that this matter is difficult for them—how is it possible for a person to confess with a broken heart as is proper to confess, while he is a youth, strong as cedars, his heart tranquil and secure from all fear? For this, he gave them proper advice: that he should take to his heart that perhaps he will die "tomorrow."

This is comparable to one who went on a road to do business at a distance, and the road was disrupted by troops. Before he went out from his house to the outside, he gathered and called all his sons and daughters and all the members of his household to take leave of them, and he was weeping intensely. The children said, "What is it with you, our father, that you weep this day?" And he answered them and said: "If I were taking leave of you completely—meaning that I were going to die without a doubt—I would separate from you with weeping. And now, do you not know that the road is disrupted by troops, and many go and do not return? If so, I worry lest I do not merit, Heaven forbid, to see your faces again, and this separation will become a separation and a departure into eternity; and how shall I not weep?"

The moral lesson is that there are two times before a person to confess: 1) on Yom Kippur, 2) before his journey to the house appointed for all the living, as in the statement of Chazal: One who fell ill and was listing toward death, they say to him, "*Confess, for all who are put to death confess*" (Sanhedrin 43b).

And in the early days, when the world was conducted in a proper conduct according to the temperaments of man and his strengths, and we lived a life of tranquility and quiet, we would make light of the matter of confession and its frequency on Yom Kippur, because the primary reliance of a person was upon the confession that he would confess on the final day. Not so now, when under turning circumstances, the hinges and wheels of circumstances have turned upon us, and bad, new events have recently arrived, which our forefathers did not imagine, to the point that it cannot be hidden even from those of coarse intellect that a person is not secure in his life even for a moment. Just as our eyes saw in these years, how many hundreds of thousands of strong, healthy men were brought down to the dust in a moment of their honor. And from which side can this one be more secure than that one? For is there not one Father to all of us, and who knows what a day may bring forth? Remember, please, how many men were with us here in the synagogue and were praying *Kol Nidrei* together with us, and now, who do you have here of them?

And if so, there is reason to fear that the confession which is on Yom Kippur will not suffice as provisions for the way of eternity, and who knows if at such a time you will reach the kingdom, to have permission to confess as is proper. Therefore, let him return today in complete repentance like a man who resolved in his heart to confess on the final day, lest he die tomorrow, for a person is not secure in himself even for a moment. Just as King Solomon, peace be upon him, warned (Koheles 12:1), "*And remember your Creator in the days of your youth, until the evil days do not come.*"

And King David, peace be upon him, warned a person about this also for the sake of another matter, honorable and good before a person: for if he betters his ways and his deeds in the days of his youth, then also the children whom he fathers—the promise of the verse will be fulfilled in them (Tehillim 112:1), "*Praiseworthy is the man who fears Hashem, in His commandments he desires exceedingly; mighty in the land shall his seed be, a generation of the upright shall be blessed.*" Not so if his offspring come into existence while he is still standing among the myrtle trees that are in the depths of the sea of desires and passions; even if afterward, upon his entry into the days of old age, he betters and will better his deeds, yet the children whom he fathered will be wicked, even until old age and hoary hairs, in that same aspect that he fathered them at that time. And also afterward, they will not better their way just because their father betters his way, because the inclination of the heart of the children is evil from his youth—from birth and from the womb.

Furthermore, know that which is clarified in the Gemara (Kiddushin 40b): A person should always view himself and the world as half innocent and half liable; if he performs one mitzvah, praiseworthy is he, for he has weighted himself and the entire world to the scale of merit, etc. And this is also a great cause that a person should awaken at every moment to better and amend his deeds.

In addition, know this: that there is no end to the obligation of service which is upon a person to serve his Creator, just as the early authorities wrote, that the early pious men were all their days in repentance. For as much as their attainment in the honor of His Divinity, may He be blessed, increased, there increased in their heart the obligation and addition of service, and their righteousness of yesterday became to them like a sin, to the extent that they would obligate themselves to return in a repentance over having detracted from and having fallen short in the service of yesterday and the day before. And these pious men acquire for themselves two virtues: the virtue of the righteous person, and the virtue of the *ba'al teshuvah*. As Solomon, peace be upon him, hinted (Mishlei 8:34), "*Praiseworthy is the man who listens to me, to watch at my doors day by day.*" For this wondrous level—even if a man should live a thousand years—twice in each and every day it should be in his eyes as if he is now beginning, and now he is entering into the service of God, may He be blessed. For great righteous men, even if they multiply mitzvos and good deeds, and their stature mounts up to the heavens and their head reaches the clouds, they do not hold goodness for themselves, and all their deeds are considered by them as nothing.

As I explained on Tehillim: If a man rose to such a level, even though he did and accomplished much good, and he called out and said, "*Open for me the gates of righteousness, I will enter into them*" (Tehillim 118:19)—implying that I have not yet entered into the gates of righteousness and I have done nothing—if a man says so, "*This is the gate to Hashem, the righteous shall enter into it*" (Tehillim 118:20); he is on the level of a completely righteous person, for only the righteous would say so. And likewise, I explained the statement of Chazal in Tractate Shabbos 30a: King David, peace be upon him, prayed, "*Inform me, Hashem, of my end, and what the measure of my days is*"—on which day I shall die. He said to him, "*On Shabbos you shall die.*" David requested, "*Let me die on the first day of the week,*" etc. The desire of David was to know on what level he would stand at the time of his death. And the Holy One, Blessed be He, said to him that you have already completed your law, and finished all your work in the service of Hashem, therefore on Shabbos you shall die—just as on Shabbos all the work of the weekdays is completed. And upon this David replied, "*Let me die on the first day of the week,*" because I have still done nothing to fulfill my obligation; "*open for me the gates of righteousness,*" and let my death be on the first day of the week, when the days of work begin.

And this is the intention of the statement of the Masorah: "*Let the king and Haman come to the banquet... and tomorrow I will do according to the word of the king*" (Esther 5:8). This is the advice of the *yeitzer hara*, for now in the days of his youth let his heart be glad in the pleasures of this world, "*and tomorrow*" I will do according to the word of the king—meaning to say, he promises himself to better his deeds in the days of old age. And this is an iniquity to be punished by the judges, as in their statement: Do not say to your friend—this is the Holy One, Blessed be He—"*Go, and return, and tomorrow I will give*" (Mishlei 3:28), when it is already in your hand today to amend your misdeeds; and why should you postpone this precious matter to a time that will come, as in their statement (in Tractate Avos), "*and if not now, when?*" For who knows what a day may bring forth—perhaps it will happen to you that "*and tomorrow you and your sons will be with me*" (I Shmuel 28:19), and then it is no longer in the hand of man to amend that which he ruined.

And according to our aforementioned words, it hinted to him also the wonderful benefit that will reach a person if he carries a yoke in his youth—then he will gain that his sons will be like plants grown up in their youth, and through him will be fulfilled, "*mighty in the land shall his seed be.*" And this is "*and tomorrow you and your sons*" will also be with Me in My chamber. Which is not the case if you say to postpone this to the time of old age and hoary hairs.

And it said further: "*and tomorrow He will be angry against all the congregation of Israel*" (Yehoshua 22:18), for if you say to postpone the amendment and to do for the time being as your heart desires, how will you not have pity on innocent, poor souls? Perhaps the world is half innocent and half liable, and you will come and perform a transgression to weight the entire world to the scale of guilt, Heaven forbid, and this is "*and tomorrow He will be angry against all the congregation of Israel.*" And therefore, "*tarry... also today and tomorrow*"—meaning to say, why should you lose the time and consume the day of now? Behold, you will succeed more if you set your heart also today to search into your deeds. And yet further: "*And sanctify them today and tomorrow, and let them wash their clothes*" (Shemos 19:10), for even when a person awakens in the days of his youth and sanctifies his time as holy to Hashem to walk in the path of the righteous, he should choose also the virtues of the *ba'al teshuvah*. For according to the addition of his attainment day after day, so he will obligate his soul in an additional service over the past day. And this is "*And sanctify them today and tomorrow, and let them wash their clothes*"—this is the description of the masters of repentance who sanctify themselves and purify themselves each day with greater intensity and greater strength.

And this is what the Tanna said: "*One who awakens at night*"—meaning to say, when he is already stripped of physical desires, whether from the perspective of old age and weakness or from the perspective of troubles and events, both of which are compared to night, and because of this he is awakened from the sleep of his foolishness and his ignorance—"*behold, this person is liable for his life.*" If the Holy One, Blessed be He, does not accept him as a voluntary gift, behold he himself caused it to himself, for he should have awakened prior to this.

"*Or who travels on the road alone*"—it appears to me correct to explain it according to what Chazal said in the Mishnah (in the first chapter of Berachos): Rabbi Tarfon said, "*I was coming on the road, and I reclined to recite the Shema in accordance with the words of Beis Shammai, and I endangered myself because of highwaymen.*" They said to him, "*You were worthy to be liable for your life, because you transgressed the words of Beis Hillel.*" And the reason is because they established the Halachah like Beis Hillel; and the rule from this is with us that "*after the majority one must incline*" (Shemos 23:2), and in a dispute between an individual and a majority, the Halachah is like the majority, whether to rule stringently or whether to rule leniently. And it is not proper that an individual should say, "*I will go in the path of the individual since it is stringent,*" for behold, we see that Rabbi Tarfon became liable for his life when he turned his step from the path of accepted judgment with us. And the reason has already been stated, that it is a stringency that leads to a leniency.

And this is what he means by "*or who travels on the road alone*"—the word *yechidi* is an adjective of the predicate, meaning to say, that he walks in a path that the individual chose for himself—he becomes liable for his life, just as we see regarding Rabbi Tarfon.

"*And turns his heart to idleness*"—according to its plain meaning, it is difficult of understanding, for it should have said, "*and does not engage in Torah,*" or the like.

However, in my opinion, it can be said that the word *hamapneh* (one who turns) is the verb from the root of *pen* (lest), like "*I bore Your dreads, I am perplexed*" (*afunah*, Tehillim 88:16), whose matter is: I fear and doubt at every moment lest it come—the dread of my end. Since it is in the nature of deep thinkers to rise in their intellect into the investigations of matters which the knowledge of man, who dwells in houses of clay, is weary to stand upon their secret; and through this, they make their heart heavy with several doubts which they encounter in investigating the conduct of the world, and in the guidance of the creation, and in matters of the revelation of Divinity, and similar investigations. And through this, they spend their days in thoughts of chaos and in imaginations of vanity. But the wise man will understand and know that into that which is hidden from him he must not inquire, for in everything similar to this, it is proper for us to rely upon faith, and the thought of diligence in this is of no utility.

And so Chazal said: One who looks into what is above and what is below... it is fitting for him that he had not come into the world (Chagigah, the chapter *Ein Dorshin* 11b). And so Chazal said (there, 14b): Four entered the Orchard (*Pardes*): Ben Azzai looked and died; Ben Zoma looked and was harmed; Acher cut down the plantings; and only Rabbi Akiva entered in peace and departed in peace. And this “*entered the Orchard*” means to look into the analytical matters that are hidden from the weight of intellect of an average man, such as the Work of Creation and the Work of the Chariot. And anyone who does not possess in his hand the bit and the bridle to straighten his intellect, lest it exit from the boundary that was bounded for human beings, and fear and dread from the fear of the Exalted and Uplifted, so as not to send his thoughts free—behold, this one is designated to fall into the snare of error or into the pit of heresy. For he removed from upon his face the veil of modesty and shame to inquire into that which is concealed from human beings, whose foundation is in the dust. And about this it is said (Mishlei 28:14), "*Praiseworthy is the man who fears always, but he who hardens his heart will fall into evil.*" According to my opinion, this means that he makes his heart heavy with doubts which it is impossible for a person to reach their nature.

And so it is clarified to me in the verse that Hashem said to Moses His prophet, the trusted one of his house, at the first vision, and He said (Shemos 3:5), "*Do not draw near here; remove your shoes from upon your feet, for the place upon which you stand is holy ground.*" And at first glance, one must ask: what relationship does the removal of shoes have with standing in a holy place? On the contrary, one is not to enter the gate of the king barefooted!

However, according to my opinion, the removal of shoes hints to the matter that we mentioned. For although there is no doubt that Hashem certainly found the heart of Moses our teacher, peace be upon him, prepared and fitting for the resting of the Shechinah—for he cleaved his good thought to the First Cause, and was contemplating analytical intellects, and was astounded by the wonders of his Creator, may He be blessed, and at this hour it was most fitting that the Divine spirit should rest upon him, just as Chazal said (Berachos 55a), "*The Holy One, Blessed be He, does not give wisdom except to one who has wisdom*"—for the intention, according to my words, is that the Holy One, Blessed be He, does not bestow the bounty of His holy spirit upon a person at a time when he is held in the thicket of the vanities of this world and his thoughts are bewildered by the needs of the time, but rather to one who has wisdom, meaning at a time when he is occupied with intellects and investigates the wonders of his Maker. For then he is prepared and fitting to have bestowed upon him the bounty of the Supernal wisdom. And certainly, when the hand of Hashem was upon the master of prophets, even for the first time, He already found him deepening his thought and his intellect in Divine matters.

Nevertheless, upon this, His wisdom, may He be blessed, saw fit to warn him that he should not transgress the law and boundary placed for human beings, and he should be careful and zealous lest he stumble in the trap of his investigation. Therefore, let him walk slowly, slowly, lest he scale with his intellect to the height of analysis excessively. Therefore, He called out to him this call: "*Remove your shoes.*" For behold, all garments are necessary for a person to cover the flesh of his nakedness even in warm places, except for shoes, which are the covering of the foot—they are not as necessary for his existence. And on the contrary, our eyes see that those who walk barefoot are stronger and healthier, and so wrote the wise physician Hufeland in his *Macrobiotics*.

Yet, despite this, human beings invented shoes for their feet so that they would not need to be meticulous over each and every step; for without this, he would be susceptible to damage from thorns and briers which would damage his feet, and only by putting shoes on their feet will they run and not stumble. And this is "*Remove your shoes from upon your feet*"—it hints in this that you should be very meticulous in all your steps to walk slowly, slowly, *and to be fearing always from stumbling like a man walking barefoot. "For the place upon which you stand is holy ground*"—the One Who sees all secrets in his thought sees that he is analyzing at this time with a fine analysis and with a great and awesome analysis, and his foot is standing upon holy ground, elevated and exalted, in a manner that strength and falling are found within it if he is not meticulous in all the paces of his foot—his intellect—lest he transgress law and boundary.

The outcome of what has been said is that one who makes his heart heavy with doubts in a matter that it is impossible to resolve to its clarity, and does not lean upon faith—behold, he is liable to fall into a snare, and his soul will not be. And this is what the Tanna of our Mishnah said: for "*one who turns*" (*hamapneh*) his heart to idleness—who makes his heart heavy with doubts in vain—just as we said that *hamapneh* is from the root of *pen* ("lest")—behold, this person is liable for his life, for he will surely fall.

And as we said, the Tanna juxtaposed these three teachings, for all of them are actions of goodness in themselves, except that the one who performs them did not do so at the proper time, or in the proper place, or in the proper measure.

For "*one who awakens at night*"—even though he performed a good action, that he awakened from the sleep of foolishness, yet he did not do so at the proper time. And similarly, "*one who travels on the road alone,*" according to the way we explained—even though his intention is for the good, to be stringent like an individual, yet he does not do so in the proper place, for the loss is greater than the gain in that he nullifies through this the words of the majority; and as they said (there, in Tractate Berachos 10b), "*lest the disciples see and establish the Halachah erroneously for generations.*" And similarly, "*one who turns his heart to idleness*"—even though his intention is to stand upon the truth in Divine matters, he did not do so in the limited measure and in the proper place, for in this matter, the analytical investigation is but superfluous and in vain (*levatalah*).
