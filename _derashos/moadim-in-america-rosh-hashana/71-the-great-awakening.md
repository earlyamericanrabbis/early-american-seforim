(A Sermon for the First Day of Rosh Hashonah)

The tensions which mark our age, have given rise to a whole new direction and attitude, as well as literature, which stresses the importance of inward serenity and outward physical relaxation. Books have become popular which emphasize the peace of mind, the peace of soul, and the relaxing mood which one must seek in order to counteract the harsh demands of our time and the severely taxed abilities of modern man, who must contend with the most debilitating strains placed upon him by the machine age.

Strangely enough, the message of Rosh Hashonah preaches just the contrary. It is a day dedicated to a stern call, to a reminder of one’s purpose and to a great awakening. This very message seeks to disturb the peaceful slumber in the self-induced relaxation of every one.

<span dir="rtl">עורו ישנים משנתכם</span>

While it may be forcibly argued that religion’s great purpose should be to give on peace and inward serenity at the same time, it cannot be denied that whilst these physical manifestations of repose are highly essential, they should never lull us into the mistaken belief that one’s conscience, one’s spiritual yearnings, one’s longings of the soul, could ever be placed at ease.

I would like to select as a text for this Rosh Hashonah sermon not one of the usual quotations about the life of Abraham, Sarah and the other patriarchs, but indeed one of the most unique and mysterious personalities in the entire wide range of Biblical literature…Job… of whom we can say that due to his many-fold pleasures and extensive holdings, he probably was relaxed, as we say, and he too perhaps sought to repose blissfully amidst all his blessings when the day had come and Satan appeared before the Lord to talk against him. Commenting upon this statement:

<span dir="rtl">ויהי היום</span>

Our sages in the Midrash tell us:

<span dir="rtl">אותו היום ראש השנה כמו שאמר יום תרועה יהיה</span>

Because where our sages seem to imply that since his character was challenged and his attributes were questioned, there had to be a day which called for re-awakening. So they tell us that the words Yom Terua indicate that instead of blissful repose there was a need for the thrilling call of the Shofar and the alarmed awakening which it implies. So unto us as well, it is the Yom Terua that should be paramount, and not the opportunity for quiet and entirely too peaceful a season.

There is need for an awakening and there is place for a call toa rms which should remind us of life’s purpose and of the place which we should occupy in the great struggle which for the lack of a better term we call human existence. There is apparently a sharp conflict between the modern tendency for full relaxation and the religious message which calls upon an Awakening.

The two are not necessarily contradictory, and the seeming conflict could easily be resolved if we but give it a moment’s thought on this hallowed morning of Rosh Hashonah.

This is indeed a tired age and why is everyone so weary? Why the need of escape and why the necessity of seeking re-birth? In the physical sense, due to the magnificent advance of technical marvels, man works every less and most of his truly difficult labors are relegated to the machine. Why is this generation more tired than a generation, let us say, 100 years ago, where the average toil and physical exertion were at least twice as debilitating and taxing on the human being than his labors are today? The scientist will attribute this strain to the fearful competitive spirit of our age. Where Darwinism finally had won its triumph, and the strong reached the top, and the highways and byways leading to the so called top are marked and marred by the charred remains, by the broken dreams, by the utter frustrations of the weaker individuals who failed to make their turn. This is of course true, but like so many other misleading concepts in all times, it is not the entire truth.

Man is tired because to begin with, the work he does is not something he does by choice, because life about him is dictating to him. Because we all of us, with some rare and noble exceptions, permit ourselves to be driven by established standards and accepted rules of society. And once a person has to do that which he does not want or live in a way he does not choose, he will tire long before normally he has any justification for weariness. If we do not seek to define our mode of life dictated by our own desires, by our own individual choices and preferences, but slavishly adhere to a mode of conduct and to a rule of procedure dictated by others, we are prone to become weary and worn-hearted.

It is here where religion comes to remind us that if we do the right thing and we live the right way, life will be an outstanding challenge for refreshing and invigorating endeavors. Our ancestors and forefathers did not need the self-induced hypnotic relaxation because they lived for the Lord, and they thrilled and exulted at the opportunity to serve Him who was the Creator of all of us. When life is but a means of serving the Lord with joy, then one does not tire so easily. For that which has been true so many centuries ago is still true today:

<span dir="rtl">וקוי ה׳ יחליפו כח</span>

“For those who hope in the Lord renew their strength.”

This is the key to our problems, for we hope, trust, and live by the commands of the Lord…then our strengths will be reborn, our efforts will not sag, and our labor will result in active life, purposeful in direction, and happy in its consequence.
