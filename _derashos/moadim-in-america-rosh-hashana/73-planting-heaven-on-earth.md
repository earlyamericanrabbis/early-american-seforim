The message of Rosh Hashonah is in essence so simple that sometimes I feel that Rabbis ought to repeat the same message year in and year out.

Only recently, I read the story of a young Rabbi in his first pulpit, who delivered a most inspiring talk the first Sabbath, on a topic close to his heart. The congregation was well pleased with his utterance, but to their surprise he repeated the same sermon the following Sabbath. They ascribed this to his youthfulness and said nothing, but when, on the third and fourth Sabbaths he still delivered the identical sermon, some of the officials spoke to him about it, and asked him why he did this. The young man, in all sincerity looked at them quizzically and said, “I have to keep preaching the same sermon for you haven’t done anything about it yet, and I will keep preaching it until you will take this sermon to heart and do something about it.”

In the same fashion, I am moved to think that Rabbis should preach the classic message of spiritual rebirth and moral regeneration until people will really mend their ways and perfect their conduct. If they do so, there will be no further nee for any more preaching. Fortunately for our men of the ministry, new developments always occur which come to the rescue of the Rabbi, so he can still preach the old message in the frame of the new concepts of our times.

This is my experience this morning when I want to speak to you about a very old Rosh Hashonah theme. Our country is greatly stirred by the latest developments in military science, which seek to send the artificially contrived satellite into the stratosphere for experimental purposes designed to probe into mysteries yet unrevealed unto us. I am not concerned, for the moment, with the scientific, political, or military aspects of his adventure. I am merely approaching it from the angle of man’s insatiable desire to dominate beyond our frontiers of space, his unwillingness to be satisfied with the dominance and rulership assigned to him by Biblical injunction:

<span dir="rtl">ומלאו את הארץ וכבשה</span>

What I have in mind to say is that this dispatch of a satellite to the heavens indicates to me that we want Earth to control Heaven, whereas the highest ambition of man should be to establish the Heavens here on Earth, to spiritualize our material resources and to endow with divine attributes man’s everyday life.

There is a vast difference between trying to control the Heavens, and trying to imbue this earth with the heavenly ideals of Justice, Charity, Righteousness, and Truth. Once before, in the early dawn of history, man tired to reach the heavens for the purpose of controlling them for his own advancement and aggrandizement. Thus we read that the men who tried to erect the Tower of Babel wanted to do so:

<span dir="rtl">ונעשה לנו שם פן נפץ על כל פני הארץ</span>

To endow themselves with a great name and to provide for themselves greatness and glory. When, on the other hand, we plant Heaven here on Earth, in the words of the Prophet, then we help make God’s name great, and proclaim his glory for all eternity.

The whole procedure which this scientific experiment implies, indicates man’s reckless experimentation with elemental forces that can lead to his doom and invite his destruction. Our newspapers write and our magazines speculate about the awesome consequences possibly to be endured by all of us because we have taken God’s greatest blessings, and instead of using them for good, have employed these blessings for good and evil.

Rosh Hashonah, being the anniversary of Creation, is the proper time to remind ourselves that when God commanded Adam not to touch the tree of knowledge or its fruit, He did so because he spoke of:

<span dir="rtl">עץ הדעת טוב ורע</span>

If the tree of knowledge were to be used only for good, there would be no objection to man’s tasting its fruit. But when both evil and good are possibilities, then a danger point is reached which calls out a warning of the possibility of doom.

The great Maggid of Dubno was fond of telling an ancient legend about a king of antiquity who felt old age coming on, and not being sure which of his three sons would be most worthy to succeed him, told them to take atrip around the known world and discover something of extraordinary usefulness. They were to report to him after a certain lapse of time, and the son who would bring back the most unusual and utilitarian discovery would inherit his kingdom. More than a year passed, and the three sons returned. One brought back a strange and powerful glass, similar to the modern telescope, with which, according to the legend, the young man could see events and happenings anywhere in the world.

The second son’s acquisition was a magic carpet which took him from place to place in a matter of moments. While the third son brought home a strange concoction, supposedly a medicine that could cure almost any illness then known to mankind.

The king was greatly perplexed in determining which of these three unusual gifts was the most outstanding. While they were discussing the comparative merits of each, the son who possessed the telescope suddenly exclaimed that thousands of miles away, in the magnificent palace of a great and powerful monarch, the only daughter of this regal personage was deathly ill with a strange malady. As they watched the girl’s slow but sure demise, the father exclaimed to his sons, “Why are we waiting? You have the magic carpet, you have the medicine; fly there and save the Princess.” This was done, and in a short time the young girl was nursed back to complete and exuberant health.

The father of the lovely girl was now deeply perplexed, for the combination of the three mysterious powers which the three sons had brought, had made possible the recovery of his daughter. He said, “I would like to show my appreciation by giving her hand in marriage to the one who did most to bring her back to life.” But which of the three sons deserved the privilege was a dilemma he couldn’t resolve. Finally, he turned to his daughter and said, “ My child, help me solve this disturbing problem.” Wisely, the young lady answered, “It is true that without the telescope they could not have seen my dreadful disease. It is equally true that without the magic carpet, they could not have arrived here in time to save me. But even after they discovered my misery and arrived here at a moment’s notice, these two tremendous achievements would have been completely helpless, it not for the medicine, the magic potion, discernment and knowledge?

Surely, my friends, this is only a legend, but a legend which illustrates, dramatizes, and focusses the light of understanding upon the problem that faces humanity today. Truly we have discovered great telescopes which comb the by-ways of the heavens and by which we are daring enough to want to send up a satellite in an attempt to add one more to the heavenly bodies that have moved about in their orbits since the beginning of Creation. We have even gone beyond and can truly boast of magic carpets that span continents in a matter of hours and fly across the vast expanse of oceans between breakfast and lunch. But what will these two extraordinary achievements avail mankind, if they are not used with sense, compassion, discernment and knowledge.

Until we humbly turn to the elixir of life, of the Lord God, until we have availed ourselves in the fullest measure of the life giving attributes of God’s eternal law, until we will turn our hearts unto Him to guide us and direct us, then and only then can we use to our advantage the scientific advancements which man’s daring experimentations have made possible for him.

On this Rosh Hashonah morning, when we contemplate the future in all its possible awesomeness and hope, it behooves us to truly retrace our steps and to make the word of God our guiding light in the unknown days ahead.
