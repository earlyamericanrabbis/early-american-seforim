Second Day Rosh Hashono 5701.

Rosh Hashono HAS ALWAYS been a solemn day; however, the solemnity of Rosh Hashono 5701 has been immeasurably heightened by the seriousness of the times in which we live. Never, perhaps, in history have Jews assembled in their synagogues in a more critical period for Israel and mankind as a whole. We come into the synagogue, however, not to be either haunted, crushed, or terrorized by the stories of death and destruction, of the best sons slain in the fields, of homeless cities, of little children crying for mother, whose mother is still in death. No; the purpose of this day is not to impregnate us with fear, the fear of the “angel of death” who is on the loose and has left a path of suffering, moaning, and groaning humanity over half of the world. Rosh Hashono was erected by our religion as a guide post to life, happiness, and well-being.

Rosh Hashono 5701—another station on the highway of life. A whole year we traveled on the ticket that we purchased with our prayers last Rosh Hashono and Yom Kippur. Our trip may have been pleasant or unpleasant, thrilling or boring—but we are all thankful that we have arrived at the station safe and sound. At this station, “Rosh Hashono,” all the passengers have to get off the train to buy new tickets for another year’s trip through life. We come here to pray God for His gift of life, for He is the source and fountain of all our existence. We come here to prepare ourselves for the trip with new inspiration and insight. We shall need wisdom, courage, and character to make the trip a successful one. No one can tell us what the year ahead has in store for us. The future is hidden in the folds of time. One thing, however, can be said with certainty: that our trip through life in the year ahead is not going to be a smooth one; it is not going to be a pleasure trip. We pray God that we shall be spared the ravages of war. We cannot altogether escape the effects of this worldwide conflagration. How shall we prepare? The shofor reminds us.

The shofor is the symbol of the akeda, that event in the early story of our people which has become a symbol of Jewish life throughout the ages. We read of it in our Torah today. God asked Abraham to take his son to Mt. Moriah and there offer him as a sacrifice; that is, slaughter him and burn his body upon the altar. At the last moment, when Abraham was about to carry out the command of God and offer his son as a sacrifice, a messenger of God stopped him. God wanted the heart of Abraham, and not his son. The spirit of Abraham, the willingness to sacrifice the most precious thing in his life, this son, for whom he had prayed all his life, and who was granted to him only when he was one hundred years old—that spirit sanctified all of Jewish life. It gave us Akiba and the countless others who have glorified the pages of Jewish history by their readiness to suffer extreme hardship for the sake of God. This is the spirit that America needs, and the Jew needs, if they are to survive.

The war has uncovered weaknesses, rotten spots in the foundation of civilization. War is a test. Marshall Petain said that the reason France fell was that “she had become soft. The people were only interested in pleasure and good times.” France was doomed because it lacked the spirit of Abraham. Dr. Alexis Carrell, the famous scientist and Nobel prize winner, says: “Everything has been too easy for us. Amusement has been the national cry; a good time is our chief concern. The perfect life as viewed by the average youth or adult is a round of ease or entertainment: motion pictures, radio programs, parties, alcoholic and sexual excesses. The indolent and undisciplined way of life has sapped our individual vigor and imperiled the democratic form of government.” Dr. Carrell sums up his thesis with a proverb from the Chinese to the effect: “You cannot carve rotten wood.” The substance and foundation of modern manhood and womanhood has been eaten away by lack of discipline.

Miss Dorothy Thompson, famous commentator and observer of the current scene, comes to similar conclusions.4 “In order to survive,” she says, “we must make ourselves over. We must undergo a fundamental change. We must stop thinking that the happy life is the life of least effort. The aim of man is to improve himself. Our whole civilization for a generation has been busy educating children not to live beyond but below themselves; never to walk if they can ride; never to do more than necessary; never to do anything himself that one can do cheaply hiring some one else to do.” In short, it is the opinion of the experts quoted that the foundations of civilization are threatened more by our lack of discipline than by Hitler’s bombers. Hitler’s strength, we must admit, lies in the discipline that he has imposed upon the people. The word “discipline” you will hear a great deal in the coming weeks and months. America, the only great and strong democracy left, feels that it must rearm, it must strengthen itself in order to keep the enemy from the gates. Battleships and bombers are being built in large numbers, officers and men are being trained, but the real strength of a nation is the strength of its people as a whole. And strength means discipline. The word “discipline” probably has its origin in the word “disciple”; that is, “pupil.” A person of discipline is one trained in the school of privation, a person toughened by hardships. Mt. Moriah was such a school for Isaac. To quote Dr. Carrell again: “Discipline summons from our deepest cells unsuspected stores of energy. Applying discipline in the performance of irksome tasks, or in conquering slothful habits, we generate high voltages of power. Daily drill and years of discipline can make a man over in his conduct.” And this, also, is the message of the Shofor. To attain the greatest amount of good out of our life we must discipline ourselves. Discipline is needed not only for war but for peace as well.

This has been the Jewish way of life, the so-called orthodox way, as distinguished from the other brands. This is its glory. To live up to every requirement of the Jewish law is quite a task, but it builds men and women of strong character. The Jewish boy and girl who observe the Kosher laws in the school cafeteria learn as much during their lunch hour as they do in the classes. Self-control in the presence of tempting food makes for strong character. To observe Shabbos is a hard task. It requires discipline—don’t do this and don’t do that. It is so much easier to go shopping for the Saturday specials, but in doing this one not only destroys one of our most precious possessions, the Shabbos, forfeiting thereby a well-spring of blessing deliberately; but one also loses a great factor in the building of the will. The mikvah laws have been the target of laughter and scorn; but in reality they constitute a body of laws which spell health, happiness, and strength of character. In the words of Dr. Carrell: “Restraining our appetite for indulgence and indolence, for food, alcohol, and tobacco, all these are priceless disciplines, firm moulders of character and will. The man who daily drills in exercise of will, who has been ascetic and heroic in small matters will find that he is supported by strong inner buttresses when the winds of adversity rage about him. He will then feel the joyous sparks of living within himself.”

The real joy of living is experienced by those trained in discipline. “And when the burnt offering began, the song of the Lord began also.” Gladness is the child of devotion, and music the soul of consecration.

There is a young mother. She is kneeling by a little crib. Her baby has been ill. The young woman’s eyes glisten with tears, and yet she is smiling through them. A few years ago that young mother was a thoughtless and carefree girl. What is the secret of the change that has been wrought? Simply this: she has, with an awakened conscience and a delicate spiritual awareness, begun to offer her sacrifice, to evoke the silent music of her soul, and to bestow everything she has and is for love’s sweet sake. To stand in her presence, and to look into her eyes, is to understand that in her, and with her suffering, she has discovered her song.

In a lighter vein, Will Rogers once stated the same truth. “What the younger generation needs is to chop more kindling wood, and to cultivate a few inhibitions.” The message of the Shofar on this Rosh Hashono day is: “Take your son and daughter to Mt. Moriah; teach them the way of sacrifice and discipline; cultivate within them the Jewish way of life. Then will they attain the summit of the Lord’s mountain.
