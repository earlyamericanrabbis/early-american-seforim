Rosh Hashono 5696.

SINCE THE FIRST DAY OF Rosh Hashono was Shabbos,¹ the Shofor² will be sounded today for the first time. But whether the Shofor is blown or not, a good deal of Rosh Hashono thought and sentiment is focused upon the Shofor. The Shofor in ancient times was used to sound a call to the people to assemble. And some thinkers would have it that the purpose of the Shofor on Rosh Hashono is to rally our people to the colors against Satan, who personifies temptation, and to mobilize the forces against every type of sin. But according to most commentators, the Shofor is the symbol of the akedo.³ It would remind us of Abraham’s readiness to sacrifice, and of Isaac’s willingness to be sacrificed, in submission to the will of God. The ram’s horn should bring to mind that singular episode and impress upon us that self-sacrifice is one of the essentials of our Jewishness.

But according to Mainonides, the purpose of the Shofar ceremony is to awaken us from our sleep. It is the annual alarm clock. Sleep has been highly praised by poet and sage, and any physician will tell you that, while a person can do without food for a number of days, he could not possibly survive without sleep for more than about three days. Sleep is such a wonderful gift to man that the psalmist has said: “Kain yitain il’ydidó shano \[God gives his beloved sleep\].” And Shakespeare seconded that thought when he said: “Our foster nurse of nature is repose.” A similar thought was expressed by the Roman poet Ovid, “Sleep, thou most gentle deity.” However, sleep may not always be put in the category of the divine. The mother of the “Sleeping Beauty” of Chicago, who has been sleeping for 1,313 days, ever since February, 1932, would not attribute divine qualities to sleep. That kind of sleep was probably referred to by the ancient poet of the Greeks, Diogenes, when he said, “Sleep and death are brothers.” For, in that case, sleep is indeed the antechamber of the grave.

If this can be said of physical sleep, of the sleep of the senses, with how much greater force does it apply to spiritual sleep, to the sleep of the soul. Dosa, the son of Horkinas, listed morning sleep among the factors that put a man out of this world. According to some commentators, this refers to spiritual lethargy in one’s youth, which leads a person to neglect his opportunities to make something out of himself. Such sleep is tantamount to spiritual suicide. And it is this kind of sleep from which the Shofar comes to awaken us.

The sleeping sickness of the spiritual variety often proves fatal whether it strikes individuals or peoples. Egypt, Assyria, Babylon, Persia, Rome, and Greece, and all the other mighty empires of antiquity—gone are they and every trace of them. But they did not just die suddenly. They were seized at first with the sleeping sickness, morally and spiritually. They lost every sense of right and wrong, of true and false, and then their empires came down crumbling all about them. Our people is the only exception, in the history of humanity, that has been able to survive this sleeping disease. Our people, too, fell a victim at certain periods of its existence to sleeping sickness, but it possessed enough vitality and resistance to shake off the stupor.

Recall for a moment the story of Honi Hamagol, the Rip Van Winkle of the Talmud. Once, while traveling, he noticed a man, bent with age, planting a carob tree. The carob tree takes seventy years from the time its seed is planted in the ground before it yields fruit. Honi Hamagol asked the man: “Do you expect to live another seventy years to eat the fruit of this tree?” And the man simply answered, “No; but my forefathers planted for me, and I plant for my children.” Honi Hamagol thereupon fell into a deep sleep by the road, from which he did not wake up until seventy years later. When he did rouse himself, he found a man picking the fruits off the carob tree. He asked him: “Are you the one who planted the tree?” “No,” the man said, “my grandfather was the one.” Honi Hamagol is the symbol of the entire Jewish people that fell asleep for about seventy years during the Babylonian exile. But miracle of miracles—the people did awake! How was this wonder achieved? The rabbis of the Talmud explain it to us in their symbolic language. The individual did not live only for the present. The grandfather did not focus his eyes exclusively on the past; he looked to the future and labored for it. He planted the seeds, seeds of Torah, of loyalty, and devotion in the rising generation. Here is the clue to the mystery of a people that had, apparently, ceased to exist and then burst forth into new life. The same process repeated itself time and again. The sound of the Shofar should fill us with optimism and hope, for it recalls to our minds the potential ability of the Jew to awaken.

One of the most marvelous examples of this great power which Providence has planted in us is to be found in the history of the recent past. In the latter part of the nineteenth century, powers within and without the Jewish community seemed to have joined in a conspiracy to destroy every vestige of Jewish existence. On the one hand, the Russian government adopted a policy to liquidate its Jewish subjects by the following three methods. A third of the people would be compelled to emigrate. Another third would be forced into the arms of the Church. That left one-third of the Jews still unaccounted for. However, this number, they figured, could easily be eliminated through massacres. Such was the force without that threatened destruction. Within, the Jewish people had to fight moral degeneration, despair, and escape. Young Jews fled from their faith and people into the arms of assimilation, all a result of the intolerable conditions to which the Jew was subjected. Then there came a mighty awakening, powerfully symbolized in the leadership of Herzl. A people that was dangerously asleep came to life. The climax of the movement was not, as is commonly supposed, the issuance of the Balfour Declaration. The Declaration marked a significant turning point. The real marvel, however, was the creative forces that the awakening released. A great literature in the sacred tongue of the Bible came into being. The youth caught the vision of a new future. The people as a whole were uplifted on the wings of a great historic destiny. Thus began the march towards a new life under the inspiration of the old tradition. Israel awoke. The Shofar challenges each one of us with the message, “Awake!” But how does one awake? Well, let us see how a person awakes from his physical sleep; and then we shall also know how one awakes spiritually. At first, the sleeper opens his eyes with an expression that amounts to a question, “Who am I?” He does not know. What actually takes place is symbolized in the story of a Jew and a general who were sleeping in an inn in the same room. The Jew had arrived rather late at night and the only room that had some space available for sleeping purposes was occupied by the Russian general. The inn-keeper was perplexed as to the course of action. The Jew suggested that, since he had to get up very early, the general should never find out that a Jew shared his room. And the inn-keeper agreed to wake him very early. And so he did. But in the darkness the Jewish guest grabbed the general’s uniform, quickly dressed himself, and, in the twinkling of an eye, he was on his way to the station to make an early train. He was naturally puzzled by the unusual attention people paid him. When he came up to a mirror in the station and saw himself garbed in a general’s uniform, he became even more perplexed, and complained aloud: “These inn-keepers are not dependable. I told him to wake me up, and he woke up the general. Now I will surely be late for my train.” You will admit that our hero was not very much awake, when a mere general’s uniform was enough to make him feel like a general. The awakening of every person comes when he remembers who he was before he went to sleep.

The Zionist movement, and particularly the religious national movement, has done just this. It has awakened our people by linking them with the past, by bringing to life the Hebrew tongue that was dead for centuries, by reviving the ideals of the past. What this awakening has meant to our people collectively was best seen at the last Zionist Congress. I shall not speak of the enthusiasm, the surge of energy and vision that was so plainly in evidence, and which could not but fill every sincere Jew with pride and respect. But the most remarkable event was the union between the religious and non-religious forces. There was a division of long standing: Mizrachi and Histadruth were at loggerheads. The Mizrachi, being the religious wing of the Zionist movement, insisted that the new Judea must be built according to the pattern of the Torah; but the leaders of the Histadruth were, at best, indifferent to traditional religious values. The late Chief Rabbi Kook said to a Jew who was unwilling to leave his son to study in Palestine because of the irreligious chalutzim: “This is a health resort, and at a health resort you find a lot of sick people. They will be cured, however, in due time.” But in the meantime there was friction between the religious and non-religious elements within the movement. At the World Congress, however, the wonder of wonders took place. These two groups joined hands in token of peace and harmony, the entire Congress pledging to respect and uphold all of the Jewish traditions. Our people, indeed, have awakened. When people are asleep they do not recognize each other. But when people awake, the brotherly relationship is bound to be restored again.

The message comes to each of us, admonishing us to awake. Throw off the chains of sleep and remember that the spiritual awakening comes by recalling who we were before we dropped off into slumber. Each one of us carries a slumbering Jew within him, the soul of one whose ancestors were prophets, sages, teachers, rabbis, philosophers, and poets. Each one of us has a giant, a spiritual giant, sleeping within him. An Yeshayahu may be sleeping within us, a Rabbi Yehudah Hanosi, or a Maimonides. The Shofar challenges us to awaken these tremendous spiritual powers latent within Israel, for the enrichment of humanity and the glory of God.
