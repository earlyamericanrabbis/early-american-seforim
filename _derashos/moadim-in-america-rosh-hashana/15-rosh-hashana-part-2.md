*In Midrash Rabbah (Bereishis 55) "And he split the wood for the burnt offering," in reward for the two splittings that Abraham our father split the wood for the burnt offering, he merited the splitting of the sea before the children of Israel, etc.*

And it requires explanation.

*"And Abraham returned to his young men," and Isaac—where is he? Rabbi Berekiah in the name of the Rabbis of there: He sent him to Shem to learn Torah.* (Midrash Rabbah ibid. Section 56).

Behold when we contemplate this creature and he is the human, we see in him bizarre incongruity and radical contradiction, and two opposites in one subject. From one side he is the weakest of all creatures. The human is prone to incidents more than all the creations upon the face of the earth, he requires guarding and sustenance and rest more than all the created beings. And every small thing that hits him fells him to the earth, God preserve us. And as the poet reckoned: A human, his foundation is from dust and his end is to dust, with his soul he brings his bread, likened to a broken shard, etc. And from the second side we see in him that he is the greatest, the strongest of all. The human is the ruler over all the creations. He soars to the heavens and descends to depths and in the righteous and men of action we see yet wonders and wonders of wonders: the sea was torn, the manna descended, Abraham was cast into the furnace of fire and emerged alive, Daniel was cast into the lions' den and was not eaten, and more and more.

But when we enter into the innermost part of the matter, we shall see and be convinced that both of them are true: he is weakest of all and he is mightiest of all. And it is this: when we look at him from the side of his material body, he is the weakest of all, but when we look at him from the side of his soul, his spirit, and his breath of life, he is the most powerful and strong, and all nature surrenders to him.

And behold we who believe in the resurrection of the dead and the immortality of the soul, and that this world is similar to a corridor before the world to come, and if so, the human is eternal and lives forever and he has an advantage and an excess over all the creation and its hosts, and this thing Abraham our father showed in his offering his son for a burnt offering. Because in every single action that a human does, the purpose is greater than the action itself. For example, a human does commerce and spends expenses every month of 100 dollars. Certainly, his profit—and it is the purpose of his commerce—will be 200 dollars or more. And this is not possible, that a man should spend expenses and the purpose (that is, his profit) be less than the expense and the action: the expense being one hundred dollars and the purpose being fifty dollars.

And behold, if the purpose of a man is only the life of this world, and if so, how did Abraham our father want to slaughter his only son and to destroy the purpose with his hands? But if this world is only a corridor before the world to come, the deeds of Abraham our father were very much justified.

And if so, in the act of the Binding, Abraham our father showed before all those who come into the world that there is God in the earth. And the human is immortal and he is eternal—that is to say, his soul. And this is the explanation in Midrash Rabbah above, that in reward for the two splittings that Abraham our father split, that in the act of the Binding he split the matter into two—namely, that the human is composed of body and soul, and the body, its foundation is from dust and its end is to dust, but the soul is eternal; and if so, the righteous human is ruler over nature, and nature is compelled to surrender before him, and therefore the sea was split before the children of Israel, measure for measure.

And this knowledge of the immortality of the soul and its eternity, it is impossible for us to attain except only through the Torah; and Abraham, who was going and aging, and he must have\]a spiritual heir who will finish that which he began, therefore after the Binding he sent Isaac his son to learn Torah.[^1]

[^1]: The essence of the idea I saw these many years ago in the book "Or Chidot".
