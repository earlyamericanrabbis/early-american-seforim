Delivered on Rosh Hashanah, 5708

Of the manifold traditions which give credence to the awesomeness of this day, one of the most interesting is the series of events which preceded Adam’s birth.

Adam, who was born on Rosh Hashana, constitutes the high point of Creation, and, like all great ideas whose ultimate realization usually involves the element of difficulty, Adam’s birth was no exception. In the *Midrash* we are told by Rabbi Simon, “When the Almighty announced to the angels that He was about to create Man, they separated into factions.”

“*Chesed oman, yivro, shehu gomel chasodim*- The Angel of Mercy said, “Let him be created for man is merciful.’” “*Emes omeres, al yivro, shekulo shekarim*- The Angel of Truth said, ‘Let him not be created for man is the essence of untruthfulness.’” “*Zeddek omar, yivro, shehu oseh tzedokos-* The Angel of Righteousness said ‘Let him be created because mandoeth righteousness.’” “*Sholom omar, al yivro, shekulo kettata*- The Angel of Peace said ‘let him go uncreated for man is a contentious being.’”

Hod did the Almighty respond to this confusion? He took the Angel of Truth and cast him earthward. Thereupon, the angels rose up in protest: “Holy One,” they cried, “Master of the Universe, why do You disgrace Your own seal which is *Emes*- Truth. Raise up Truth, we implore Thee!” While the angels continued their disputations, the Holy One created Man. Then He said unto them: “Why do you continue your controversy? Man is already created?”\]

Now to many this portrayal of celestial conflict is amusing at best. The student of Jewish life, however, discerns in this unique incident the *alpha* and *omega* of a grave challenge which is straddling Jewish life in this country. When the angels were made aware of man’s appearance, instead of following through, they bickered an, in the course of their casuistic deliberations, Truth was shunted aside. In the end the very object of their contention came into being.

Have not indecision, procrastination, and quarreling over picayune details been a negative factor in our personal lives and in the life of the American Jewish community?

How many can stop and search their own lives and recall some minor difficulty which sent a great ambition reeling? Instead of action, we preferred conjecture and, in the end, the very problem which we feared most arose to haunt us.

Consider the realm of Jewish family life today. We Jews have ever considered strong family ties as the mainstay of our survival. I recall an intriguing incident which is related in the Talmud. When the sage Rabbi Gamliel was about to bid his daughter farewell after her betrothal, he said to her, “May the Almighty grant that your home will be blessed with the ‘Oh’ expression.” Somewhat confused by the particular remark, the daughter asked, “But Father, pray tell me why you should want me to ‘Oh’ all the time.” “My child,” replied the sage, “I am blessing you with the greatest and the best there is. My blessing involves the birth of progeny and their constant care. When you realize this happiness, you will go about saying,. ‘Oh, this child has not eaten; ‘Oh, this child has not eaten; Oh, this child could have hurt himself; Oh, I wish this child would eat better.’”

Our lives today also involve the ‘Oh, factor, but alas so very different. All one hears nowadays is, “Oh, why doesn’t my child respect me? Oh, why doesn’t my child confide in me? Oh, why doesn’t she appreciate my efforts and my sacrifice?”

Reverence and consideration, which characterized the Jewish home of yesterday have gone begging in the average Jewish home today. The ‘O yeh’ has replaced the ‘Amen.’

Although we cant always blame one single factor for this sad state of affairs, we must not overlook the sad fact that in too many homes today indecisiveness and dilly-dallying about major-objectives is the theme of living.

Too many of us are spineless with our children. There is a time for praise, but there also should be a time for reprimand. When a parent is weak and equivocal, he forces his child to decide his own destiny; he is creating his own nemesis.

When, for instance, a parent rationalizes and says, “I am not going to decide my child’s faith, I prefer to wait until he grows up and makes up his own mind,’ that particular parent is overstepping the bounds of liberalism. He is not being decisive; he is merely reflecting his own inability to act intelligently. Like the celestial debaters, he is causing truth to be shunted aside. He is allowing other forces in life to guide and influence his child.

Very often, some parents will say to me, “I want to give my child those things in life which I missed.” Such words bring to my mind such items as plenty of spending-money, a sports car, a rich wardrobe, a college education, music lessons-and what have you.

Truthfully, there isn’t anything wrong with giving your child certain advantages denied you in your youth. Yet, such an approach reflects unsound and misguided thinking. A parent who is really decisive would add, “I want also to give my child those opportunities which I did receive as a youth. I want to make certain he is steeped in Jewish values. I was to teach him the meaning of reverence: I want him to be proud of his heritage.”

Offer your child the sundry opportunities indigenous to our American milieu, for you cannot disregard the environment in which we live; yet be decisive about those values which make for Jewish wholesomeness.

Now, there are some parents who argue that they have sought to expose their children to Judaism’s sway. Yet, what kind of genuine effort have they made? Too many give up too readily. Their reasoning is as unsound as was that of Hershel Ostropler, the famous Jewish wit.

Hershel was very poor, so much so that he was wont to use the floor for a bed and his fist for a pillow. “Hershel,” asked a friend, “Why don’t you stop wasting your substance on drink and buy yourself a pillow?” “A pillow? Never in your life. I know all about them. It once happened that I placed one feather under my head and when I got up the next morning I was tormented with a stiff neck and a frightful headache. So, if one feather can cause so much havoc, imagine what a pillow full of feathers can do to me!”

Positive values in life, continuous effort, unyielding decision and dedicated purpose will ultimately prove rewarding. Procrastinating, debating, will give birth to a stiff-necked child who will refuse to become involved in Jewish life and end up being headache to the entire Jewish community.

Sigmund Freund demonstrated to what a large extent an adult’s evaluations are hangovers from childish ways. What happens to us as a child can influence our adult life. A child mirrors his parent; therefore, to be a parent is a great challenge.

Consider the case of Orthodoxy in America and you will readily discover that indecision has been its worst enemy.

AS in bygone years, the traditional Jew who is affiliated with a traditional synagogue is still in the majority. Despite our numerical strength, we are far overshadowed by others numerically inferior and ideologically subservient to the mood of the day. Although the preponderant majority of American rabbis, leaders, social-workers, educators, and theological students hail from traditional homes, Traditional Judaism hasn’t been able to hold them. We definitely do not occupy the proverbial driver’s seat. Why?

The answer, although not conclusive, is nevertheless pointed and pertinent. We Traditional Jews and our Traditional leaders are too interested in making *Shabbos* for ourselves. Instead of joining hands and facing up to the challenges of our American environment, we prefer to become involved in incidentals. Instead of pouring new vogues into old forms, we choose to dilly-dally.

The opposition, however, is crafty enough to measure our inadequacies and proceed to take over. It carries on its handiwork without restraint. Consider how tragic is our lot. We Traditional Jews haven’t a pattern of organizational life and a rationale of observance which can appeal to the average layman.

We still do not possess an effective propaganda literature which could appeal to the Jewish man in the street.

The problem is haunting us and will still are inclined to spend our time holding pitched battles behind closed doors. Granted that we parade our seeming sense of courage and obduracy. But the nature of our weapon is no more effective that that of him who boasted that he would fight for his rights even if it cost him his life.

One morning a burglar broke into his business and flashing a toy pistol, he ordered our hero to raise his hands. “This is a hold up!” cried the intruder. Without hesitation, the frightened victim carried our the order and the thief proceeded to ransack the till. The following day, when his friends learned of the incident, they began to tease him. “Where is your courage? Why, you obeyed him like a frightened cat.” “True,” scowled the victim, “but you should have seen how I raised my hands. My fists were clenched.” Some of us are high-pressure cookers who set up 25 pounds of steam to soft-boil an egg.

If Traditional Judaism is to survive, the Traditional Jewish leadership must end its petty peeves, its contention, and worthless debate. We spend too much time shadowing-boxing. Little wonder that the rabbis of yesteryear admonished that a leader must be firm and upright. Aside from personal charms, he must cast a clear eye upon every problem. He must be fearless in his contact with the masses which he leads.

In the desert, the Ark must go forward. In the struggle with modern Amalekites, we must keep our arms uplifted and firm all through the long day of battle, as did Moses, until the sun sets upon our victory.

Jewish life is sadly in need of a vitamin of unyielding determination. Instead of “indefiniteness” we need “definiteness,” instead of conjecture, we need concentration, instead of perplexity, we need purpose.

The pessimist says, “It *can’t* be done.”

The optimist says, “It *can* be done.”

But the “peptomist” says, “I just *did* it.”
