*And Rabbi Yitzchak said: "Any year in which they do not blow the shofar at its beginning, they sound a broken blast for it at its end"*

(Rosh Hashanah 16b).

Our holy Torah commanded us to meet each and every year with various different sounds—with straight long blasts, and also with broken blasts, and also with rapid whimpering blasts, like a sighing groan and like a wailing sob. And we must understand what precious value our holy Torah found in this shofar?

And we will also deepen the question: why, at the time of the giving of the Torah at Mount Sinai, there too did this shofar accompany our holy Torah? What is the matter of the shofar at Mount Sinai, to the extent that from this we see that the good Hashem gave a special value to the sound of the shofar? For what reason? And why?

But, honored masters! Song as a tool occupies a great place in life. True, for the most part we utilize them in life only to take us out of simple life and to bring us into the world of song, which elevates the soul, to forget thereby the bitterness of life and to elevate oneself above the smallness of existence in life. But there are times when we utilize them also for the benefit of the wholesome life, the simple life.

For a simple soldier, the first lesson that they teach him is to know how one strides with intelligent steps when walking in a great multitude, lest he enter into his fellow’s boundary, lest he strike with his feet the feet of his counterpart walking before him, and lest he trip his companion with his feet. For if he takes an aggressive step and enters the boundary of his fellow walking before him and strikes him with his feet and they fall, he too will stumble over his companion due to the shove he will receive from his fellows coming behind him, and then the entire camp will become a trampling one to another. Therefore, they teach them to stride to the beat of the drum that is beaten before him, and according to the rhythm of the drum, he places his feet in a correct step, a straight step, a moderate step. By means of a complete harmony in song and music, they always lead the entire hosts of the army, whether to joy or to disaster: whether to pursue the enemy in times of war at the hour of victory, or to flee in correct orders when success does not smile upon a particular nation and it is forced to turn its back—even then, it must retreat backward in correct orders lest they fall slain...

And if in war, in the literal sense of the word, it is necessary to utilize a specific rhythm with musical instruments in order to teach the soldiers how to stride their steps—when to pursue and how to pursue, and when to retreat—how much more so in the great war, the war of life, a terrible war, a war where there is no vacant place in the universe where it has not shown its strength, its might, and its influence, which requires a specific strategy: to know "*a time for every object and a period for every desire under the heavens,*" and limited laws of how to conduct oneself therein.

And the book of laws for this war is that which was given to us at Sinai. There they commanded us from the midst of the fire, accompanied by the sound of the shofar, lest any man enter the boundary of his fellow, and lest any man squeeze the steps of his counterpart; for in the stumbling of your fellow, you too will stumble. "*Thou shalt not steal! Thou shalt not murder!*" and so on and so forth. For our holy Torah and the sound of the shofar are one \[and the same\], teaching us and straightening our paths in the war of life.

And this is the metaphor of the Targum, which translates "*and it did not cease*" as "it did not stop"—his intention is that the voice of Hashem in strength which we heard at Mount Sinai has not stopped even now, and we hear it in the private life of each and every man, and in the collective life of each and every nation. For each and every man who walks to the sound of the shofar, to the voice of our holy Torah, is happy in life; he does not squeeze the feet of his fellows and his fellows do not squeeze his feet, and in every place he turns he finds before him only the pleasantness of life, a complete harmony in life, and it all speaks glory to life.

Not so the man who does not know how to direct his steps in life; he squeezes the feet of others and others squeeze him, he pursues others and others pursue him, he always finds before him the despair in life, the negative in life, until he grows disgusted with others and with himself.

And how much more so in the life of each and every nation! If all the individuals do not unite to one center, to bring a sacrifice from their private pleasure upon the altar of love for their nation, then each and every one on his own will be for a trampling under the feet of the bad happenstances that roam the earth, and which each one on his own cannot stand against; and only when they unite together in a courageous bond can they stand against them, and in this—that one man helps his fellow—he too will be helped...

Honored masters!

We stand at the beginning of a new year, and together with this, before the war of life anew. And just as in war, the victory of any war depends upon their walking in a correct order and upon the bond between the soldiers—if they fulfill their duty properly, to work with one shoulder, and each one will be ready to surrender his life for the sake of the entire camp, then in the natural way the victory will be to their right hand—so too in the war of life: if we unite to work with one shoulder, one man for the benefit of his fellow, and all of us together for the benefit of our nation, then we will succeed and achieve valor. Not so if each one squeezes the feet of his fellow without looking at his counterpart, and in his walking with aggressive steps he trips his fellow without paying attention to this—like the destiny of soldiers in war who are found in such a spiritual state, where each one on his own will fall slain, so is the destiny of each one of us in the war of life.

And this appears to me to be the idea of the Gemara (Rosh Hashanah 18a): "*'And all inhabitants of the world pass before Him ki-vnei maron.' What is ki-vnei maron? Here (in Babylonia) they translated it: like a flock of sheep.*" The explanation in this is: just as with the tithe, the sheep that passes under the staff passes on its own, and it itself is to blame for the fact that the staff landed upon it—so too all mankind, and so too each one of us who passes under the staff of life, he himself is to blame for it; he, through his bad conduct, caused the staff of wickedness and bad happenstances to land upon him.

And just as before the departure of any camp to war, then the music of the camp will play before the camp, first a song that gladdens the camp, and afterwards it will play a sad song so that each of the soldiers will take to his heart that which he might encounter on his dangerous path—a song that arouses its hearers to sighing groans and wailing sobs, and terrible doubts will penetrate the heart of each one of them: who knows if he will merit to see the beloved ones of his soul who stand ready to receive from him the blessing of parting? Who knows if they will merit to receive his care? Who knows if he will not be one of those who fall in the war? Who knows if he will return whole in his limbs? Who knows? And who knows? And afterwards they begin once again to play a joyous song to arouse the courage of their hearts, and so that they will walk in correct orders.

So too are we, honored masters, as we stand ready toward a new year, toward a new war of life—our holy Torah commanded us to blow first of all a straight long blast, to remind us that we must make our path in life in correct orders and in the strategy of our holy Torah. And afterwards, to remind us of the sighing groan and the wailing sob \[*yaluli yalil*\] that exist in life, the mishaps of time and the bad happenstances that are found in life—so that even if we walk our path in correct orders according to the organized strategy of our holy Torah, like soldiers who walk according to the beating of the drum, we will be saved from them. And afterwards, once again a straight long blast to arouse in us good hopes.

And this is the meaning of the words of the Gemara which we placed at the opening of our words: "*Any year in which they do not blow the shofar at its beginning*"—meaning, any year in which we do not prepare ourselves at its beginning to walk our path in order and discipline in complete harmony—"*they sound a broken blast for it at its end,*" meaning there will be heard in this year the sound of broken blasts and the sound of rapid whimpering blasts, a sound of a sighing groan and a sound of a wailing sob.

Let us hope, honored masters, that the straight long blasts will be a lamp to our feet in the year coming upon us for good, and there shall not be heard therein a sound of breaking or a sound of wailing, but rather a sound of gladness, a sound of joy, a voice heralding abundant peace to all our nation, and we shall merit therein to our land, Amen!...
