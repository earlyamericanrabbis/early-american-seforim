Preached at Selichos Services, 5717

The Penitential (*Selichos*) prayers repeated in the early morning hours several days before the advent of the High Holidays observances underscore the Jew’s classical approach to his faith.

Like the blooming flower in the field, Judaism demands a sympathetic climate. It could never conceive of Man as an automaton nor religious observances as a mechanical exercise. To thrill to the presence of the beneficent Creator, one must first attune his heart-strings to the keyboard of ethical sublimity.

Hence, our fathers, before they began their daily devotions, would sit and meditate on the theme of prayer. Throughout the week, the Jew would gird himself for the spiritual excellence of the Sabbath day of rest. The penitential prayers which usher in the High Holiday interlude, likewise stimulate the spiritual mood which can inspire the Jew to accept his religious responsibility.

The basic theme of the *Selichos* prayers is *Teshuva*, repentance. Man can never aspire to the lofty heights of divine love unless he unburdens his heart of the structuring menace of transgression. The *Selichos* hours of prayer are intervals of inventory when the Jew takes stock. But there are countless defeatist attitudes which arrest the God-love in man whose mastery is mandatory. The drab connotations of these throttling attitudes lie in an unusual comment our sages offered when referring to the penitent soul which has abandoned the cesspool of sin and has sought freedom with his Creator.

“*Bimkom shabaalay techuvah omdim ayn tzadikim gemurim yecholim lamod*- In the place where the repentant stand,” note our rabbis, “the most pious cannot hope to enter.” At first, one is taken aback by this curious observation.

Why should the victim of temptation be preferred to one who has pursued the righteous life? More intimate reflection however, points up the rabbis keen evaluation of the human psyche. Their appraisal of man’s nature is perhaps best described by recalling the unique method employed by Dr. Jonas Salk in perfecting the polio vaccine.

The Salk method consists of taking dangerous polio germs and destroying them. The neutralized organism is then used as the basis for the polio scrum. This concentration of dead polio germs can protect against the crippling contagion of infantile paralysis. The genius of modern science has miraculously converted the deadly envoys into emissaries for life.

Similarly, he who has straddled the contagion of sin is far better conditioned to thwart the menace of further transgression. He is better fortified against the clandestine machinations of crippling sin; his mental Geiger counter can readily detect the dangerous mines which like hidden beneath the highway of life. Whereas the pious and the innocent often fall victims to the serpentine thrust of vice, the penitent soul can readily detect the cloven foot.

By this graphic analysis, we face the Almighty’s sense of tolerance. The penitential, *Selichos*, prayers chide man to maintain his faith in human possibilities. They admonish the resentful and vindictive man who is unforgiving, to renew his faith in the weak and the derelict.

All too often, we condemn the men who have strayed from the beaten path. Our stubborn refusal to discredit their past waywardness has driven some to sweep forward into the abyss. Believing that they can never atone for the past errors, they further indulge their nefarious pursuits.

Man too often lacks the forgiving spirit of Moses, who, though seeing his people deny their God for a golden calf, nevertheless champions their cause and pleads for their salvation.

Unlike Abraham, who rallied to the defense of the reckless Sodomites, we are tempted to turn a deaf ear to him who begs forgiveness. It is this debilitating attitude which sees no future for Traditional Judaism; it is this sense of intolerance which writes off Jewish youth as a lost cause.

One of the focal characters of the High Holiday service is the Patriarch Issac. Because of his willingness to give his life for his faith, we ask God’s forgiveness. In his declining years, Isaac began to grow blind. As a result, he could not detect the deceptive practices and artifices of his son Esau.

The rabbis in the *Midrash* point out the cause for his blindness. It seems as he lay bound on the altar on Mt. Moriah, the angels began to weep and their fiery tears fell on Isaac’s eyes. In time, these heavenly tears destroyed his eyesight.

Remarking on Isaac’s failure to recognize the odious behavior of the wicked Esau, someone aptly noted, “When you have angelic tears in your eyes, you can see no evil.”

When one has become saturated with the divine spirit and permits the Almighty to motivate his life, then he can see no evil. He is as tolerant and forgiving as the Almighty Himself.

It is this attitude which can stimulate the righteous mood that can enable one to enter the presence of God on the Day of Judgment.
