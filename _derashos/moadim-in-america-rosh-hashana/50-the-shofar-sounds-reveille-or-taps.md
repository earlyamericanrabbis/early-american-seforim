Second Day Rosh Hashono 5703

THE BLOWING OF THE Shofar is a high point of the Rosh Hashono service. In the past, however, the weird sound would also evoke snickers among the sophomoric. Back of that cynical reaction was the question, What is the idea of struggling with a relic of a horn to produce an outlandish sound, anyway? If our religion requires certain sounds as part of the Rosh Hashono service, why not use any one of a large number of trumpets to produce them? These cynical and irreverent attitudes will not be this year—people feel that the Shofor conveys a message of great import, a message that would not be conveyed by any other means, not even the most musical of instruments. For the Shofor speaks to us with the combined voice of thirty-five centuries; the Shofor recreates for us the vision, the wisdom, and the courage of a people that faced wars, pogroms, and the auto-da-fe and survived.

The Shofor is our bugle and calls us to service, even as the bugle calls the soldier to duty. Two familiar tunes are produced with the bugle, one is known as “reveille” and the other as “taps.” Reveille awakens the soldier in the morning. It is a joyous sound, a melody proclaiming that a new day is at hand, a day of sunshine, of warmth, and of healing. Reveille has in it the song of interesting labor, rendered on behalf of a dear cause; it is a promise that brightness and laughter, friendship and comradeship, will greet the sleeper on awakening. Taps, on the other hand, is a mournful tune. The bugle sounds taps at the end of the day’s activities and tells the soldier that the day is done, that all activity must come to an end because the shadows of night have fallen. Taps is the farewell to the soldier when he is done with the day’s duties; taps is also the farewell sounded to the soldier whose life’s journey is ended.

When the Shofor, our bugle, is sounded today, what will it proclaim? Reveille or taps? Does the Shofor say, “taps,” that night is here, the night of slavery; or does it proclaim “reveille,” a message of hope, that the morning of a new life is dawning? The answer is quite obvious. Jews have never for a moment relinquished their belief that the future holds the promise of man’s salvation. The Shofor proclaims that although God’s winter, stinging and chilling, must come, God’s spring will follow. The Shofor is a token of hope and faith in the future, for the Shofor is the instrument destined to herald the arrival of the Messianic era. It reminds us also that the war will some day end, and admonishes us to prepare for that day now.

A recognized thinker made the statement the other day, commenting on the beginning of the fourth year of war, that this must be the year, “for the preparation of the peace to come, and a year of great intellectual and spiritual activity and audacity.” Unless we are prepared for the victory, it will be as great a disaster as the war itself. Already statesmen and thinkers are busy preparing blueprints for the peace to come. How shall we prepare?

It is evident, that, when the guns will be silenced on the battlefields, a hunger wave will sweep the world, a hunger not for bread alone, but a hunger and a yearning for the things of the spirit. More than ever before men will need the solace and the sustaining power afforded by religion, for we shall inherit a world in ruins. At best, it will be a desolate world. To make life endurable, men will need faith. John Bennett, nominee for governor of the state of New York, made the statement recently: “I believe that after this war we will see a tremendous revival of faith in God.” His reason is that it will be “an impossible world, unless man has faith, because the future peace treaties will be meaningless if they will be based only on human documents created and signed by man. To insure the future of mankind, it will be necessary that man in his heart and soul will remember and practice in his daily life the Ten Commandments which God gave Moses on Mt. Sinai."

It is well to remember that victory will not solve all of our vexing problems. Victory will not mean the end of anti-Jewish prejudice. Comradeship in the army between the Kellys and the Levins will doubtlessly alleviate the situation to a considerable degree. But it would be fooling ourselves to indulge the hope that a victory will be the sulfanilamide that will drive out all the Hitler poison from the American body. Anti-Semitism, in greater or lesser degree, is here to stay. The disillusionment and bitterness that usually follow a war create a fertile field for prejudice. Leaders of American Jewish life are convinced that, in spite of all the money we will pour out to fight it, there is mighty little we can do to stem the tide. This, however, we can do—we can vaccinate our children against the disease. We cannot hope to destroy the germs of hatred in all men, but we can make sure that our children will not be victimized by them, by implanting in the hearts of our little ones a love for Judaism and a loyalty to Jewish ideals; ideals, incidentally, that are still so fresh and vigorous as to transform democracy into a religion for which men would be ready to work and fight with every ounce of their energy and will.

On the other hand, the Jew who lacks faith in the value of Judaism comes to hate himself. “He comes to hate his parents, because he holds them responsible for his having been born a Jew. He comes to hate his fellow Jews, because their very existence makes it hard for him to be accepted by Gentile society as one of its own. He comes to hate his Gentile neighbors, because they identify him with his despised people. He is at peace neither with himself nor with the world. Not having made his peace with the world, he cannot experience the peace of God.” This dibbuk of self-contempt must be driven out of the modern Jew if he is to find joy and pride in his Jewishness. This is the purpose of Jewish education. It aims to make our children happy in their Judaism, to build inner defenses against anti-Semitism, and to prepare them for intelligent participation in American Jewish life.

We must diligently prepare our children for the rough sailing that is ahead. One of the most heroic stories that this war has yielded thus far is known as Thirty-four Days on a Raft. It concerns three men aboard a Navy plane who were forced down in the Pacific and then spent thirty-four days on a raft. Their experiences, which are now embodied in a book, again prove that life can be stranger than fiction. Those thirty-four days were days in which the sun baked their naked bodies until they were all blisters. Those were days in which the sun rose on three empty bellies. One of them put his hand in the water in the hope of catching something to eat, but the only thing he attracted was a shark which bit off his finger. They covered 1,000 miles in thirty-four days with little to eat and less to drink. Death stared them in the face to such an extent that an agreement was made among them that whoever died first, his liver and heart would be eaten by those who remained; and yet their will to live was so strong that they survived to tell the tale. What enabled them to struggle on? Prayer and the Bible gave them the necessary strength to carry on. Everyday they held a prayer service. One of the men knew some Bible stories, so he began relating them. But the demand for Bible stories became so great and his knowledge was so limited that he decided to ration them. If he did not, the stories would have lasted only one day. I was wondering to myself, how many of our men have a supply of Bible stories even for a day? Prayer and Bible are our food and drink—not only on the high seas. Fortunately, neither our religion nor our Bible is rationed.

The Shofar sounds reveille. It inspires us with a new hope for the future, rooted in the conviction that God is still the commander-in-chief; but it warns us that, unless we do our duty as becomes His soldiers, we might be termed traitors. Judaism must have a rebirth on this continent. The lamps of faith, extinguished on the European continent, must be relighted here. Our sages say that the reason Israel is compared to a dove is that the bird is unique among fowls. When the dove tires, she does not cease flying; she rests one wing and flies with the other. One wing of Israel has been clipped. European Jewry is bleeding. The American wing of Judaism must be strengthend that it may lift Judaism onward and heavenward.
